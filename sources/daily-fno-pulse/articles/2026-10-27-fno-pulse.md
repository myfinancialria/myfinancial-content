## Decoding today's open interest

Open Interest (OI) represents the total number of outstanding derivative contracts, such as options or futures, that have not yet been settled. It's a crucial indicator of market participation and potential support or resistance levels.

When we talk about the **highest Call OI strike**, we are referring to the specific strike price where the maximum number of Call option contracts are open. Call options give the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy an underlying asset at a specified price. For Call *writers* (sellers), this strike represents a level where they expect the market to not go above, as they profit if the price stays below their strike. Therefore, the highest Call OI strike often acts as a significant **resistance level** – a price point where upward movement might face strong selling pressure.

Conversely, the **highest Put OI strike** indicates the strike price with the maximum number of open Put option contracts. Put options give the buyer the right to sell an underlying asset. For Put *writers*, this strike is a level where they expect the market to not fall below, as they profit if the price stays above their strike. Thus, the highest Put OI strike typically serves as a strong **support level** – a price point where downward movement might find buying interest.

Today's specific data for fresh writing (new Call or Put selling) at particular strikes is not available. However, in a typical analysis, we would look for strikes where there was a significant increase in OI, especially from writers. If there was substantial fresh Call writing at levels above the current spot price, it would suggest that option sellers believe the upside is capped at those levels. Conversely, if there was significant fresh Put writing below the current spot, it would imply that option sellers are confident the market will find support there. This fresh writing activity helps us understand where market participants are building their defensive walls for the upcoming expiry.

## PCR — what the put-call ratio is whispering

The **Put-Call Ratio (PCR)** is a widely used sentiment indicator that compares the number of traded Put options to the number of traded Call options. It can be calculated based on Open Interest (PCR(OI)) or based on the day's trading volume (PCR(Volume) or PCR(today's flow)).

*   A PCR value above 1 typically suggests that more Put options are being traded or are open compared to Call options. This can sometimes be interpreted as a bullish signal, as Put writing (selling) is often done by those expecting the market to rise or remain stable.
*   A PCR value below 1, where Call options dominate, is often seen as a bearish signal, as Call writing is done by those expecting the market to fall or remain stable.

Today's specific PCR(OI) and PCR(today's flow) values for NIFTY 50, SENSEX, and BANK NIFTY are not available. However, if we had these numbers, a combination of, for instance, a low PCR(OI) (indicating more Call open interest) and a further drop in PCR(today's flow) (indicating more Call buying or Put selling today) would typically reinforce a bearish sentiment. Conversely, a high PCR(OI) coupled with rising PCR(today's flow) could suggest underlying bullishness or a potential for a bounce. It's important to remember that PCR is a dynamic indicator and its interpretation often depends on its historical range and the prevailing market context.

## Max pain and the magnet effect

**Max Pain** is the strike price at which option writers (sellers) would collectively experience the least financial loss if the underlying asset were to expire at that price. It's a theoretical concept often referred to as a "magnet effect" because, as an expiry date approaches, the underlying index or stock tends to gravitate towards its Max Pain strike. This happens because option writers, who are typically large institutions, have a significant influence on market movements, and they often try to manage their positions to minimize their losses or maximize their profits.

Today's specific Max Pain levels for NIFTY 50, SENSEX, and BANK NIFTY are not available. However, if we had these figures, we would compare them against the current spot prices. For example, if NIFTY's Max Pain was significantly below its current spot price with only a day or two left for expiry, it would suggest a potential downward pull. The closer we get to the weekly or monthly expiry, the stronger this "magnet effect" tends to become. It's a useful data point to gauge where option writers are collectively positioned and what price level might offer them the most comfort.

## FII positioning — what the big money did today

Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) are often considered the "big money" in the Indian markets, and their derivative positioning can offer significant insights into market sentiment.

Today, FIIs have maintained a notably low **Index futures long ratio** at **11.7%**. This means that for every 100 index future contracts held by FIIs, only about 11.7 are long (betting on a rise), while the vast majority, 88.3, are short (betting on a fall). Specifically, FIIs hold 33,588 long index futures contracts against 254,645 short contracts. This is a significantly bearish stance, indicating that FIIs are largely positioned for a potential downside in the broader indices. Historically, such a low long ratio in index futures has tended to coincide with periods of market caution or outright bearish sentiment, often preceding or accompanying downward movements in the indices.

In **Stock futures**, FIIs show a more balanced, albeit slightly bullish, positioning with a long ratio of **57.1%**. They hold 3,494,763 long stock futures contracts against 2,630,311 short contracts. This suggests that while FIIs are bearish on the overall index, they might be selectively bullish or hedging in specific individual stocks. This divergence between index and stock futures positioning can sometimes indicate a 'stock-picker's market' where broader market movements are less clear, but specific sectors or companies might perform differently.

## How to read this yourself daily

Understanding these data points daily can empower you to form your own informed perspective on market sentiment. The primary source for FII derivative positioning is the National Stock Exchange (NSE) website. You can navigate to the "Derivatives" section and look for "Participant-wise Open Interest" or "FII Activity" reports. These are often available as CSV files at the end of each trading day. Focus on the "Index Futures" and "Stock Futures" sections to see their long and short positions.

For Open Interest (OI), Put-Call Ratio (PCR), and Max Pain data, you can typically find this on the NSE website under "Option Chain" for NIFTY, BANK NIFTY, and other indices. Many brokerage platforms also provide user-friendly interfaces to view the option chain, showing OI for each strike, PCR, and sometimes even calculated Max Pain. Look for the highest Call OI and highest Put OI strikes to identify immediate resistance and support levels. Observe how the PCR is trending – is it rising or falling? By combining these data points – FII positioning for the overall market direction, and OI/PCR/Max Pain for specific levels and sentiment – you can build a comprehensive picture of what market participants are collectively expecting and where potential turning points might lie.
