The year was 2022, and the air around BYJU'S, India’s ed-tech champion, crackled with triumph. News broke that the company had secured another $800 million in funding, catapulting its valuation to a staggering $22 billion. This figure not only crowned BYJU'S as India's most valuable startup but also positioned it as the world's leading ed-tech giant. Founder Byju Raveendran, who personally invested $400 million in that round, seemed to embody the limitless potential of India’s digital future. The company’s logo, emblazoned across the Indian cricket team’s jerseys, was a ubiquitous symbol of national pride and technological ascendancy. The world watched, impressed, as BYJU'S charted an aggressive course, seemingly unstoppable.

## The world before

Before BYJU'S became a household name, India’s education sector was a behemoth deeply rooted in tradition. Physical coaching centers dominated the competitive exam preparation landscape, and for K-12 students, rote learning in classrooms, supplemented by private tutors, was the norm. Online learning, while present, was largely considered a niche, supplementary tool, far from a mainstream solution. Skepticism abounded about whether digital platforms could genuinely replicate the rigor and personalized attention of traditional methods, especially for core curricula. The idea of a company disrupting this deeply entrenched system, let alone achieving a multi-billion dollar valuation by offering app-based learning, seemed audacious, if not impossible. The pandemic, however, would soon change everything, creating an unprecedented vacuum that BYJU'S was perfectly positioned to fill.

## The crack

BYJU'S ascent began modestly in 2011, leveraging India's burgeoning smartphone adoption and a culture keen on test preparation. Its interactive video lessons and engaging content resonated with students, offering a novel approach to complex subjects. The "freemium" model, providing basic content for free while charging for premium features, fueled rapid user adoption. The real inflection point arrived with the COVID-19 pandemic. As schools shut down, millions of students and parents turned to online learning, and BYJU'S became an overnight necessity. The company capitalized on this surge, scaling rapidly through aggressive marketing, celebrity endorsements, and a relentless pursuit of acquisitions.

This period marked the company's "growth-at-all-costs" philosophy. Between 2019 and 2021, BYJU'S embarked on a shopping spree, acquiring over two dozen companies. Two acquisitions, in particular, highlighted its audacious strategy. In August 2020, BYJU'S acquired WhiteHat Jr, a coding platform for kids, for $300 million in an all-cash deal. WhiteHat Jr promised to teach young children to code, positioning them for lucrative tech careers. However, it quickly became a lightning rod for controversy due to its aggressive marketing tactics, including ads featuring an "imaginary" child prodigy named "Wolf Gupta" who purportedly landed a multi-million dollar job at Google. These claims led to numerous complaints and instructions from the Advertising Standard Council of India to pull down the ads.

Then, in April 2021, BYJU'S made its most significant move, acquiring Aakash Educational Services Limited (AESL) for nearly $1 billion in a mix of cash and stock. Aakash, a 33-year-old traditional test preparation institution with over 200 physical coaching centers, was meant to provide BYJU'S with an offline presence and blend digital learning with conventional teaching. This massive acquisition, touted as one of India's largest ed-tech deals, was intended to solidify BYJU'S position as an "omni-channel" education provider.

However, beneath the veneer of rapid expansion, cracks began to show. BYJU'S was widely criticized for its aggressive sales tactics, with reports detailing high-pressure sales pitches, misleading claims, and the exploitation of parental aspirations. Sales teams were reportedly pushed to aggressively market expensive packages, sometimes encouraging low-income families to take out loans that they later struggled to repay. Customers recounted being coerced into purchases, sometimes through deceptive practices or privacy invasions.

Compounding these issues were concerns about opaque accounting and financial mismanagement. The company faced prolonged delays in filing its audited financial results. The FY21 results, for instance, were delayed by over 17 months, and FY22 results by approximately 22 months. When the FY22 financials were finally released in January 2024, they revealed an operating revenue of ₹5,014 crore but staggering losses of ₹8,245 crore. Auditors, including Deloitte and later BDO (MSKA & Associates), resigned, citing delays in receiving financial statements and a lack of transparency. Allegations also emerged of funds, including $533 million from a $1.2 billion US term loan, being moved through obscure entities, raising suspicions of fund diversion.

Byju Raveendran himself later acknowledged that the aggressive growth strategy, pushed in part by investors, was a mistake, stating that the company had "overestimated the potential growth in the next two-to-three years."

## What happened next

The unchecked expansion and financial irregularities led to a dramatic and precipitous fall. From its peak valuation of $22 billion in March/October 2022, BYJU'S valuation began to plummet. By November 2023, investor Prosus, which held a significant stake, marked down BYJU'S valuation to less than $3 billion – an 86% decline. The downward spiral continued, with a January 2024 rights issue valuing the company between $220 million and $225 million. By October 2024, BlackRock, another prominent investor, effectively valued its stake in BYJU'S at $0, a sentiment echoed by Byju Raveendran himself who stated the company's net worth was "zero now."

The human cost was immense. BYJU'S underwent multiple rounds of layoffs, shedding over 10,000 employees in the span of two years, with rounds impacting 600 in June 2022, 2,500 in October 2022, 900 in February 2023, and at least another 1,000 in June 2023. These cuts were often conducted via phone calls, with reports of delayed full and final settlements for laid-off staff.

Governance issues escalated as key investor-nominated board members from Peak XV Partners, Prosus, and the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative resigned in June 2023. Advisory council members also departed in May 2024.

Legal battles mounted. The company became embroiled in a dispute with US lenders over a $1.2 billion term loan, with allegations of debt covenant violations and fraudulent fund transfers. In July 2024, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) appointed an Insolvency Resolution Professional for BYJU'S parent company, Think & Learn, following a petition by the Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI) over unpaid sponsorship dues. The founder, Byju Raveendran, even faced a six-month prison sentence by a Singapore court in May 2026 due to a loan default dispute, though he announced plans to appeal. In a striking turn, the founders are reportedly preparing a $2.5 billion lawsuit against investors and lenders, blaming them for the company's downfall.

## Lessons to take away

1.  **Growth without Unit Economics is a House of Cards:** BYJU'S aggressive expansion, fueled by acquisitions and debt, masked fundamental issues with its business model. While top-line revenue grew, the unsustainable burn rate and high operational costs meant profitability remained elusive. True valuation stems from sustainable unit economics, not just subscriber numbers or acquisition volume.
2.  **Transparency is Non-Negotiable:** The prolonged delays in financial reporting and the resignation of auditors eroded investor trust and attracted regulatory scrutiny. Opaque accounting practices, particularly questionable revenue recognition, signal deeper governance issues and make it impossible for stakeholders to accurately assess a company's health.
3.  **Ethical Sales Drive Sustainable Customers:** The reports of aggressive and misleading sales tactics, while perhaps boosting short-term numbers, ultimately damaged BYJU'S reputation and led to customer backlash. Building a loyal customer base requires ethical engagement and delivering genuine value, rather than coercive sales pitches that push customers into debt.
4.  **Acquisition Spree Requires Integration Mastery:** BYJU'S rapid acquisition strategy, buying numerous companies like WhiteHat Jr and Aakash, stretched its resources thin. The challenges of integrating diverse business models, cultures, and technologies were underestimated, leading to operational inefficiencies and draining capital without yielding expected synergies.
