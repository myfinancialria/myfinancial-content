## Yes Bank: How Rana Kapoor Built India's Fastest-Growing Bank Then Personally Destroyed It

The year is 2017. Rana Kapoor, the charismatic and audacious founder of Yes Bank, stands at the pinnacle of his career. The bank, which he co-founded in 2003, had just recorded a balance sheet size of ₹3.5 lakh crore in merely 14 years, a growth trajectory that seemed almost impossible in India's competitive banking landscape. Kapoor, a billionaire since 2017 thanks to Yes Bank's surging share price, was celebrated as a visionary, having transformed a startup into India's fourth-largest private sector bank by assets. He often spoke of building "the Best Quality Bank of the world in India". Yet, beneath the veneer of success, cracks were forming, hidden from public view, ready to unravel the empire he had so meticulously constructed.

### The World Before

Before Yes Bank's meteoric rise, India's banking sector was largely dominated by established public sector giants and a handful of older private banks. These institutions, while stable, were often perceived as slow-moving and bureaucratic. The idea of a new private player, especially one founded by career bankers without traditional industrial backing, achieving rapid scale was met with skepticism. Rana Kapoor, along with co-founder Ashok Kapur, envisioned a "knowledge bank" – an agile, technology-driven lender focused on corporate clients and faster, relationship-led services. They aimed to fill a perceived gap in the market, catering to fast-growing firms that felt underserved by the incumbents.

Yes Bank obtained its banking license in May 2004 and quickly made its mark, listing on stock exchanges in June 2005 with a successful IPO. The initial years were marked by prudent growth, but the sudden death of co-founder Ashok Kapur in the 2008 Mumbai terror attacks left Rana Kapoor as the sole driving force. This moment, while tragic, also removed a crucial check and balance, paving the way for Kapoor's increasingly aggressive and unchecked pursuit of growth.

### The Crack

Kapoor's ambition, once a strength, began to morph into recklessness. Under his leadership, Yes Bank embraced an aggressive lending strategy, particularly to corporate clients. While this fueled impressive balance sheet expansion, it increasingly involved extending loans to companies in stressed sectors or those with questionable financial health – borrowers often avoided by other banks. Among these were high-profile names like Dewan Housing Finance Corporation (DHFL), Anil Ambani's Reliance Group, Cafe Coffee Day (V.G. Siddhartha), and Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services (IL&FS).

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) began to notice irregularities. Between 2014-15 and 2017-18, the central bank repeatedly flagged "poor compliance culture," "serious violations of statutory and regulatory guidelines," and "persistent governance and compliance failure" at Yes Bank. A key concern was the bank's practice of "evergreening" loans and under-reporting Non-Performing Assets (NPAs), which are loans unlikely to be repaid. For instance, the RBI's asset quality review uncovered a massive divergence between Yes Bank's reported NPAs and the actual figures, with some estimates suggesting the actual figure could have been as high as ₹17,000-₹50,000 crore, significantly higher than the reported numbers. Kapoor's method often involved lending to a different project of a struggling developer, with the "extra cash" meant to prop up the first, defaulting project – a tactic that helped inflate Yes Bank's balance sheet and profits artificially.

The RBI's patience wore thin. In September 2018, the regulator took a decisive step, refusing to extend Kapoor's term as CEO and MD beyond January 31, 2019, despite the board's endorsement for a longer extension. This was a clear signal of the RBI's deep discomfort with his management practices and the bank's governance. Kapoor eventually stepped down on January 31, 2019.

The departure of its founder, however, did not stem the tide. The true extent of the bank's stressed assets began to surface. In April 2019, Yes Bank reported its first-ever quarterly loss of ₹1,500 crore. Investor confidence plummeted, and the stock, which once traded at a peak of ₹393, crashed to ₹16.60, a staggering 95% wipeout. Deposits began to flee, falling from ₹227,558 crore to ₹105,311 crore in just one year – a 53% drop.

Amidst this, investigations by the Enforcement Directorate (ED) and Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) intensified. They alleged that Kapoor had received kickbacks, including a ₹600 crore loan from DHFL to a company owned by his family (DOIT Urban Ventures Pvt Ltd), in exchange for Yes Bank investing ₹3,700 crore in DHFL's short-term debentures. The ED claimed the total proceeds of crime in this case amounted to ₹5,050 crore.

### What Happened Next

The situation reached a critical point on March 5, 2020. The Reserve Bank of India, in coordination with the Ministry of Finance, imposed a 30-day moratorium on Yes Bank, superseding its board and limiting withdrawals to ₹50,000 per depositor. This unprecedented move sent shockwaves through the Indian financial system, shaking public trust in private banking. The bank's share price plummeted further, opening at ₹33.20 and crashing to an intraday low of ₹5.55 on the BSE, an 85% decline. Its market capitalization fell to ₹2,711 crore.

Just three days later, on March 8, 2020, Rana Kapoor was arrested by the Enforcement Directorate on charges of money laundering exceeding US$100 million.

To prevent a systemic crisis, the government and RBI swiftly orchestrated a rescue plan. On March 13, 2020, the Union Cabinet approved the "Yes Bank Ltd Reconstruction Scheme 2020". State Bank of India (SBI), India's largest lender, led a consortium of banks, including ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, Axis Bank, and Kotak Mahindra Bank, to inject capital into the ailing bank. SBI committed to acquiring a 49% stake for ₹7,250 crore, providing the largest capital contribution of ₹60.5 billion. The moratorium was lifted prematurely on March 18, 2020.

Post-rescue, under new leadership, Yes Bank embarked on a path of recovery. Its deposit base, which had halved before the moratorium, rebounded to ₹2.2 trillion by June 2023. The bank sold ₹48,000 crore of stressed assets to JC Flowers Asset Reconstruction Company and shifted its lending focus more towards retail and MSME segments. Gross NPAs significantly dropped, from a crisis peak to under 2% by Q4 FY25. In July 2024, the RBI approved the sale of up to a 51% stake in Yes Bank, potentially valuing the bank at around $10 billion, marking a significant step towards a full return to normalcy for its saviors.

### Lessons to Take Away

1.  **Unchecked ambition can be corrosive:** Rana Kapoor's relentless pursuit of growth, unmoderated by robust internal controls or external oversight, ultimately led to the bank's downfall. His personal stake and control created an environment where risky lending decisions were prioritized over sound banking principles.
2.  **Regulatory vigilance is paramount:** The RBI's repeated warnings and eventual intervention, though perhaps delayed, were crucial in uncovering the extent of the rot. The case highlights the importance of an independent and empowered regulator in maintaining financial stability, even when faced with resistance from powerful individuals.
3.  **Governance failures precede financial collapse:** The Yes Bank crisis wasn't merely about bad loans; it was fundamentally a crisis of corporate governance. The alleged kickbacks and under-reporting of NPAs demonstrate how a lack of transparency and ethical leadership can compromise an entire institution, even one built on a promising vision.
4.  **Systemic risk demands collective action:** The rapid collapse of Yes Bank posed a significant threat to India's financial system. The SBI-led consortium rescue, a unique public-private partnership, showcased how coordinated intervention by regulators and other financial institutions can prevent a contagion effect and protect depositors' interests.
