## Why CRED Spent ₹400 Cr on 'Useless' Celebrity Ads — and Cracked Premium Distribution

The year was 2021. India, gripped by the IPL frenzy, suddenly found itself staring at a new, bewildering phenomenon. Rahul Dravid, "The Wall" of Indian cricket, known for his unshakeable calm and gentlemanly demeanor, was stuck in a Bangalore traffic jam. His face contorted in a furious rage, he smashed a car's rearview mirror with his bat, screamed "Indiranagar ka Gunda hoon main!" from the sunroof, and flung a coffee mug in exasperation. The internet exploded. Virat Kohli tweeted his astonishment. Memes flooded social media. The ad, for a credit card bill payment app called CRED, was absurd, unforgettable, and seemingly had nothing to do with finance.

This wasn't a one-off. Earlier, during the 2020 IPL season, Bollywood legends Anil Kapoor, Madhuri Dixit, and Bappi Lahiri had appeared in equally bizarre "audition" ads for CRED, playfully poking fun at their own celebrity personas. Each ad ended with a cryptic tagline: "Not everyone gets it". These campaigns, reportedly costing CRED a substantial sum, including an estimated ₹400 crore for IPL ads in 2021 alone, were widely mocked for their perceived pointlessness and extravagant use of celebrity. Yet, beneath the surface of this apparent financial profligacy lay a calculated strategy by founder Kunal Shah that would redefine premium distribution in India's booming fintech landscape.

### The World Before: A Sea of Sameness

Before CRED's disruptive entry, the Indian fintech market, particularly for credit cards, was a crowded and often uninspired space. Marketing largely revolved around direct benefit explanations, cashback offers, and feature-heavy comparisons, all aimed at a broad, undifferentiated audience. Banks and new-age payment apps alike competed on who could offer the most immediate, tangible incentive, often resorting to aggressive sales tactics and generic advertising that blended into a monotonous hum. The goal was mass adoption, often at the expense of brand distinction or a premium feel.

Credit cards themselves, despite being symbols of financial access and spending power, were rarely viewed as "sexy" or aspirational in advertising. The focus was transactional: pay your bills, get points, avoid penalties. There was little emphasis on building a community, fostering a sense of belonging, or elevating the user experience beyond utility. For a market segment that was growing rapidly – individuals with strong credit scores and high disposable incomes – there was a glaring gap in offerings that spoke to their aspirations for exclusivity and a premium lifestyle. Kunal Shah, having previously built and sold FreeCharge, understood consumer behavior deeply and saw this vacuum. He envisioned CRED not just as another payment app but as a "members-only club" for financially responsible individuals. The catch? Membership was restricted to those with a credit score of 750 or higher.

### The Crack: Absurdity as Exclusivity

CRED's marketing strategy was a deliberate and audacious departure from the norm. Instead of explaining what the app did – allow users to pay credit card bills and earn rewards – the ads chose to be deliberately confusing, entertaining, and utterly unforgettable. The core idea was simple: "Not everyone gets it". This tagline served a dual purpose: it acknowledged the absurdity of the ads while simultaneously signaling the exclusivity of the CRED platform. If you understood the joke, you were in the club. If you didn't, well, that was the point.

The 2020 IPL campaign, featuring Anil Kapoor, Madhuri Dixit, and Bappi Lahiri, saw these yesteryear stars performing bizarre, self-parodying acts, hinting at their desperation for work or their eccentricities. Anil Kapoor, for instance, did strange exercises while singing, while Bappi Lahiri belted out a love ballad about credit card payments. These ads were crafted to create conversations, not necessarily to explain product features. The absence of a clear product pitch was a feature, not a bug. It sparked curiosity and drove people to search for what CRED actually was.

The strategy escalated in 2021 with the now-legendary Rahul Dravid ad. Conceptualized by comedian Tanmay Bhat and his former AIB colleagues, the ad played directly against Dravid's public image, creating a viral sensation that trended higher than the IPL matches themselves. The ad garnered millions of views within days and achieved exceptional brand recall, even though CRED spent less than title sponsors. This high "earned media value" – the equivalent of free advertising generated by public buzz – far outstripped the paid media investment.

Kunal Shah's rationale was clear: for a product targeting India's top 1-2% of consumers who account for 70-80% of discretionary spending, direct conversion wasn't the immediate goal. Instead, it was about building a powerful brand that resonated with this affluent, creditworthy demographic. The ads leveraged nostalgia for a slightly older, higher-income audience (typically 25-50 years old) who grew up watching these celebrities. By making the brand cool, exclusive, and a talking point, CRED turned the mundane act of paying bills into a status symbol. The reported ₹400 crore expenditure on IPL ads in 2021, while significant (representing a large portion of its overall marketing spend), was an investment in creating an aspirational brand that would attract and retain a high-value user base. This was an "acquire now, monetize later" model, betting on the long-term value of a highly curated community.

### What Happened Next: Validation and Evolution

The strategy paid off handsomely in terms of user acquisition and valuation. Immediately after the Rahul Dravid ad launched on April 9, 2021, CRED saw a 6-7x increase in daily signups and an 8x spike in app downloads. Just three days prior to the ad, CRED had achieved unicorn status with a $2.2 billion valuation. By October 2021, valuation discussions reached $5.5 billion, and in June 2022, CRED raised $80 million in a Series F round, valuing the company at approximately $6.4 billion. By January 2021, the app boasted 5.9 million users, controlling 25% of all credit card bill payments in India by focusing on quality over quantity. By 2026, CRED was serving over 12 million premium members.

While the marketing spend was high (₹976 crore in FY22, the year of the Dravid campaign), leading to substantial losses (₹1,280 crore in FY22), CRED's revenue from operations has shown significant growth. In FY25, CRED reported an operating revenue of ₹2,735 crore, a 16% year-on-year increase, and managed to narrow its operating losses by 51% to ₹298 crore. Total losses also declined to ₹1,457 crore in FY25. The company has diversified its revenue streams beyond just payment processing fees, expanding into lending, insurance, wealth management, and an e-commerce marketplace (CRED Store). Monthly transacting users increased by 14.5% to 1.26 crore in FY25, with transaction frequency rising 34%.

However, the journey hasn't been without its adjustments. In 2025, CRED underwent a "down round," raising $72 million at a reduced valuation of $3.64 billion, down from its peak of $6.4 billion in 2022, as investors increasingly prioritized sustainable growth and profitability. Despite this, in June 2026, Meta Platforms announced a $900 million investment, acquiring a 20% minority stake, valuing CRED at around $4.5 billion. This investment further validates CRED's strategy of building a high-value user base, even if profitability remains a long-term goal.

### Lessons to Take Away

1.  **Exclusivity as the Ultimate Status Signal:** In a world saturated with mass-market products, CRED demonstrated that deliberate exclusivity can be a powerful draw. By restricting membership to high credit scores, it cultivated an aspirational "club" mentality, turning a functional app into a status symbol. The ads, rather than explaining the product, reinforced this sense of belonging for those "in the know."
2.  **The Power of Absurdity and Earned Media:** CRED proved that marketing doesn't always need to be direct or logical. Intentionally absurd, humorous campaigns featuring celebrities playing against type generated immense viral buzz and earned media, far beyond what traditional advertising spend alone could achieve. This created cultural moments that kept the brand top-of-mind.
3.  **Long-Term Brand Building Trumps Immediate Conversion for Niche Audiences:** For a premium, niche audience, immediate conversion isn't the only metric. CRED's strategy was about building deep brand recall and trust over time, even at a high initial cost. The bet was that owning the relationship with India's most creditworthy consumers would create a valuable ecosystem for future monetization through various financial services.
4.  **Data as the New Oil for Monetization:** While the ads focused on brand, CRED's underlying business model is deeply data-driven. By aggregating financial data from its high-trust user base, CRED positions itself to offer highly personalized financial products like lending and wealth management, moving beyond simple bill payments to become a comprehensive financial super-app.
