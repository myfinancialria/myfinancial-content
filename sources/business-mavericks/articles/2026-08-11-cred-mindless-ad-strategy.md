It was April 9, 2021, the opening day of the 14th season of the Indian Premier League. As millions tuned in, a new advertisement hit screens that would instantly become a national talking point. The 23-second clip featured legendary cricketer Rahul Dravid, known for his unshakeable calm and composure, in an utterly uncharacteristic fit of road rage. "Indiranagar ka gunda hoon main!" he bellowed, smashing a car's side mirror with his bat, his face contorted in fury. The internet erupted. Virat Kohli, the then-captain of the Royal Challengers Bangalore, tweeted, "Never seen this side of Rahul bhai," amplifying the virality.

This wasn't just another ad; it was a carefully orchestrated moment of delightful absurdity from CRED, a fintech startup that had chosen to spend hundreds of crores on celebrity campaigns that often seemed to mock the very idea of advertising. Many called it useless, a colossal waste of investor money. But for CRED, led by Kunal Shah, the absurdity was the point. It was a calculated, exclusionary strategy designed to crack premium distribution in India's booming financial services market, turning a mundane task into a status symbol.

### The World Before

Before CRED's aggressive marketing blitz in 2020-2021, India's fintech landscape was largely defined by a race for the masses. Companies like Paytm, PhonePe, and Google Pay were locked in a fierce battle for transaction volume, focusing on onboarding the "next billion users" through low-friction payments and cashback offers. Their goal was breadth, serving the unbanked and underbanked with transactional convenience.

Credit card bill payment, the core function CRED would later gamify, was typically a dull, functional chore. Users navigated clunky bank apps, often forgot due dates, and dealt with a maze of net banking redirects. There was little to no premium experience for the financially disciplined.

Kunal Shah, a serial entrepreneur who had previously sold his digital payments company FreeCharge to Snapdeal in 2015, saw this gap. He realized that while the bottom of the pyramid was well-served, the affluent, creditworthy Indian consumer was largely ignored when it came to a superior financial experience. Conventional wisdom dictated chasing a large user base, but Shah believed that for a specific segment, "exclusivity was itself the product." His vision was counterintuitive: build an app for credit card users with a CIBIL score of 750 or above, effectively cutting out roughly 90% of India's population. Many viewed this strategy as "insane" in a market obsessed with growth, questioning the viability of building for "the one percent."

### The Crack

CRED, founded in 2018, launched with a simple premise: reward users for timely credit card bill payments with "CRED Coins" redeemable for exclusive offers and experiences. But the true genius lay in its marketing. From the outset, CRED embraced a tagline that highlighted its deliberate exclusivity: "Not everyone gets it."

The company's marketing strategy truly hit its stride during the Indian Premier League (IPL). In 2020, CRED rolled out its "Not everyone gets it" campaign, featuring beloved but slightly aging celebrities like Anil Kapoor, Madhuri Dixit, and Bappi Lahiri. These ads depicted them humorously "auditioning" for a CRED ad, often performing absurd acts, only to be rejected because they "didn't get it" – subtly reinforcing the app's selective membership. The audacious approach worked, reportedly leading to a 700% jump in app downloads after the campaign launch.

The following year, during IPL 2021, CRED doubled down with the "Great for the Good" campaign, which included the now-iconic Rahul Dravid "Indiranagar ka Gunda" ad. The campaign featured other celebrities like Jim Sarbh (as narrator), Anil Kapoor, Madhuri Dixit (again), Kapil Dev, and Kumar Sanu, all performing wildly out-of-character antics. The ads were deliberately designed to be baffling, entertaining, and shareable, focusing on generating brand memorability and distinctiveness rather than directly explaining product features.

This strategy came with a hefty price tag. CRED spent ₹418 crore on marketing and promotions in FY21, which was 438% of its revenue that year. In FY22, the year the Dravid ad went viral, the marketing spend soared to ₹976 crore, while revenue stood at ₹394 crore – meaning 248% of its revenue was burned on advertising alone. Many critics pointed to these figures as evidence of irrational spending, but Kunal Shah defended the approach, stating that CRED aimed to build a "trust brand" rather than a "product brand." He argued that for its target audience, who already understood credit card bill payment, direct feature explanation was redundant. Instead, memorable and aspirational positioning was key to building trust. The absurd ads generated an immense amount of "earned media value"—estimated at 10 to 15 times the paid media investment—through social shares, memes, and news coverage, proving that the madness had a method.

### What Happened Next

CRED's unconventional advertising strategy, coupled with its exclusive membership model, fueled a meteoric rise in valuation and user adoption within its niche. By April 2021, soon after the Rahul Dravid campaign, CRED joined India's unicorn club with a valuation of $2.2 billion. This figure continued to climb rapidly, reaching $4 billion by late 2021 and peaking at an impressive $6.4 billion in June 2022.

The user base, though deliberately restricted, grew significantly. From 5.9 million users in 2021, CRED expanded to over 13 million monthly active users by mid-2024. More importantly, the company captured a significant share of the premium credit card payment market, processing approximately 22% of all credit card bill payments in India monthly by April 2021, a figure that hovered around 20-25% by 2022-2024.

While the initial years were marked by heavy losses due to marketing spend—₹360.31 crore in FY20, ₹524 crore in FY21, and ₹1,280 crore in FY22—CRED's financial performance began to show improvement as its monetization strategy matured. Revenue saw substantial growth, jumping from a mere ₹52 lakh (₹0.52 crore) in FY20 to ₹95.5 crore in FY21, then to ₹394 crore in FY22, and a remarkable ₹2,473 crore in FY24, representing a 66% year-on-year increase. Operating losses also began to narrow, reducing by 41% to ₹609 crore in FY24.

CRED evolved beyond a simple bill payment app, layering on additional financial products. It introduced CRED Cash (for instant loans), CRED Mint (peer-to-peer lending), CRED Pay (UPI integration), CRED Store (e-commerce), and CRED Garage (vehicle management), transforming into a financial super-app for its affluent members. The model shifted to monetizing its high-quality user base by selling access to partner brands, who paid to reach CRED's exclusive, creditworthy audience through its reward catalog and financial product offerings. As of May 2025, the company's valuation stood at around $3.5 billion, a recalibration from its 2022 peak but still cementing its position as a significant player in the Indian fintech space.

### Lessons to Take Away

1.  **Exclusivity Creates Aspiration:** In a market saturated with mass-market offerings, CRED's decision to deliberately exclude a vast majority of potential users by requiring a high credit score was a masterstroke. This counter-intuitive "gate" transformed a mundane financial utility into an aspirational lifestyle product, where membership itself became a status signal. The rejection of users was as powerful a marketing tool as the acceptance.
2.  **Strategic Absurdity Cuts Through Noise:** CRED's intentionally outlandish celebrity advertisements were not about direct product communication; they were about generating maximum attention and memorability. By using familiar faces in unexpected, humorous roles, CRED created viral moments that generated immense "earned media," proving that breaking traditional advertising rules can be highly effective in a crowded digital landscape if the goal is brand recall and buzz.
3.  **Build a "Trust Brand," Not Just a "Product Brand":** For a sophisticated target audience already familiar with credit card mechanics, explaining features was redundant. Kunal Shah's focus was on building a distinctive "trust brand" that resonated emotionally and aspirational. This brand equity, once established through memorable advertising and exclusive experiences, could then be leveraged to cross-sell a diverse range of financial products, moving beyond the initial core offering.
4.  **Invest for Long-Term Value of Premium Users:** The seemingly exorbitant marketing spend in CRED's early years was a calculated, albeit risky, investment in acquiring a high-quality, creditworthy user base. The thesis was that these premium users, once acquired, would have a high lifetime value, making them receptive to various monetization avenues like lending, insurance, and commerce. This long-term bet on the financial discipline of a niche segment justified the high initial customer acquisition costs.
