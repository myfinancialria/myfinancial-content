## Naukri.com: How Sanjeev Bikhchandani Built India's Most Profitable Internet Company by Saying No

In March 1997, the idea of finding a job online in India seemed like a fantasy. The internet itself was a nascent concept, with barely 14,000 connections across the entire country. Most job seekers still scoured the thick classified sections of Sunday newspapers, and setting up a website required servers in far-off America. Yet, from a modest servant’s quarter above a garage in Delhi, a 34-year-old Sanjeev Bikhchandani embarked on a journey that would defy conventional wisdom and birth Naukri.com, India’s pioneering online job portal. He didn't even have an internet connection when he launched; job listings were manually typed into floppy disks and uploaded from a friend's home. Many dismissed the name "Naukri" as too "downmarket" or "too Hindi," preferring anglicized alternatives. But Bikhchandani saw relatability and zing where others saw limitations, a choice that would prove prophetic for a brand that became synonymous with employment itself in India.

### The World Before the Click

The India of the mid-1990s was a vastly different landscape for entrepreneurs, especially those dreaming of a digital future. The liberalization of the economy was still unfolding, and the information technology sector, while growing, was largely focused on services for overseas clients. The concept of a consumer-facing internet business, let alone one that would aggregate job listings, was virtually unheard of. Job hunting was a laborious, geographically constrained process. Candidates relied on word-of-mouth, recruitment agencies, and, most prominently, the dense pages of national and regional newspapers.

Starting an internet company then was not just challenging; it was an act of faith. Capital was scarce for such speculative ventures, and the infrastructure to support them was rudimentary at best. The dot-com boom and bust, a phenomenon that would sweep the West in the early 2000s, had not yet fully arrived, leaving Indian entrepreneurs to navigate uncharted waters with limited resources and even less precedent. The prevailing business philosophy often leaned towards quick wins or leveraging existing relationships, not building scalable, long-term digital platforms from scratch with an almost non-existent user base.

### The Crack: A Blueprint for Sustainable Growth

Sanjeev Bikhchandani's journey with Naukri.com, under its parent company Info Edge, was characterized by a deliberate, almost stubborn, refusal to chase ephemeral trends. Launched in April 1997 with a database of 1,000 jobs painstakingly collected from 29 newspapers, Naukri.com began as a simple aggregator. Its initial revenue in the first year was a modest ₹2.35 lakh, growing to ₹18 lakh by the close of 1998-99. This slow, organic growth was a stark contrast to the cash-burning strategies many internet startups would later adopt.

Bikhchandani’s philosophy was clear: focus on solving a real customer problem and build a business that makes money from day one. "Bootstrapping is a great experience as it teaches you the value of money," he once said. "You learn to do more with less and you develop a sharp focus on the customers' needs and problems". Naukri.com’s business model was primarily B2B (business-to-business), offering services like resume database access, job postings, and email marketing to corporate clients. They also provided B2C (business-to-consumer) services like resume writing to job seekers, though the bulk of revenue came from employers.

Crucially, Info Edge avoided the early dot-com frenzy of aggressive spending. While many peers burned through investor cash, Naukri focused on unit economics and profitability. In the early 2000s, when the global dot-com bubble burst, numerous internet companies collapsed. Naukri.com, having prioritized sustainable revenue generation, not only survived but thrived.

It wasn't until April 2000, three years after its launch and after proving its business model and initial profitability, that Info Edge raised its first significant venture capital funding of ₹7.3 crore from ICICI Ventures, valuing the firm at ₹45 crore. This wasn't a desperate plea for survival, but a strategic infusion to scale an already validated business. The company further strengthened its operational capabilities with the recruitment of Hitesh Oberoi, now the Managing Director and CEO, who helped build out an effective offline sales team to reach corporate clients. This blend of digital innovation and traditional sales rigor proved instrumental.

Bikhchandani's "saying no" extended to overseas adventures and aggressive, unfocused acquisitions for Naukri itself. The focus remained laser-sharp on dominating the Indian recruitment market. This discipline allowed Info Edge to build a robust, cash-generating engine that would later become the bedrock for strategic investments in other promising Indian internet companies.

### What Happened Next: A Quiet Empire

The deliberate path chosen by Sanjeev Bikhchandani and Naukri.com paid off handsomely. In November 2006, Info Edge made history by becoming the first Indian internet company to go public. The IPO was an overwhelming success, oversubscribed by a remarkable 54.73 times, raising between ₹154-174 crore at a price band of ₹290-320 per share. This public listing was a validation of a business model built on patience, profitability, and deep market understanding.

Naukri.com cemented its position as India's undisputed leader in online recruitment. By 2011, it commanded a dominant 55% market share, growing to over 70% by 2018. Today, Naukri.com boasts the largest job seeker database in India, with over 8 crore resumes and serving over 90,000 paying clients. It remains the primary revenue engine for Info Edge, contributing significantly to its profits.

Info Edge's market capitalization stands at approximately ₹78,073 crore (roughly $9.3 billion USD), a testament to its sustained growth. Sanjeev Bikhchandani, through his stake in Info Edge, became one of India's first internet billionaires, with a net worth of $4.25 billion as of October 2024. The company's stock has shown impressive long-term compounding.

While the article's core focus is Naukri, it's important to note that the cash flow generated by Naukri allowed Info Edge to strategically invest in other Indian internet startups, most notably taking early bets on Zomato and Policybazaar, both of which went public in 2021. These investments further exemplify Info Edge's long-term vision and its ability to compound capital, with its consumer technology and consumer AI portfolio (the oldest and largest segment) delivering a roughly 34% gross Internal Rate of Return (IRR).

### Lessons to Take Away

1.  **Profitability Over Hype:** In an era often dominated by valuation multiples and growth-at-all-costs, Naukri.com's story underscores the enduring power of a business model designed for profitability from the outset. Burning cash for market share might yield temporary gains, but sustainable revenue ensures longevity and resilience.
2.  **Deep Customer Insight Trumps Glamour:** Bikhchandani didn't chase the next big, glamorous idea; he identified a tangible problem (fragmented job market) and built a useful solution. His focus on "being useful" became their competitive moat. Success often lies in solving fundamental problems, even if the solution seems mundane at first.
3.  **Strategic Patience and Focused Growth:** By saying no to premature diversification and aggressive overseas expansion for Naukri itself, Info Edge concentrated its resources on dominating its core market. This strategic patience allowed them to build an unshakeable foundation before selectively venturing into other areas or making investments.
4.  **Bootstrapping Builds Discipline:** The initial period of bootstrapping forced frugality and a deep understanding of unit economics. This discipline, born out of necessity, ingrained a culture of efficiency that served the company well even after it secured funding and went public. It taught them to "do more with less".
5.  **Customer Money is the Best Validation:** As Bikhchandani noted, relying on customer revenue for growth validates the business model more effectively than relying solely on investor funds. If customers repeatedly pay for a product or service, it’s a clear signal of market fit and value creation.
