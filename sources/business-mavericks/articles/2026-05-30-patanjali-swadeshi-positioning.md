## How Ramdev's Patanjali Used 'Swadeshi' to Take ₹10,000 Cr from HUL, Nestle, and Colgate

In a packed press conference in February 2016, amidst a flurry of camera flashes and eager journalists, Baba Ramdev, the saffron-clad yoga guru, made a bold declaration. "Multinational firms like Hindustan Unilever, Colgate and Nestle are bribing people to get fake and adulterated Patanjali products tested in labs," he asserted, accusing them of a conspiracy born out of fear. "Patanjali has become the point of discussion in the boardrooms of MNCs." His words, laced with defiance and nationalistic fervor, weren't just a defensive posture; they were a battle cry in an economic war he had unilaterally declared, aiming to reclaim India's consumer market with 'Swadeshi' products.

### The World Before Patanjali

For decades, the Indian fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) market was a fortress guarded by global giants. Companies like Hindustan Unilever (HUL), Nestle, and Colgate-Palmolive reigned supreme, their ubiquitous brands deeply embedded in the daily lives of millions. They had mastered the art of Indian consumerism through extensive distribution networks, aggressive advertising campaigns featuring Bollywood stars, and products meticulously tailored for the masses. From a tube of Colgate toothpaste to a sachet of Dove shampoo or a packet of Maggi noodles, these multinational corporations (MNCs) dictated tastes and preferences, enjoying immense customer loyalty built over generations. The thought of an indigenous brand, particularly one rooted in traditional Ayurveda and spearheaded by a yoga guru, challenging this well-entrenched hegemony seemed, to many, either impossible or foolish. The market was competitive and dynamic, with established players like Procter & Gamble and ITC also vying for dominance.

### The Crack

Patanjali Ayurved, co-founded by Baba Ramdev and Acharya Balkrishna in 2006, had a different vision. While it began as a small pharmacy unit focused on Ayurvedic herbal medicines, its ambition soon expanded to encompass a full-fledged enterprise aiming to bring Ayurveda to every household, not as a luxury, but as a necessity.

The real disruption, however, began to accelerate around 2012. Patanjali didn't try to beat MNCs at their own game; it changed the game entirely. The core of Patanjali's strategy was a potent cocktail of traditional Indian wisdom (Ayurveda), aggressive pricing, and a fierce 'Swadeshi' (of one's own country) narrative. Ramdev, already a household name due to his televised yoga sessions, became the charismatic face and spiritual leader of the brand, leveraging his personal trust and mass appeal as an unintentional launchpad for Patanjali's products.

His rhetoric was sharp and direct. He publicly accused foreign companies of "financial slavery" and "looting" India by repatriating profits, drawing parallels to the East India Company. "No MNC comes here to give you money, they have no love for India, they are here to earn profits. They bring Re 1 with them and take back Rs 100 with them," he declared in 2017. This nationalistic appeal, coupled with the promise of "natural" and "chemical-free" Ayurvedic products, resonated deeply with Indian consumers, particularly in rural areas.

Patanjali's growth was explosive. From a revenue of approximately ₹400 crore in 2011, it surged to ₹5,000 crore by 2016, and then more than doubled to hit ₹10,561 crore in the fiscal year 2016-17. This represented an unparalleled feat in India's FMCG industry, with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of over 100% in just three years.

They cracked rural India, a market often underserved or misunderstood by MNCs, through a multi-pronged approach:
*   **Affordable Pricing:** Patanjali products were typically 15-20% cheaper than their competitors, achieved by sourcing raw materials directly from farmers, minimizing marketing spends, and maintaining lower overheads. For instance, their popular Dant Kanti toothpaste was priced significantly lower than competitors.
*   **Ayurveda + Trust:** The products were marketed as a blend of ancient Indian science and modern technology, tapping into a deep-seated cultural trust in Ayurveda. Ramdev's image as a yoga guru ensured that products were perceived as natural and trustworthy.
*   **Innovative Distribution:** Beyond their own "Patanjali Chikitsalaya" and "Patanjali Mega Marts," they partnered with large retail chains like Future Group, Reliance Retail, Hyper City, and Star Bazaar, as well as e-commerce platforms. In rural areas, they leveraged small village stores, health centers, and even mobile health vans, supported by yoga camps and word-of-mouth promotion. This grassroots strategy focused on the 4 A's: Availability, Affordability, Acceptability, and Awareness.
*   **Product Diversification:** Starting with medicines, Patanjali rapidly expanded into almost every FMCG category imaginable: from herbal toothpaste (Dant Kanti) and hair oil (Kesh Kanti) to ghee, biscuits, noodles, and even apparel. Patanjali Ghee became one of their largest selling products.

The impact was undeniable. In the oral care segment, Patanjali's Dant Kanti doubled its market share to 7.4% in the year through March 2018, largely at the expense of market leader Colgate Palmolive, whose volume share fell from 55.1% to 53.4%. HUL also saw its share drop. Patanjali's success forced rivals like Colgate to launch their own herbal toothpaste, like Cibaca Vedshakti, priced competitively. HUL, Dabur, Emami, and Himalaya all found themselves needing to expand their herbal product portfolios and improve formulations to remain relevant.

### What Happened Next

Patanjali's meteoric rise peaked in FY 2016-17. While it achieved an annual turnover of ₹10,561 crore in that period, its growth trajectory began to falter in the subsequent years. Revenue dipped to around ₹8,136 crore in 2017-18, a drop attributed by some to factors like GST implementation and demonetization.

The dominant MNCs, initially caught off guard, reacted by re-strategizing. They ramped up their own "natural" and "herbal" offerings, increased advertising spends, and strengthened their distribution in rural areas. By 2018, while Patanjali continued to pose a significant challenge, especially in categories like oral care, its overall sales growth slowed considerably. In fact, Patanjali's stagnant growth in 2017-18 meant HUL, ITC, and Nestle all recorded double-digit growth, outperforming Patanjali.

More recently, Patanjali has diversified its business, including the acquisition of Ruchi Soya (now Patanjali Foods Ltd.), giving it a significant foothold in the edible oil market. Patanjali Foods reported its highest net sales for the quarter at ₹10,483.71 crore for the nine months ended December 2025, with revenues growing at an annualized rate of 21.42%. The company's total income for the full financial year 2024-25 stood at ₹34,289.40 crore. However, the overall Patanjali Ayurved entity has seen its market presence normalize somewhat, even as its broader group (Patanjali Ayurved and Patanjali Foods Ltd. combined) aims for an even larger footprint, valued at over ₹45,000 crore as of 2024-25.

### Lessons to Take Away

1.  **Harnessing Cultural Identity as a Strategic Asset:** Patanjali demonstrated that a strong cultural and nationalistic narrative, like 'Swadeshi' combined with the trust in Ayurveda, can be a powerful differentiator in a crowded market. It allowed them to create an emotional connection with consumers beyond mere product features or price.
2.  **Disruption Through Value and Accessibility:** Instead of competing head-on with MNCs on their terms, Patanjali disrupted the market by offering perceived "superior" natural products at significantly lower price points, making them accessible to a vast, price-sensitive Indian population, particularly in rural areas.
3.  **The Power of an Authentic Brand Ambassador:** Baba Ramdev's direct association and personal brand were invaluable. He served as a credible, low-cost brand ambassador, fostering trust and awareness through non-traditional channels like yoga camps and television discourses, proving that authentic leadership can be a potent marketing tool.
4.  **Agility in Product Expansion and Distribution:** Patanjali's rapid diversification across numerous FMCG categories and its innovative, hybrid distribution model (from exclusive stores to modern retail and deep rural penetration) allowed it to quickly capture market share and challenge established players across multiple fronts.
5.  **MNCs are not Invincible:** The Patanjali story served as a stark reminder to multinational corporations that even deeply entrenched market leaders can be challenged by agile, locally resonant players who understand and cater to evolving consumer sentiments and unmet needs. It forced them to innovate and adapt their own product strategies to counter the 'natural' and 'Swadeshi' wave.
