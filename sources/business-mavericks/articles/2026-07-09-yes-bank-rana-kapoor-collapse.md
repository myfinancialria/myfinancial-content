## Yes Bank: How Rana Kapoor Built India's Fastest-Growing Bank Then Personally Destroyed It

In the nascent years of India's private banking boom, Rana Kapoor harbored a vision that was audacious, even grandiose. He didn't just want to build a bank; he wanted to build "the finest quality large bank of the world in India". It was 2003, and with co-founders Ashok Kapur and Harkirat Singh, the stage was set for Yes Bank. What followed was an astonishing sprint of growth, transforming a startup into a behemoth with ₹3.5 lakh crore in assets within 14 years. Yet, this tale of meteoric rise is inextricably linked to a dramatic fall, a cautionary saga of ambition unchecked, where personal empire-building eclipsed sound banking principles, ultimately leading to the bank's near collapse and Kapoor's arrest.

### The World Before

The early 2000s marked a transformative period for India's banking sector. Following economic liberalisation, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) began issuing licenses to new private banks, challenging the long-held dominance of public sector lenders. This era saw the emergence of institutions like HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank, and Kotak Mahindra Bank, each carving out niches in a rapidly expanding economy. The landscape was ripe with opportunities, but also fraught with challenges. Public sector banks often grappled with inefficiencies and poor loan underwriting, while the corporate debt market was still underdeveloped, leaving a gap for project financing.

Against this backdrop, Yes Bank entered the fray in 2004, receiving its banking license from the RBI. Its initial strategy was distinct: focusing on "knowledge-based corporate banking, structured finance, and investment banking". Rather than chasing the mass retail market immediately, Kapoor aimed at carving out a niche by serving small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) — businesses often overlooked by larger, more conservative banks. The idea was to grow with these emerging companies, participating in their ascent. This "can-do" ethos, as embodied by the bank's name, was designed to emphasize a customer-first approach and a willingness to innovate.

### The Crack

Under Rana Kapoor's relentless leadership, Yes Bank's growth was phenomenal. He was known to work over 18 hours a day, micromanaging almost every aspect of the business, driven by an intense desire to propel Yes Bank to the top tier of Indian private banks. By 2015, the bank had evolved from the "largest small private sector bank" in 2010 to the "largest medium-sized private sector bank," leveraging technology and a focus on new-age sectors like renewable energy and media. Its market share in loans and deposits reportedly tripled in less than a decade by FY2019.

However, this aggressive growth came at a cost. The bank increasingly became a "lender of the last resort," extending credit to corporate groups and projects that more conservative banks deemed too risky. The list of borrowers included some of India's most prominent, yet financially troubled, entities. Yes Bank had significant exposure to the Anil Ambani-led Reliance Group, Dewan Housing Finance Corporation (DHFL), Cafe Coffee Day, Jet Airways, Cox & Kings, and the Avantha group.

The cracks began to show by mid-2017. The RBI, during its asset quality reviews, forced Yes Bank to disclose that its non-performing assets (NPAs) were a staggering $630 million higher than the $113 million it had reported for the financial year ending March 2016. This divergence, or understatement of bad loans, widened to almost $1 billion a year later. Despite Kapoor's fierce denials, often attributing the bank's seemingly low NPA figures to its "small and nimble" size, questions mounted about the true health of its loan book.

The RBI's concerns extended beyond just asset quality; corporate governance issues at Yes Bank also came under scrutiny. In September 2018, a pivotal moment arrived when the RBI, citing these concerns, refused to extend Rana Kapoor's tenure as Managing Director and CEO beyond January 31, 2019. This decision sent shockwaves through the market, causing Yes Bank's shares to plummet over 30% in a single day, wiping out $3.1 billion in market value and closing at ₹227.05.

Kapoor's involvement in alleged irregularities deepened. The Enforcement Directorate (ED) later initiated a probe into accusations that he received kickbacks worth ₹600 crore through a family-linked company in exchange for loans sanctioned to DHFL. The CBI also charged that Yes Bank lost over ₹2,700 crore due to Kapoor's "unilateral decision" to invest in Anil Ambani's financial companies.

By late 2019, the situation became critical. Rating agencies began downgrading Yes Bank's debt, triggering a wave of deposit withdrawals. The bank's gross NPAs had dramatically doubled between April and September 2019, reaching ₹17,134 crore. In November 2019, Kapoor even sold all of his remaining Yes Bank shares. Without fresh capital, the bank's liquidity rapidly deteriorated, setting the stage for an unprecedented intervention.

### What Happened Next

The inevitable arrived on March 5, 2020. In a drastic move to prevent a systemic crisis, the Reserve Bank of India imposed a 30-day moratorium on Yes Bank, superseding its board of directors. Depositors were blindsided, with withdrawals capped at a mere ₹50,000 per person. The news sparked widespread panic, with customers rushing to ATMs, fearful for their savings.

A swift rescue operation was orchestrated by the government and the RBI. The State Bank of India (SBI) stepped in to lead a consortium of investors, including HDFC, ICICI Bank, and Axis Bank. SBI committed to investing a maximum of ₹10,000 crore, initially injecting ₹2,450 crore and acquiring a 49% stake in the restructured bank. The moratorium was lifted prematurely on March 18, 2020, just two weeks after it was imposed, as the reconstruction scheme gained traction. Crucially, general depositors did not incur losses, although holders of Additional Tier 1 (AT1) bonds faced a complete write-down.

For Rana Kapoor, the architect of Yes Bank's rise, the fall was swift and absolute. He was arrested by the ED on March 8, 2020, under money laundering charges, accused of receiving kickbacks amounting to ₹43 billion (US$450 million). After more than four years in judicial custody, he was granted bail in April 2024.

The financial impact on Yes Bank and its shareholders was severe. After hitting a lifetime high of ₹393.85 on August 20, 2018, the stock began its precipitous decline. By the time of the crisis, it had crashed nearly 85% in a year, and between August and September 2019 alone, shareholders lost 84% of their value, with the stock plunging from ₹366 to ₹60. The bank's deposits plummeted by 54% between September 2019 and March 2020, falling to ₹1.1 trillion. Post-rescue, the bank began a slow recovery; its deposit base rebounded to ₹2.2 trillion by June 2023. As of July 2026, Yes Bank's total assets stand at around ₹4.7 lakh crore, with a market capitalization of approximately ₹74,418 crore.

### Lessons to Take Away

1.  **The Perils of Unchecked Aggression:** Yes Bank's rapid ascent was fueled by aggressive lending, particularly to sectors and entities that other banks deemed too risky. While this strategy initially drove growth, it concentrated risk, making the bank highly vulnerable to economic downturns and the financial distress of its key borrowers. A growth-at-all-costs mindset, without robust risk management, is a ticking time bomb.
2.  **Corporate Governance is Paramount:** The Yes Bank saga underscores the critical importance of independent and vigilant corporate governance. Rana Kapoor's alleged micromanagement and the reported inability of independent directors to question his decisions allowed unsound practices to fester unchecked. A strong board and ethical leadership are not mere formalities; they are essential bulwarks against financial misconduct and systemic risk.
3.  **Regulatory Scrutiny is a Lifeline (Even When Resisted):** The RBI's persistent scrutiny, particularly its insistence on accurate NPA disclosures and its refusal to extend Kapoor's term, proved crucial, even if belated. While difficult in the short term, regulatory oversight acts as a necessary check on private ambition, safeguarding the broader financial system and, ultimately, public trust.
4.  **Interconnectedness Amplifies Crisis:** The collapse of Yes Bank sent tremors across the Indian financial sector, highlighting how interconnected the system is. The failure of a single large private bank can quickly cascade into a wider crisis, impacting depositors, other financial institutions, and the economy as a whole. This interconnectedness necessitates swift, coordinated action from regulators and government to prevent systemic contagion.
