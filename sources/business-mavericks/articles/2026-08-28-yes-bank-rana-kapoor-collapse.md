## The Day the ATMs Went Dry: When Yes Bank Froze

It was the evening of March 5, 2020. Across India, millions of Yes Bank customers found themselves locked out. Their debit cards declined, UPI payments failed, and ATM screens flashed "transaction denied." The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) had imposed a 30-day moratorium on Yes Bank, capping withdrawals at an astonishingly low ₹50,000 per depositor. Panic rippled through India's financial system. How could a bank, once hailed as the fastest-growing private lender, crumble so spectacularly? The answer lay in a dramatic tale of ambition, unchecked power, and a lending spree orchestrated by its charismatic founder, Rana Kapoor.

## The World Before: A Maverick's Entry

In the early 2000s, India's banking landscape was largely dominated by state-owned behemoths and a handful of established private players like HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank. Breaking into this entrenched sector was no small feat. Yet, in 2003, Rana Kapoor, a seasoned banker with a background spanning Bank of America and ANZ Grindlays, co-founded Yes Bank with his brother-in-law, Ashok Kapur. Their vision, as articulated by Kapoor, was to build "the finest quality large bank of the world in India."

Yes Bank received its banking license in May 2004 and opened its first branch in August of the same year. Initially, Kapoor aimed to cater to small and medium enterprises (SMEs), a segment often overlooked by larger banks. This strategy allowed the bank to grow steadily, building a reputation for being agile and customer-centric. However, the tragic 2008 Mumbai terrorist attacks claimed Ashok Kapur's life, leaving Rana Kapoor in sole command. With his co-founder gone, Kapoor's ambition shifted into overdrive, setting the stage for aggressive growth that would eventually unravel the institution he had built.

## The Crack: A "Yes" to Every Risky Bet

Under Rana Kapoor's singular leadership, Yes Bank embarked on a period of hyper-growth. Its balance sheet swelled from approximately $10 billion in loans by 2014 to $40 billion (around ₹2.7 lakh crore) by 2018. By its peak, the bank's total assets reached roughly ₹3.5 lakh crore. Kapoor’s strategy was simple: lend aggressively, especially to large corporate groups. This earned Yes Bank the moniker of being the bank that always said 'Yes,' even when other lenders turned away.

However, this aggressive lending came with a severe cost. Yes Bank began extending massive loans to companies already under financial stress or those struggling to secure funding elsewhere. Among its most prominent, and eventually problematic, borrowers were:

*   **Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Ltd (DHFL):** Yes Bank invested ₹3,700 crore in DHFL's short-term debentures. In return, DHFL's promoter, Kapil Wadhawan, allegedly paid a kickback of ₹600 crore to DoIT Urban Ventures (India) Private Limited, a company controlled by Kapoor's wife and daughters, disguised as a builder loan. The CBI later stated that DHFL also sanctioned over ₹400 crore in loans to companies owned by co-accused Sanjay Chhabriaa without following norms, shortly after receiving Yes Bank's funds.
*   **Anil Ambani Group:** Multiple companies within the Anil Ambani conglomerate received substantial loans from Yes Bank.
*   **Cafe Coffee Day (CCD) / Essel Group:** The bank had significant exposure to these groups, which later faced severe financial difficulties.
*   **IL&FS and Jet Airways:** These financially troubled entities also found Yes Bank willing to lend.

For a time, this strategy painted a rosy picture. High-interest income fueled profits, allowing Yes Bank to pay good dividends and keep shareholders, including Kapoor, happy. However, the quality of these loans steadily deteriorated. The RBI, India's banking regulator, began to raise red flags as early as 2017, forcing Yes Bank to disclose a divergence of $630 million in non-performing loans (NPAs) from its reported figures for the year ended March 2016. This divergence widened to nearly $1 billion a year later.

Kapoor, however, remained outwardly confident, often assuring regulators that he would "take care" of bad loans. But the "taking care" often involved evergreening loans or providing fresh, larger loans to troubled borrowers, allowing them to repay older dues, thus masking the true extent of the stress.

By 2018, regulatory patience wore thin. The RBI cut short Kapoor's term as CEO, asking the bank to find a new leader by February 2019. This marked the beginning of the end of Kapoor's reign. With his influence waning and the bank struggling to raise fresh capital, Kapoor sold his entire stake in Yes Bank by November 2019. The departure of its founder only intensified investor fears, triggering a rapid decline in the bank's share price.

The culmination came on March 5, 2020, when the RBI superseded Yes Bank's board, citing failures to raise new funding, inaccurate statements, and underreporting of NPAs. A moratorium was declared, freezing customer withdrawals and plunging the bank into crisis.

## What Happened Next: Rescue and Rebuilding

The immediate aftermath was chaotic. On March 8, 2020, Rana Kapoor was arrested by the Enforcement Directorate (ED) under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA) in connection with alleged fraud exceeding $100 million, specifically linked to the DHFL case. He was accused of receiving kickbacks worth ₹43 billion (US$450 million) through family-controlled companies for sanctioning large loans. Kapoor was granted bail in April 2024 after four years in judicial custody.

To prevent a systemic collapse, the Indian government and RBI swiftly orchestrated a rescue. On March 13, 2020, the Union Cabinet approved a reconstruction scheme led by the State Bank of India (SBI). SBI committed to taking a 49% stake in Yes Bank, initially investing ₹7,250 crore, with a consortium of other private banks like ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, Axis Bank, and Kotak Mahindra Bank also injecting capital. Prashant Kumar, a former deputy managing director of SBI, was appointed as the new MD & CEO.

The moratorium was lifted on March 18, 2020, allowing customers full access to their funds. However, a controversial aspect of the rescue was the permanent write-off of Yes Bank's Additional Tier 1 (AT1) bonds, impacting thousands of investors.

The financial impact on Yes Bank was severe. Its market capitalization, which had crossed ₹1 lakh crore in August 2018, plummeted to less than ₹5,000 crore on March 6, 2020, with shares plunging nearly 85%. From a lifetime high of ₹393.85 on August 20, 2018, the stock closed at ₹227.05 a month later after the RBI's decision to curtail Kapoor's term.

In the years following the bailout, Yes Bank embarked on a painstaking journey of recovery. Under Prashant Kumar, the bank focused on cleaning up its balance sheet, selling ₹48,000 crore of stressed assets to JC Flowers Asset Reconstruction Company, and recovering over ₹13,000 crore in bad loans. The lending strategy shifted from high-risk corporate exposures to a greater focus on retail and MSME segments, which now account for 60% of its loan book. New investors, including private equity players Carlyle and Advent, infused fresh capital, and in 2025, Japan's Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation (SMBC) announced plans to acquire a significant stake.

By Q1 FY27 (April-June 2026), Yes Bank reported a net profit of ₹1,071 crore, its first time crossing the ₹1,000 crore mark since the crisis. Gross NPAs had declined significantly to 1.3%, a sharp improvement from its crisis peak. The bank's advances grew by 18.4% year-on-year to ₹2.85 lakh crore, and deposits rose by 14% to ₹3.15 lakh crore. While challenges remain, Yes Bank has demonstrably put its crisis years behind it.

## Lessons to Take Away

1.  **Unchecked Ambition Can Be a Bank's Worst Enemy:** Rana Kapoor's relentless pursuit of growth, divorced from prudent risk management, proved catastrophic. The desire to be "the fastest-growing" overshadowed the imperative to be "the most stable." For any business, understanding the sustainable pace of growth and the inherent risks associated with aggressive expansion is paramount.
2.  **Corporate Governance is the Bedrock of Trust:** The Yes Bank saga highlighted severe lapses in corporate governance, including alleged kickbacks and underreporting of stressed assets. A strong board, independent directors, and transparent reporting are not just regulatory requirements but essential pillars for maintaining public and investor trust, especially in a financial institution.
3.  **The Perils of Concentrated Lending:** Yes Bank's significant exposure to a few large, high-risk corporate groups created an Achilles' heel. When these borrowers defaulted, the impact on the bank was disproportionately severe. Diversifying a loan book and avoiding excessive concentration in risky sectors are fundamental principles of sound banking.
4.  **Regulatory Oversight is a Continuous Process:** While the RBI eventually intervened decisively, the crisis also raised questions about the timing and effectiveness of earlier regulatory actions. For any regulated industry, consistent and proactive supervision is crucial to identify and address systemic risks before they escalate into full-blown crises.
5.  **The Power of Systemic Support:** The government and RBI's swift and comprehensive rescue package was critical in preventing a broader financial contagion. This underscores that in sectors vital to the national economy, collective action and a robust regulatory framework can mitigate the impact of individual institutional failures.
