The year is 2020. The world is grappling with an unprecedented lockdown, and in India, a different kind of challenge looms for businesses built on proximity and instant gratification. For Sriharsha Majety, co-founder and CEO of Swiggy, the moment presented both an existential threat and an audacious opportunity. His food delivery giant, born in the bustling streets of Bengaluru, was about to embark on a pivot that few saw coming: diving headfirst into quick commerce with Instamart. "Instamart delivers groceries in 10 minutes. Two years ago people said that was impossible in India. Now it is expected," Majety would later reflect, encapsulating the dramatic shift in consumer expectations and the company's pivotal role in shaping it. This wasn't just a new service; it was a defiant move against the prevailing asset-light startup playbook, one that would redefine convenience for millions of Indians.

## The World Before

Before 2020, India's online delivery landscape was largely defined by food. Swiggy and its arch-rival, Zomato, were locked in a fierce battle for market share, each striving to be the fastest and most reliable conduit between hungry customers and their favourite restaurants. The prevailing wisdom for digital platforms was to remain "asset-light"—meaning, avoid owning inventory or physical infrastructure. Companies connected buyers and sellers, leaving the heavy lifting of warehousing, stocking, and direct sales to traditional retailers or third-party partners. This model minimized capital expenditure and allowed for rapid scaling, a crucial factor in India's competitive startup ecosystem.

Grocery delivery did exist, with players like BigBasket offering scheduled deliveries and Amazon Fresh making inroads. However, the idea of "quick commerce"—delivering groceries and daily essentials in 10-15 minutes—was largely unproven and considered a logistical nightmare. The Indian market, with its dense urban centres, chaotic traffic, and fragmented retail, made such promises seem foolhardy. Setting up and managing a network of dedicated micro-warehouses, or "dark stores" (small warehouses exclusively for online orders, inaccessible to walk-in customers), was a significant departure from the asset-light model. It meant tying up capital in real estate, inventory, and operational staff, increasing fixed costs dramatically. In 2020, the quick commerce market in India was a nascent USD 0.91 billion, indicating the immense untapped potential, but also the scale of the challenge.

## The Crack

The crack in this conventional thinking emerged in August 2020, amidst the initial waves of the COVID-19 pandemic. With lockdowns accelerating consumer preference for doorstep deliveries of everything, not just food, Swiggy saw an opportunity to extend its core mission of "unparalleled convenience." That month, Swiggy launched Instamart, starting with Gurugram. The initial promise was delivery within 45 minutes, a stark contrast to the 10-15 minute benchmark it would later champion. This wasn't a fully fleshed-out strategy but an experiment, an "add-on" within the main Swiggy app.

Sriharsha Majety, with his characteristic conviction, saw beyond the immediate chaos. He believed that if Swiggy could master food delivery in Indian traffic, they could deliver anything. "India has 1.4 billion people and most of them are discovering the convenience of delivery for the first time. The market is just starting," he observed. The company's earlier attempt at grocery, "Swiggy Stores," which listed products from local shops, was soon consolidated under the Instamart banner, signalling a more focused approach.

The core of Instamart's strategy was the establishment of a vast network of dark stores. These were not traditional retail outlets, but strategically located mini-warehouses, often 2,500 to 4,000 square feet in size, stocked with fast-moving consumer goods, groceries, and household essentials. By placing these fulfilment centres close to densely populated urban areas, Swiggy aimed to dramatically cut down delivery times. Orders placed on the app would be processed, picked, and packed by in-store staff, then handed to Swiggy's existing fleet of delivery partners, who would then complete the last-mile delivery, typically on two-wheelers.

This vertical integration, owning the inventory and the distribution hubs, was a bold move. It required substantial capital. In December 2021, Swiggy announced a massive $700 million investment into Instamart, a clear signal of their commitment to this new quick commerce vertical. This investment came at a time when competition in the nascent 10-minute grocery delivery space was heating up, with new entrants like Zepto and Zomato-backed Grofers (later Blinkit) also pivoting to ultra-fast models.

Building this network was fraught with challenges. The initial setup cost for a single dark store could range from INR 50-75 lakh. Operational hurdles included maintaining real-time inventory synchronization across multiple locations, managing perishable goods to prevent expiry and wastage, and ensuring consistent product availability. The regulatory environment for these new-age "dark stores" also lacked clarity, leading to scrutiny from bodies like the Competition Commission of India (CCI) regarding transparency and compliance.

Despite these complexities, Swiggy pressed on. They leveraged their superior technology and AI-driven logistics to optimize delivery routes and streamline operations. Instamart wasn't just about groceries; it rapidly expanded its product range to include electronics, beauty products, fashion, and home essentials, aiming to become a comprehensive convenience platform. Majety's confidence was palpable: "Our Instamart business is growing like a weed," he declared in November 2024.

## What Happened Next

The audacious bet paid off. By November 2024, when Swiggy went public, Instamart was no longer a side experiment. It contributed approximately 40% of Swiggy's consolidated gross order value (GOV) – a measure of the total value of goods sold through the platform. This remarkable growth transformed Instamart into a significant pillar of Swiggy's business, strengthening its position against rivals like Zomato and newer quick commerce players like Zepto.

Instamart's expansion was rapid and widespread. From its initial launch in Gurugram, it quickly scaled to 28 cities by April 2022, reached 100 cities by April 2025, and by July 2026, operated across 131 cities with 1,171 dark stores. This extensive network, comprising over 1,062 dark stores by late 2025, formed a robust infrastructure, allowing Swiggy to claim it had "created sufficient capacity on the dark store network to easily double our business from here without having the need to add more stores."

While Swiggy's food delivery business achieved EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization) profitability for FY23, Instamart, with its aggressive expansion, initially incurred higher losses. However, the company demonstrated steady improvement in its unit economics. Instamart's contribution margin, which measures how much revenue is left after covering variable costs directly associated with an order, improved from a negative 5.6% in Q4 FY25 to a negative 0.2% in Q1 FY26. This indicated a clear path towards profitability, driven by increased order density, a wider product assortment (including higher-margin non-grocery items which rose to 26% of sales in Q2 FY26 from 9% a year earlier), and growing advertising revenue. Swiggy also announced plans to shift Instamart to an inventory-led model, which could further improve contribution margins by about 80 basis points through bulk purchasing and better supply chain management.

Instamart created a powerful "moat" against competitors. By leveraging Swiggy's existing 5.4 lakh delivery partners and a loyal customer base, it offered an unparalleled convenience ecosystem. The diversification into quick commerce transformed Swiggy from a mere food delivery app into a "super-app" for hyperlocal needs, making it harder for rivals to compete solely on food. Majety's belief that "In India market share is destiny" drove this aggressive expansion, creating a formidable presence in the rapidly growing quick commerce market, which itself expanded from USD 0.91 billion in 2020 to an estimated USD 7.46 billion in 2026.

## Lessons to Take Away

1.  **Strategic Experimentation Can Unlock New Markets:** Instamart began as a relatively small experiment within Swiggy's larger food delivery operations. Its success demonstrates the power of allowing agile teams to test new ideas and, if successful, scale them aggressively. The initial 45-minute delivery promise was a testing ground that quickly evolved into the 10-15 minute standard.
2.  **Defy Conventional Wisdom When the Market Demands It:** In an era where "asset-light" was the mantra for tech startups, Swiggy's decision to invest heavily in dark stores and inventory was counter-intuitive. However, this vertical integration was essential to guarantee the speed and reliability that defines quick commerce. When a market need is strong enough, breaking from established playbooks can create a significant competitive advantage.
3.  **Leverage Existing Infrastructure and Customer Trust:** Swiggy didn't start Instamart from scratch. It capitalized on its extensive network of delivery partners, its sophisticated logistics technology, and a massive, already engaged customer base. This allowed for faster scaling and reduced customer acquisition costs (CAC) compared to a standalone quick commerce startup.
4.  **Indian Consumers Value Convenience, Within Reason:** Majety famously stated, "The biggest lesson from our journey is that Indian consumers will pay for convenience. They just will not overpay." Instamart’s success underscores this point: speed and ease of access to daily essentials became a premium service that a significant segment of the urban population was willing to pay for, creating a viable business model despite operational complexities.
5.  **Long-Term Vision Fuels Aggressive Growth:** Despite initial losses and the significant investment required, Swiggy continued to pour capital into Instamart. This commitment, rooted in Majety's belief in the long-term potential of India's quick commerce market, allowed the business to achieve critical mass and move towards profitability, showcasing the importance of patience and strategic investment over immediate returns.
