## Why CRED Spent ₹400 Cr on 'Useless' Celebrity Ads — and Cracked Premium Distribution

The year was 2021. India, gripped by the IPL frenzy, was accustomed to a predictable parade of cricketers endorsing everything from soft drinks to cement. Then, something jarring happened. Rahul Dravid, "The Wall," India's famously calm and composed cricketing legend, appeared on screens not in his usual dignified avatar, but as "Indiranagar ka Gunda" – a furious, bat-wielding maniac stuck in Bengaluru traffic, yelling epithets and smashing side mirrors. The internet exploded. Virat Kohli himself tweeted, "Never seen this side of Rahul bhai."

This deliberately absurd, almost cringe-worthy commercial was from CRED, a fintech startup that rewarded users for paying credit card bills. It followed a similar campaign from 2020 featuring Bollywood icons like Anil Kapoor, Madhuri Dixit, and Bappi Lahiri awkwardly "auditioning" for a CRED ad, seemingly poking fun at the very concept of celebrity endorsements. These ads felt expensive, bizarre, and utterly uninformative about the product. Critics questioned the wisdom of burning hundreds of crores on such "useless" marketing. Yet, these campaigns weren't a misstep; they were a masterstroke in building a premium, aspirational brand.

### The World Before CRED: A Transactional Grind

Before CRED arrived on the scene in 2018, the Indian credit card market was a rapidly growing, yet largely unglamorous affair. While the number of credit cards in circulation was increasing—crossing 78 million by July 2022—only about 3% of the Indian population held a formal credit card. For most users, managing credit cards was a functional, often tedious task. Payments were transactional, and while banks offered loyalty points, the experience rarely felt rewarding or premium. The market was dominated by a few large players, with the top four issuers holding a significant 71.83% market share by November 2021.

Fintech companies were emerging, attempting to simplify payments and financial management. However, the core challenge remained: how do you make paying bills, a fundamentally mundane activity, exciting? CRED's initial attempts at marketing were conventional, including a print ad criticised for being illegible and an explainer video featuring actor Jim Sarbh that received a "lukewarm response." The market was cluttered, and a direct, feature-heavy approach wasn't cutting through the noise. The prevailing wisdom was to explain your product clearly and convince as many users as possible to sign up. CRED, under founder Kunal Shah, was about to flip this script entirely.

### The Crack: Building a "Velvet Rope" with Absurdity

CRED's strategy was built on two audacious pillars: exclusivity and deliberately absurd marketing.

First, **exclusivity**. Unlike almost every other app vying for mass adoption, CRED wasn't for everyone. From its inception, the platform restricted membership to individuals with a high credit score, typically 750 or above. This "velvet rope" approach instantly turned a utilitarian app into a status symbol. Being a CRED member wasn't just about paying bills; it was about being part of an elite club, a recognition of one's financial discipline. Users began sharing their successful sign-up screens on social media, providing free, organic marketing and reinforcing the brand's aspirational image. Kunal Shah articulated this by stating CRED aimed to build a "trust brand" rather than a "product brand," comparing it to luxury brand building.

Second, and most visibly, came the **absurd advertising**. CRED made a significant investment, becoming an official sponsor of the Indian Premier League (IPL) for three years from 2020 to 2022, a deal reportedly worth ₹120 crore. This was their stage for disruption.

In 2020, their IPL campaign featured 90s Bollywood stars like Anil Kapoor, Madhuri Dixit, and Bappi Lahiri in self-deprecating skits, humorously failing auditions for CRED ads. The tagline, "Not Everyone Gets It," subtly reinforced the exclusivity. The goal wasn't to explain the app, but to create immense curiosity.

Then came 2021, and the "Indiranagar ka Gunda" ad featuring Rahul Dravid. It was an instant viral sensation, dominating social media conversations and sparking a flurry of memes. Other ads in the series featured Jackie Shroff doing Zumba and Kapil Dev comically struggling. These ads were intentionally uninformative about the app's functionality. Instead, they leveraged nostalgia, persona subversion (seeing calm Dravid lose his cool), and sheer entertainment to grab attention. As one expert noted, the team behind the ads drew from a "reservoir of quirky humour" tailored for "upmarket urban Indians."

The cost was substantial. While the IPL sponsorship itself was ₹120 crore over three years, CRED's total marketing and advertising expenses were significant. In fiscal year 2020, high marketing and advertising expenditure contributed to losses of ₹360.31 crore. By fiscal year 2021, marketing expenses surged to ₹418 crore, driven by heavy IPL and ad campaign spending. For the 2021 IPL season alone, the total ad spend was estimated to be around ₹400 crore. This aggressive spend on seemingly "useless" ads was a deliberate, long-term play. Kunal Shah consciously framed it as building "enduring brand equity and trust that would deliver value over years rather than immediate quarters."

And it worked. The sheer virality and talkability of these campaigns generated massive brand recall and organic curiosity. Sequoia Capital, an early investor, reported a "six to seven times increase in daily sign-ups" during CRED's IPL campaigns.

### What Happened Next: Soaring Valuations and Expanding Reach

The audacious marketing strategy paid off in spades. CRED's user base grew from 5.9 million in 2021 to 12 million by 2024, with 13 million monthly active users reported by June 2024.

Financially, the company's trajectory was remarkable. CRED achieved unicorn status, meaning a valuation of over $1 billion, in April 2021, reaching $2.2 billion. This valuation continued to climb, reaching $5.5 billion by October 2021 and peaking at $6.4 billion in June 2022.

Revenue also saw significant growth, soaring from ₹18.14 crore in FY20 to ₹95.5 crore in FY21. By FY24, CRED's revenue had reached an impressive ₹2,473 crore (approximately $260 million), demonstrating a 66% year-on-year growth. While still incurring losses, the company showed a clear path to profitability, cutting operating losses by 41% in FY24 to ₹609 crore.

CRED also cemented its position in the Indian credit card payments ecosystem, processing an estimated 20% of all credit card bill payments in India by April 2021, a figure that rose to 22% by FY24. The company leveraged its engaged, premium user base to expand its offerings, including house rent payments, short-term credit lines (CRED Cash), and a marketplace of premium products and experiences.

### Lessons to Take Away

CRED's journey from a niche fintech app to a multi-billion dollar company, propelled by its "useless" ads, offers several potent lessons for businesses targeting a discerning audience:

1.  **Exclusivity as a Differentiator:** In a world where every company chases maximum users, deliberately limiting access can create immense aspiration. CRED proved that a "velvet rope" strategy can turn a utility into a coveted status symbol, driving organic desire and loyalty among a high-value customer segment.
2.  **Brand Building Over Product Explanation:** For a sophisticated target audience, detailed product features can be secondary to brand identity. CRED's ads didn't explain *how* the app worked; they conveyed a *vibe* – smart, quirky, rewarding – that resonated with its financially responsible users. When your audience already understands the core utility (like paying credit card bills), focus on making the brand itself desirable.
3.  **The Power of Absurdity and Disruption:** In a cluttered advertising landscape, breaking conventions and embracing absurdity can be incredibly effective. CRED's willingness to make highly paid celebrities do ridiculous things created unmissable, shareable content that generated massive buzz and cultural relevance, far beyond what a conventional ad could achieve.
4.  **Strategic Long-Term Investment in Brand Equity:** While the initial ad spend seemed exorbitant and contributed to significant losses, Kunal Shah viewed it as a long-term investment in building a "trust brand" similar to luxury brands. This patient, strategic approach to brand building, rather than immediate ROI from marketing, ultimately yielded substantial returns in user acquisition and valuation.
5.  **Understanding Your Audience's Psychology:** CRED didn't just target credit card users; it targeted *financially responsible* credit card users. The ads, with their sophisticated humor and unexpected celebrity cameos, appealed directly to this demographic's intelligence and their appreciation for things "not everyone gets," reinforcing their self-perception as discerning individuals.

CRED's rise demonstrates that sometimes, the most effective marketing isn't about telling people what your product does, but about making them desperately want to be part of what your brand *represents*.
