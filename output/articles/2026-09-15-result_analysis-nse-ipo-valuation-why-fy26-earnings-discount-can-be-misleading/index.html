NSE IPO Valuation: Why FY26 Earnings Discount Can Be Misleading

TL;DR

  • The National Stock Exchange (NSE) IPO's proposed price band of ₹1,700-₹1,785 might appear attractive due to a perceived valuation discount compared to BSE.
  • This apparent discount is often calculated using projected earnings for Financial Year 2026 (FY26), which are estimates and subject to significant uncertainty.
  • Future earnings estimates for stock exchanges are highly dependent on market volumes, regulatory changes, and economic growth, making them volatile.
  • A superficial comparison based purely on future P/E ratios might overlook fundamental differences in business models, market share, and growth drivers between NSE and BSE.
  • Investors should conduct thorough due diligence, focusing on current financials, business fundamentals, and the inherent risks of relying on long-term projections for IPOs.

What this means in plain terms

When a company like the NSE goes public with an Initial Public Offering (IPO), investors try to figure out if the share price is fair. One common way to do this is by looking at its Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio, which compares the share price to the company's annual profit per share. Often, analysts will use not just past or current profits, but also future estimated profits, like for FY26, to calculate a forward P/E.

The issue arises when an IPO looks "cheap" compared to a competitor (like BSE) because its forward P/E based on distant future earnings (FY26) seems lower. This can be misleading because those future earnings are just projections and can change drastically. Market conditions, new regulations, or unexpected events can significantly alter a company's actual profits years down the line. Relying heavily on such distant estimates for an IPO, especially in a dynamic sector like stock exchanges, can give an incomplete and potentially overly optimistic picture of its true value.

Understanding IPO Valuations and Future Projections

Valuing an IPO is a complex exercise, often involving a blend of art and science. For investors, the goal is to determine if the offer price represents a fair entry point into the company's ownership. A primary metric used is the Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio, which helps gauge how much investors are willing to pay for each rupee of a company's earnings.

The Role of P/E Ratios in Valuations

The P/E ratio is calculated by dividing the share price by the Earnings Per Share (EPS). A higher P/E generally indicates that investors expect higher future growth, or that the stock is considered more stable and desirable. Conversely, a lower P/E might suggest lower growth expectations or that the stock is undervalued. In IPOs, both historical and projected P/E ratios are considered. Historical P/E uses past earnings, while forward P/E uses estimated future earnings. For an IPO like NSE, which has a strong market position, analysts often rely heavily on forward earnings to justify the valuation, anticipating continued growth.

Why FY26 Earnings Estimates Can Be Tricky

The Livemint report highlights that the apparent discount for NSE's IPO against BSE is based on FY26 earnings. Projecting earnings two to three years into the future, especially for a capital market infrastructure provider like a stock exchange, involves significant assumptions. These assumptions include:

  • Market Volumes: Future equity and derivatives trading volumes are highly sensitive to economic growth, interest rates, corporate earnings, and investor sentiment. A slowdown in any of these can drastically impact an exchange's revenue.
  • Regulatory Changes: The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) frequently introduces new regulations, changes transaction charges, or alters market structures. Such changes can directly affect an exchange's profitability.
  • Competition: While NSE and BSE dominate, new trading platforms or changes in listing preferences could impact their market share.
  • New Business Segments: Projections might factor in significant revenue from new ventures (e.g., data services, technology solutions), which may not materialise as expected.

Therefore, while FY26 earnings provide a benchmark, they are inherently speculative. Investors must critically evaluate the underlying assumptions behind these projections.

The NSE IPO: Key Numbers and the FY26 Estimate

The National Stock Exchange (NSE) is India's largest stock exchange by turnover, particularly dominant in the derivatives segment. Its IPO has been long-anticipated, and the proposed price band of ₹1,700-₹1,785 per share sets the stage for its public listing.

Deconstructing the IPO Price Band

At the suggested price band, the valuation of NSE will be substantial, reflecting its market leadership and profitability. However, the article points out that the "steep valuation discount to BSE based on FY26 earnings is misleading." This implies that while a simple calculation might show NSE's forward P/E (based on FY26 estimates) to be lower than BSE's current or projected P/E, this comparison needs deeper scrutiny.

The Problem with Distant Projections

The core issue is that FY26 earnings are still a distant target. For a company in a cyclical industry like financial markets, projections that far out are highly susceptible to changes in the macroeconomic environment. For instance, a global recession, a domestic economic slowdown, or even significant policy shifts by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) or the Ministry of Finance could impact trading volumes and, consequently, NSE's revenues and profits.

Moreover, exchanges derive a significant portion of their revenue from transaction charges, listing fees, and data dissemination services. Growth in these areas is not linear and can be disrupted. Relying on a projected FY26 EPS figure without understanding the granular assumptions and potential risks embedded in that projection can lead to an oversimplified and potentially flawed valuation conclusion.

Comparing NSE and BSE: Beyond the Superficial Discount

While both NSE and BSE are India's premier stock exchanges, a direct P/E comparison based on future earnings can be misleading due to fundamental differences in their business models, market positioning, and growth trajectories.

Market Share and Business Mix

NSE holds a commanding lead in the equity derivatives segment and has a larger share of cash equity market turnover compared to BSE. This dominance translates into higher revenue generation from high-volume, high-frequency trading activities. BSE, while having a broader range of products, has a smaller market share in the equity and derivatives segments. However, BSE has diversified into areas like mutual fund distribution (BSE Star MF) and has a strong presence in the SME listing segment. These different business mixes mean their revenue streams and growth drivers are not perfectly comparable.

Growth Drivers and Future Outlook

NSE's growth is heavily tied to the expansion of India's capital markets, particularly in derivatives and institutional trading. Its technological infrastructure and widespread reach give it an edge. BSE, on the other hand, might see growth from its non-transactional businesses and its efforts to deepen capital market penetration in smaller cities. The perceived "discount" in NSE's FY26 P/E might implicitly assume a higher growth rate for NSE to justify its current valuation, or it might discount the inherent risks associated with its larger, more concentrated revenue streams. Investors need to evaluate if the assumed growth rates for both exchanges, leading to their respective FY26 earnings, are realistic and sustainable.

Factors Influencing Exchange Valuations

The valuation of stock exchanges is influenced by a unique set of factors that go beyond typical manufacturing or service companies. These factors need to be considered when assessing the long-term prospects of an exchange's IPO.

Trading Volumes and Market Activity

The primary revenue drivers for exchanges are trading volumes across various segments – cash equity, equity derivatives, currency derivatives, and commodity derivatives. Higher volumes mean more transaction fees. Factors like investor participation, foreign institutional investor (FII) flows, domestic institutional investor (DII) activity, and overall economic sentiment directly impact these volumes. A robust bull market typically boosts exchange revenues, while a bear market or prolonged consolidation can dampen them.

Regulatory Environment and Policy Shifts

Stock exchanges operate in a heavily regulated environment under the oversight of SEBI. Any changes in transaction charges, listing norms, clearing and settlement rules, or even the introduction of new products or market timings can significantly impact an exchange's profitability. For example, a reduction in regulatory fees or an increase in compliance costs could directly affect earnings. Furthermore, policies promoting financialisation of savings or discouraging certain types of trading can alter the revenue landscape.

Technology and Infrastructure Investment

Modern stock exchanges are technology-intensive businesses. Continuous investment in high-speed trading platforms, cybersecurity, data analytics, and disaster recovery systems is crucial. These investments are significant capital expenditures but are essential for maintaining competitiveness and operational efficiency. The ability of an exchange to innovate and adapt to technological advancements, such as blockchain for settlement or AI for market surveillance, also plays a role in its long-term value.

A real example

Let's consider Anjali, 42, an IT professional earning ₹35 lakhs CTC in Hyderabad, who is looking at the NSE IPO. She sees an analyst report stating that NSE is available at a 25x FY26 P/E, while BSE is currently trading at 35x its trailing twelve-month (TTM) P/E.

Anjali might think: "NSE is a bigger exchange, has more market share, and is available at a 25x P/E for FY26, which is much cheaper than BSE's 35x. This looks like a great deal!"

Here's how this could be misleading:

  1. BSE's TTM P/E (35x): This is based on actual past earnings, which are known and verified. Let's assume BSE's current EPS is ₹30. Its share price would be ₹1,050 (30 x 35).
  2. NSE's FY26 P/E (25x): This is based on projected earnings for a financial year almost three years away. Let's assume the analyst projects NSE's FY26 EPS to be ₹70. At the IPO price of ₹1,750 (mid-point of the band), the FY26 P/E would indeed be 25x (1750 / 70).
  3. The Hidden Assumptions: The ₹70 FY26 EPS for NSE is an estimate. What if actual market conditions, regulatory changes, or increased competition lead to NSE's actual FY26 EPS being only ₹50 instead of ₹70?

    4. In this scenario, at the IPO price of ₹1,750, the actual* FY26 P/E would turn out to be 35x (1750 / 50). * This means Anjali, who thought she was buying at a "discounted" 25x P/E, would effectively be paying 35x P/E based on actual future earnings, which is no discount at all compared to BSE's current valuation. If the market re-rates NSE based on its actual* 35x P/E, the share price might not appreciate as expected, or could even correct downwards if the initial premium was justified solely on the optimistic ₹70 EPS.

Anjali's decision, based purely on a forward P/E discount without scrutinising the underlying assumptions for those future earnings, could lead to unmet expectations and potentially lower returns.

What to do this week

  1. Review the IPO Prospectus (DRHP): Download and carefully read the Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) filed by NSE with SEBI. Pay close attention to the "Risk Factors" section and the detailed financial projections, including the assumptions used for future earnings.
  2. Analyse Current Financials: Look at NSE's historical revenue, profit, and cash flow growth over the past 3-5 years. Compare these with BSE's historical performance to understand their actual track records, not just future estimates.
  3. Understand Business Segments: Deep dive into how NSE generates its revenue – from cash market trading, derivatives, listing fees, data services, etc. Assess the growth potential and risks associated with each segment independently.
  4. Evaluate Management Commentary: Pay attention to any public statements or analyst calls from NSE's management regarding their growth outlook, strategic plans, and potential challenges. Cross-reference these with independent analyst reports.
  5. Consider Your Investment Horizon: Decide if you are looking for short-term gains or long-term wealth creation. IPOs based on distant future earnings projections often carry higher short-term volatility, making them more suitable for investors with a longer investment horizon who can stomach potential fluctuations.

FAQ

Why is an IPO valuation based on FY26 earnings potentially misleading?

Valuations based on distant future earnings, like FY26, are highly dependent on projections that can be significantly altered by unforeseen market conditions, regulatory changes, or economic shifts, making the "discount" unreliable.

How do stock exchanges typically make money?

Stock exchanges primarily earn revenue from transaction fees on trades, listing fees for companies, data dissemination services, and sometimes from clearing and settlement charges.

What are the key risks specific to investing in a stock exchange?

Key risks include volatility in trading volumes, changes in regulatory policies by SEBI, increased competition, and the need for continuous investment in technology infrastructure.

Is a lower P/E ratio always better for an IPO?

Not necessarily. A lower P/E might indicate undervaluation or lower growth expectations. It's crucial to compare it with industry peers, consider the company's growth prospects, and understand the basis of the earnings used in the calculation.

Where can I find official information about the NSE IPO?

You can find official documents like the Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) on the SEBI website or the websites of the lead merchant bankers appointed for the IPO.

Should I ignore future earnings projections entirely?

No, future earnings projections are important, but they should be viewed critically. Understand the assumptions behind them, consider a range of scenarios (optimistic to pessimistic), and don't solely rely on them for your investment decision.

How does SEBI regulate IPOs and valuations?

SEBI sets guidelines for IPO disclosures, ensuring companies provide comprehensive information to investors. While SEBI doesn't "approve" valuations, it mandates transparency in the valuation methodology and risk factors.
