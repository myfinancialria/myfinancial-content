How Fevicol Made 'Adhesive' the Most Memorable Ad Category in Indian Television

In the dusty bylanes of a nondescript Indian village, a rickety, overloaded bus careened down a bumpy road. Passengers clung to every conceivable surface – the roof, the windows, even the side panels. It swayed, it rattled, it groaned, but miraculously, it held together. The secret, the ad revealed with a knowing wink, wasn't engineering prowess or divine intervention. It was Fevicol, applied liberally, holding the entire vehicle in an impossible, unbreakable embrace. This iconic "Bus Ad," aired in 1991, didn't just sell glue; it cemented Fevicol's place in the Indian psyche, transforming a mundane product into a symbol of enduring strength and quirky humor. For over three decades, Fevicol's advertising has consistently defied convention, never showing the product itself, yet building Pidilite Industries into a ₹1.7 trillion market capitalization giant.

The World Before the Unbreakable Bond

Before Fevicol, the Indian adhesives market was a drab, undifferentiated landscape. Carpenters and woodworkers, the primary consumers, relied on "saresh" – a traditional, animal-fat-based glue. This adhesive was notoriously messy, required hours of heating over a fire to prepare, offered inconsistent bonding strength, and carried an unpleasant odour. It was a cumbersome, unhygienic, and largely unorganised market, devoid of brand recognition or consumer loyalty. The idea of advertising glue, let alone making it a memorable category, seemed utterly foolish. Adhesives were functional, a commodity purchased for utility, not for brand appeal.

When Balvant Parekh founded Pidilite Industries in 1959, he saw an opportunity to disrupt this archaic system. He introduced Fevicol, a synthetic white adhesive that was ready-to-use straight from the container, offered superior bonding strength, and was far more hygienic than its animal-based predecessors. Initially, Fevicol bypassed traditional distribution channels, opting for a direct-to-carpenter approach, distributing free samples and building relationships directly in workshops. This direct engagement helped Fevicol establish its quality and build trust with its core users, allowing it to dominate the industrial adhesive market by the 1980s. However, the real challenge lay in transcending this utilitarian perception and reaching the broader household consumer.

The Crack: When Humour Became the Glue

The pivotal shift came in the late 1980s and early 1990s when Pidilite partnered with Ogilvy & Mather, an advertising agency led by the legendary Piyush Pandey. Pandey, known for infusing Indian advertising with local insights and vernacular humour, understood that a functional product like glue needed an emotional, memorable hook. The genius was in not showing the product, but rather demonstrating its effect through exaggerated, relatable, and often hilarious scenarios.

Instead of technical specifications, Fevicol focused on one simple, powerful concept: "unbreakable bonds". Their first major television advertisements started appearing in the late 1980s, gradually moving towards humorous, real-life scenarios. While some sources cite the "Dum Laga Ke Haisha" ad featuring filmmaker Rajkumar Hirani in 1997 as their first TV commercial, the 1991 "Bus Ad" (also known as the "Egg Tray" commercial) is widely recognized as the launchpad for their iconic advertising style. It was a masterclass in visual storytelling, where an entire bus, overloaded with passengers, held together by Fevicol, conveyed the product's strength without a single spoken word about its chemical composition. This ad, conceptualized by Ogilvy & Mather, made the tagline "Fevicol ka jod hai, tootega nahi" (This is Fevicol's bond, it won't break) a permanent part of the Indian lexicon.

This strategy was revolutionary for a low-involvement product category like adhesives. Other memorable campaigns followed: the "Egg Ad" where a chef couldn't crack an egg because the hen had been eating from a Fevicol bucket, the "Moochwali" campaign, and the "Sofa Ad" in 2019 that chronicled the journey of a single sofa through generations of an Indian family, symbolizing enduring bonds. The ads were short, intriguing, humorous, and deeply rooted in Indian reality, making them instantly relatable and shareable. Fevicol didn't sell glue; it sold the emotion of togetherness, reliability, and the sheer impossibility of separation.

What Happened Next

The impact of this consistent, culturally resonant advertising strategy was monumental. Fevicol became more than just an adhesive; it became a cultural phenomenon. Its name and tagline are now used as metaphors in everyday conversations, popular culture, and even political speeches to describe strong, unbreakable alliances. For instance, Prime Minister Modi famously referenced "Fevicol ka jod" to describe the strong bond between India and Japan in 2014.

Pidilite Industries, the parent company, saw its flagship brand achieve a near-monopoly status. Fevicol today commands an estimated market share of over 70% in India's adhesive market. This dominance has propelled Pidilite Industries to impressive financial heights. As of August 2026, Pidilite Industries boasts a market capitalization of approximately ₹1.71 trillion (around USD 18.1 billion). The company's revenue for the trailing twelve months (TTM) ending 2026 stood at approximately ₹14,870 crore (USD 1.6 billion), with adhesives and sealants contributing about 53% of its total revenue. This growth wasn't just in sales figures; it was in embedding itself so deeply into the Indian consciousness that "Fevicol" became synonymous with "glue" itself.

Pidilite continued to diversify its portfolio with other successful brands like Fevikwik, M-Seal, and Dr. Fixit, many of which also adopted similarly distinctive, humorous advertising approaches. This strategic expansion and consistent brand-building cemented Pidilite's position as a leader in consumer and specialty chemicals.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Don't Sell the Product, Sell the Promise (or the Problem Solved): Fevicol rarely shows its product. Instead, it focuses entirely on the benefit – an unbreakable bond – and exaggerates it to comedic effect. For a low-involvement category, this shifted the focus from technical specifications to a memorable emotional connection.
  2. Cultural Relevance Trumps Generic Gloss: Piyush Pandey's approach was to root Fevicol's advertising in everyday Indian life, using local humour, situations, and characters. This allowed the brand to resonate deeply across diverse demographics, making it a "cultural glue" rather than just a commercial product.
  3. Consistency is King, Especially in Creative Expression: For over 30 years, Fevicol's creative model has remained largely unchanged: impossible bonds, humour, and minimal product presence. This unwavering consistency has built cumulative brand equity, making each new ad instantly recognizable and reinforcing its core message.
  4. Humour as a Differentiator: In a functional and commoditized market, injecting humor into advertising created a unique brand personality for Fevicol. It made the brand approachable, likable, and highly memorable, setting it apart from competitors who focused on dry product features.
  5. Brand as a Verb/Metaphor: When a brand name or tagline transcends its product category and becomes a part of common parlance or a metaphor for a broader concept (like "Fevicol ka jod" for an unbreakable relationship), it signifies an unparalleled level of brand penetration and cultural currency.
