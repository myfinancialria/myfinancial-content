First, what is an an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine your favourite local shop, say "Sharma Sweets", has been doing really well. They want to open more branches, buy new equipment, or expand their business. To do this, they need a lot of money. Instead of taking a big loan, they decide to invite you, the public, to become a small owner in their business.

This is what an IPO (Initial Public Offering) is. It's when a private company sells its shares for the very first time to the public. Think of it as Sharma Sweets selling tiny pieces of itself to many people. When a company does an IPO, it's entering the primary market (the market where new securities are created). Once these shares are sold, they then start trading on a stock exchange like the NSE or BSE. This process is called listing – the shares are now available for anyone to buy and sell every day in the open market.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

Understanding an IPO is like reading the menu and prices at a school fete stall before deciding what to buy. Here are the key things you'll always find:

  1. Price Band: This is the range (e.g., ₹259 to ₹272) within which you can bid for shares. You can choose any price within this band. The company usually sets a "cut-off price" at the higher end of the band for retail investors.
  2. Lot Size: This is the minimum number of shares you must apply for. You cannot apply for just one share; you have to apply in multiples of the lot size. For example, if the lot size is 50 shares, you must apply for 50, 100, 150 shares, and so on.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is calculated by multiplying the lot size by the upper end of the price band. For instance, if the lot size is 50 shares and the upper price band is ₹272, your minimum investment would be 50 x ₹272 = ₹13,600.
  4. Issue Size: This tells you the total number of shares the company is offering to the public. It can also be expressed as the total value of shares being offered (e.g., ₹500 crores). A larger issue size means more shares are available.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer For Sale (OFS):

    6. * Fresh Issue: When a company issues new shares to raise money for its business operations (like expanding, paying debt, or buying assets). The money from these shares goes directly to the company. * Offer For Sale (OFS): When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares to the public. The money from these shares goes to the selling shareholders, not to the company itself.

  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the official document filed with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) that contains all the detailed information about the company, its business, financials, risks, management, and the IPO itself. It's like the detailed instruction manual for the IPO.

The IPO process, step by step

The journey from a private company to a publicly listed one involves several steps:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI. This document has all the details about the company and its plans for the IPO. SEBI reviews this.
  2. RHP & Price Band Announcement: Once SEBI approves, the company releases the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) and announces the price band (the price range for the shares) and the lot size.
  3. Bidding Period: This is when you, the investor, can apply for shares. The IPO is open for typically three working days. Investors are usually divided into categories:

    4. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Individuals applying for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs) / High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs): Individuals or entities applying for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large institutional investors like mutual funds, foreign institutional investors, and insurance companies.

  4. Allotment: After the bidding closes, the company allocates shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than available shares), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
  5. Refunds / Shares Credited: If you don't get an allotment, your application money is refunded. If you get shares, they are credited to your demat account.
  6. Listing: Finally, the shares get listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE). This is when they start trading publicly, and you can buy or sell them through your broker.

A simple way to think about it

Think of a family that runs a very successful home bakery. They've been making delicious cakes and cookies for years, and everyone in the neighbourhood loves them. Now, they want to open a bigger, fancier shop in the main market. To do this, they need a lot of money.

So, they decide to sell small "ownership coupons" for their new shop to their trusted neighbours and customers. Each coupon represents a tiny piece of the bakery. When you buy one of these coupons, you become a small owner. This is exactly what a company does in an IPO – it sells small pieces of its ownership (shares) to the public to raise money for its growth.

Things people get confused about

Myth → Fact

  • Myth: All IPOs guarantee huge listing gains.

    • * Fact: Listing gains are never guaranteed. The share price on listing day depends on many factors, including market sentiment, the company's performance, and overall economic conditions. Sometimes shares list at a premium, sometimes at a discount, and sometimes at the issue price.

  • Myth: You must apply for many lots to get an allotment.

    • Fact: For retail investors, if an IPO is oversubscribed, allotment is typically done through a lottery system. Applying for just one lot gives you the same chance in the lottery as someone applying for the maximum retail limit. Applying for more than one lot does not increase your chances of getting any shares, only the number of shares if you do* get allotted.

  • Myth: IPOs are only for rich people.

    • * Fact: IPOs are designed to allow ordinary investors to participate. The minimum investment for retail investors is usually kept around ₹10,000 to ₹15,000, making it accessible to many.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility via net banking, or through your stockbroker's platform.

What is a lot size?

A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for in an IPO. You cannot apply for shares individually; you must apply in multiples of this lot.

What is a price band?

The price band is the fixed range (e.g., ₹259 to ₹272) within which you can place your bid for the shares. You can choose any price within this range.

What is the RHP/DRHP?

The RHP (Red Herring Prospectus) or DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) is a detailed legal document filed by the company with SEBI, containing all essential information about the company, its finances, risks, and the IPO itself.

What happens if an IPO is oversubscribed?

If an IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than available shares), shares are typically allotted to retail investors through a lottery system. If you get an allotment, shares are credited to your demat account; otherwise, your money is refunded.
