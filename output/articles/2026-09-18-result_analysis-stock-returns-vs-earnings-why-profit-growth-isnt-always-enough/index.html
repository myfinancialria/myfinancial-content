Stock Returns vs. Earnings: Why Profit Growth Isn't Always Enough

TL;DR

  • Strong profit growth in a company does not automatically guarantee good returns for its shareholders.
  • Stock returns are influenced by both earnings growth and changes in how the market values those earnings.
  • A phenomenon called "de-rating" can occur, where the market assigns a lower valuation multiple to a company.
  • De-rating can significantly offset impressive earnings growth, leading to flat or even negative stock returns.
  • Investors should focus on a company's intrinsic value and reasonable valuations, not just its reported profits.

What this means in plain terms

Many investors believe that buying shares of companies with consistently growing profits is a surefire way to build wealth. While profit growth is undoubtedly a critical factor, it's not the only piece of the puzzle. Your actual stock returns are determined by two main components: the growth in the company's earnings per share (EPS) and the change in the market's valuation multiple (like the Price-to-Earnings, or P/E, ratio) that it's willing to assign to those earnings.

Imagine you buy a share for ₹100 when its EPS is ₹5, meaning it trades at a P/E of 20x. If the EPS doubles to ₹10 over a few years, but the market now only values the company at 10x earnings, your share price would still be ₹100 (₹10 EPS * 10 P/E). Despite the company's profits doubling, your investment hasn't grown. This scenario, where the valuation multiple contracts, is known as "de-rating." It highlights why even excellent businesses might not always be excellent investments if bought at the wrong price or if market sentiment shifts against them.

The Disconnect: Earnings Growth vs. Valuation Multiples

A company's earnings growth reflects its operational performance – how well it's selling products, managing costs, and expanding its business. This is fundamental to long-term value creation. However, the stock market is not just a reflection of current earnings; it's a forward-looking mechanism that prices in future expectations, sentiment, and the broader economic environment.

The valuation multiple, such as the P/E ratio, is essentially what the market is willing to pay for each rupee of a company's earnings. This multiple can expand or contract based on several factors, often independent of the immediate earnings report:

Market Sentiment and Risk Appetite

During periods of economic optimism or abundant liquidity, investors might be willing to pay higher multiples for growth stocks. Conversely, in times of uncertainty or rising interest rates, risk appetite shrinks, and investors demand a lower multiple, preferring stability over aggressive growth.

Interest Rate Environment

Higher interest rates, as set by central banks like the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), can make future earnings less valuable today. This is because the discount rate used to value future cash flows increases, leading to a contraction in P/E multiples across the board, especially for companies whose earnings are heavily skewed towards the distant future.

Sectoral Dynamics and Competitive Landscape

Even if a company's individual earnings are strong, if its sector faces headwinds (e.g., regulatory changes, new technology disruption, increased competition), the market might collectively decide that the future growth potential or quality of earnings for that entire sector is diminished. This can lead to a uniform de-rating of companies within that sector.

Corporate Governance and Management Quality

Perceived issues with corporate governance, transparency, or management's capital allocation decisions can cause investors to lose faith, leading to a significant de-rating, even if the company continues to post strong numbers. The market often discounts companies where there's a perceived lack of integrity or poor stewardship of shareholder capital.

Understanding De-rating: When the Market Changes its Mind

De-rating occurs when the market re-evaluates a company's future prospects, risk profile, or the sustainability of its growth, leading to a lower valuation multiple. It's a powerful force that can negate impressive earnings performance. Here are key reasons why de-rating happens:

Shifting Growth Expectations

A company might have grown rapidly for years, commanding a high P/E multiple. If the market perceives that its growth trajectory is slowing down, or that its addressable market is saturating, it will likely assign a lower multiple. Even if the company continues to grow, but at a slower pace than previously expected, a de-rating can occur.

Cyclical Peaks and Trough

Many industries are cyclical. Companies in these sectors often trade at low P/E multiples at the peak of their earnings cycle (when profits are highest) because the market anticipates a downturn. Conversely, they might trade at high P/E multiples at the trough (when profits are lowest) because the market expects a recovery. Buying a cyclical stock at its earnings peak, despite strong current profits, can lead to de-rating.

Commodity Price Volatility

For companies heavily reliant on commodity inputs or outputs, significant volatility in commodity prices can impact their earnings visibility and quality. If the market expects sustained high input costs or lower selling prices, it might de-rate the company, even if current earnings are strong due to a temporary favourable spread.

Regulatory Changes or Government Policy Shifts

New regulations or changes in government policy can have a profound impact on an industry's profitability and competitive landscape. For instance, stricter environmental norms or changes in taxation (as notified by the Income Tax Department) can increase operational costs or reduce demand, leading to a de-rating of affected companies.

Warning Signs Investors Often Miss

While predicting de-rating perfectly is impossible, certain indicators can serve as warning signs, prompting investors to scrutinise their holdings more closely:

Exorbitant Valuations

If a stock's P/E ratio is significantly higher than its historical average, its industry peers, or the broader market, it suggests that a lot of future growth is already priced in. Any disappointment in earnings or growth can lead to a sharp correction in the multiple.

Unsustainable Growth Drivers

Beware of growth fuelled by one-off events, unsustainable debt, or aggressive accounting practices. Growth needs to be backed by strong underlying business fundamentals, expanding market share, innovative products, or structural industry tailwinds. If growth drivers appear temporary or artificial, the market will eventually catch on.

Increasing Competitive Intensity

A company operating in a highly competitive or commoditised industry might struggle to maintain pricing power and profit margins over the long term. New entrants, technological advancements by rivals, or price wars can erode profitability and lead to a de-rating.

Promoter Activity and Share Pledging

Significant selling of shares by promoters or a high percentage of promoter shares being pledged as collateral for loans can be a red flag. While not always indicative of trouble, it warrants deeper investigation as it could signal a lack of confidence from those closest to the business. Information on shareholding patterns is publicly available through stock exchanges and SEBI filings.

Deteriorating Return Ratios

Keep an eye on key return ratios like Return on Equity (ROE) and Return on Capital Employed (ROCE). A consistent decline in these ratios, even if absolute profits are growing, can indicate that the company is becoming less efficient at generating profits from its capital, which can trigger a de-rating.

A real example

Let's consider Rahul, 38, ₹28L CTC, Bengaluru, an investor who bought shares of "TechInnovate Ltd." based on its impressive profit growth.

  • Initial Investment (Year 0):

    • * TechInnovate's Earnings Per Share (EPS): ₹10 * Market Price Per Share (MPS): ₹400 * P/E Ratio: 40x (₹400 / ₹10) * Rahul buys 100 shares for ₹40,000. The high P/E was justified by analysts predicting 30% annual EPS growth for the next five years, driven by new product launches.

  • After 3 Years (Year 3):

    • * TechInnovate delivers strong EPS growth, albeit slightly lower than the initial aggressive forecast. EPS grows by an average of 25% annually. New EPS: ₹10 (1.25)^3 = ₹10 * 1.953125 = ₹19.53 (approximately ₹19.50) * However, during these three years, the market's perception of the tech sector changed. Interest rates rose, and new competitors emerged, leading to a contraction in valuation multiples for many tech companies. * The market now assigns a P/E ratio of 20x to TechInnovate, reflecting the revised growth outlook and increased competition. New Market Price Per Share (MPS): ₹19.50 (EPS) 20 (P/E) = ₹390

  • Rahul's Return:

    • * Initial Investment: ₹40,000 Value after 3 years: 100 shares ₹390/share = ₹39,000 * Total Return: (₹39,000 - ₹40,000) / ₹40,000 = -₹1,000 / ₹40,000 = -2.5%

Despite TechInnovate's EPS growing by nearly 95% over three years, Rahul's investment actually lost 2.5% of its value. This happened because the significant de-rating (P/E contracting from 40x to 20x) completely offset the strong operational performance. This example clearly illustrates why strong profit growth alone may not make you money if the starting valuation is too high and the market's sentiment shifts.

What to do this week

  1. Review P/E Ratios: Check the current P/E ratios of the stocks in your portfolio. Compare them to their historical averages and the P/E ratios of their industry peers.
  2. Understand Valuation Drivers: For any stock trading at a significantly high P/E, research the specific reasons the market is assigning such a premium. Is it sustainable?
  3. Read Management Commentary: Go through the latest quarterly results and annual reports. Pay close attention to management's outlook on future growth, competitive landscape, and any potential headwinds.
  4. Assess Industry Trends: Evaluate the broader industry trends affecting your holdings. Are there any disruptive technologies, regulatory changes, or shifts in consumer behaviour that could impact future profitability?
  5. Diversify Your Portfolio: Ensure your portfolio is adequately diversified across different sectors and market capitalisations to mitigate the risk of a significant de-rating in any single stock or sector.

FAQ

What is de-rating in stock markets?

De-rating occurs when the market assigns a lower valuation multiple (like P/E ratio) to a company's earnings, even if its profits are growing. This often happens due to changing market sentiment, perceived slowdown in future growth, or increased risks.

How do interest rates affect stock valuations?

Higher interest rates generally make future earnings less valuable today by increasing the discount rate used in valuation models. This can lead to a contraction of P/E multiples across the market, especially for growth stocks.

Is a high P/E ratio always bad?

Not necessarily. A high P/E can be justified for companies with very strong, sustainable growth prospects, innovative products, or a significant competitive advantage. However, it also implies higher expectations, and any disappointment can lead to a sharp de-rating.

Can a company with negative earnings still be a good investment?

Yes, especially for early-stage or high-growth companies that are investing heavily for future expansion. However, such investments carry higher risk, and their valuation relies more on future potential than current profitability.

What is the difference between earnings growth and revenue growth?

Revenue growth refers to the increase in a company's sales. Earnings growth refers to the increase in its net profit. While both are important, earnings growth is crucial as it directly impacts shareholder value after all expenses and taxes.

How important is management commentary?

Management commentary provides crucial insights into a company's strategy, operational challenges, future outlook, and capital allocation plans. It helps investors understand the qualitative aspects behind the financial numbers and assess future risks and opportunities.
