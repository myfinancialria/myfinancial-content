First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local chai shop that has been doing very well. Now, the owner wants to open more branches across the city, but needs a lot of money to do so. One way to get this money is to sell a small part of the shop's ownership to the public.

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is exactly this – it's when a private company sells its shares for the very first time to the public. This is done in the primary market (where shares are sold directly by the company). Once these shares are sold, they get listed on a stock exchange like NSE or BSE, meaning they can then be bought and sold by anyone in the regular stock market (the secondary market).

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you see an IPO announcement, here are the key pieces of information to understand:

  1. Price Band: This is the range of prices at which the company is willing to sell its shares. For example, if the price band is ₹100-₹105, you can bid for shares at any price within this range. The highest price in the band is often called the "cap price".
  2. Lot Size: Shares in an IPO are not sold individually, but in fixed groups called "lots". If the lot size is 50 shares, you must apply for at least 50 shares, or multiples of 50 (e.g., 100, 150, etc.). This ensures that the company deals with a manageable number of applications.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is the smallest amount of money you need to invest to apply for an IPO. You calculate it by multiplying the lot size by the lowest price in the price band. For instance, if the lot size is 50 shares and the price band is ₹100-₹105, your minimum investment would be 50 shares * ₹100 = ₹5,000. (Note: For the IPOs above, the lot size is not yet announced, so the minimum investment cannot be calculated at this time.)
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public. It can also be expressed as the total value of shares being offered (e.g., ₹500 crores). A larger issue size means more shares are available.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer For Sale (OFS):

    6. * Fresh Issue: When a company issues new shares to raise money for its business operations, expansion, or to pay off debts. The money from these shares goes directly to the company. * Offer For Sale (OFS): When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares to the public. The money from these shares goes to the selling shareholders, not the company itself. Note: The specific breakdown between Fresh Issue and OFS is not provided in the data for the current IPOs, but it's an important detail often found in the prospectus.*

  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the detailed legal document that a company files with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) before an IPO. It contains all the crucial information about the company, its financials, risks, management, and how the money raised will be used. It's like the company's report card and business plan rolled into one.

The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO involves a few key stages:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files its Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI for approval. This document outlines all the details of the proposed IPO.
  2. SEBI Approval & RHP: Once SEBI approves, the company files the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) and announces the price band and issue dates.
  3. Bidding: During the IPO open dates, investors can place bids for shares. There are different categories of investors:

    4. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Ordinary individuals who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs) / Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs): Individuals or entities who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.

  4. Allotment: After the bidding closes, shares are allotted to successful bidders. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than shares available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
  5. Listing: Finally, the shares get listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE). This is when they start trading in the secondary market, and you can buy or sell them like any other stock.

A simple way to think about it

Think of a school fete where a popular stall is selling delicious homemade sweets. The stall owner decides to sell "ownership coupons" for a small part of their future sweet-making business to raise money for a bigger kitchen.

  • The "ownership coupons" are the shares.
  • Selling them for the first time is the IPO.
  • The price range for the coupons is the price band.
  • The minimum number of coupons you must buy is the lot size.
  • The detailed recipe book and business plan for the sweet stall is the RHP.
  • Once you buy the coupons, you can later sell them to other people at the fete – that's like the shares getting listed on the stock exchange.

Things people get confused about

  • Myth: Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares.

    • * Fact: If an IPO is very popular (oversubscribed), there are more applications than shares available. In such cases, shares are allotted through a lottery system, especially for retail investors. You might not get any shares, or you might get fewer than you applied for.

  • Myth: IPOs are a guaranteed way to make quick money.

    • * Fact: While some IPOs see a rise in price on listing day (listing gain), this is not guaranteed. Share prices can go down on listing day or in the future, depending on market conditions and the company's performance. All investments carry risk.

  • Myth: You must apply for shares at the highest price in the band.

    • * Fact: You can bid at any price within the price band. Many investors choose to bid at the "cut-off price," which means they are willing to pay the final price discovered within the band. If you bid below the final cut-off price, your application might not be considered for allotment.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility via net banking, or through your stockbroker's platform. Your money remains in your bank account until shares are allotted.

What is a lot size?

A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot apply for a single share; you must apply in multiples of the specified lot.

What does it mean if an IPO is "oversubscribed"?

If an IPO is oversubscribed, it means the company received applications for more shares than it offered. For example, if an IPO is subscribed 10 times, it means applications were received for 10 times the number of shares available.

Why do companies do IPOs?

Companies typically do IPOs to raise capital (money) for various reasons, such as funding expansion plans, paying off debts, investing in new projects, or allowing existing shareholders to sell their stake.

What is the difference between a Mainboard IPO and an SME IPO?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies and are listed on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME IPOs are for smaller companies and are listed on separate platforms like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, often with different regulatory requirements and minimum investment amounts.
