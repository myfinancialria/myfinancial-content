Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The first day these shares are traded on the stock exchange is called the listing day. Many people focus on the "listing gain" – which is the difference between the IPO price (the price you paid for the shares) and the price the shares close at on their first day of trading. A big listing gain can be exciting!

However, the listing day can be a very emotional time. There's often a lot of buzz and excitement, which can push prices up or down quite dramatically. This initial frenzy might not always reflect the true, long-term value that the market places on the company.

This is why looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after its listing can be more insightful. By this time, the initial excitement has usually settled down. Also, for many IPOs, there's a "lock-in period" for certain investors, like anchor investors (large institutional investors who commit to buying a significant portion of the IPO shares before it opens to the public). These investors cannot sell their shares for a certain period; 50% of the shares allotted to anchor investors are locked in for 30 days, and the remaining 50% are locked in for 90 days from the date of allotment. Once this lock-in period ends, their ability to sell can also influence the share price.

So, checking the price after a month or two gives us a calmer, more stable picture of how the market is valuing the company's shares, beyond the initial listing day buzz.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

The price movement of an IPO on its listing day is a result of basic economics: demand and supply.

  • Demand vs. Supply: If many investors want to buy shares of a company (high demand) and there aren't enough shares available (limited supply), the price tends to go up. This leads to a "pop" or a listing gain. If there are more shares available than investors willing to buy, the price might drop.
  • Subscription Levels: This refers to how many times an IPO was "oversubscribed." For example, if an IPO received bids for 10 times the number of shares it offered, it means demand was very high. High subscription levels often lead to a listing pop.
  • Market Mood: The overall sentiment in the stock market plays a big role. If the broader market is doing well, new IPOs often get a warmer reception. If the market is weak, even good IPOs might struggle.
  • Pricing: The company and its investment bankers decide the IPO price. If the price is set too low (making it seem like a bargain), demand can surge, leading to a pop. If it's priced too high, investors might not be interested, leading to a drop.
  • Anchor Investors and Lock-in: As mentioned, anchor investors are big investors who buy shares before the main IPO. Their participation often signals confidence. However, their shares are usually "locked in" for a period (e.g., 30 days), meaning they cannot sell them. Once this lock-in period ends, if these investors decide to sell, it can increase the supply of shares in the market and potentially affect the price.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

Think of the listing day price as the opening act of a long play. It's exciting, but it's just the beginning.

  • Volatility: The stock market can be very "volatile" – meaning prices can change quickly and unpredictably. The first day often sees high trading volumes and rapid price swings as initial buyers and sellers react.
  • Broader Market Influence: A company's share price doesn't exist in a vacuum. It's affected by the performance of the entire stock market, the sector it belongs to, and even global economic news. These factors can cause a stock to list flat but then move significantly later, or vice-versa.
  • Long-Term Fundamentals: A company's true value often depends on its business performance, growth prospects, management quality, and financial health. These factors unfold over months and years, not just on the listing day. A stock might have a modest listing gain but perform very well over time if the company's fundamentals are strong. Conversely, a large listing gain might not last if the company's performance doesn't meet expectations.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a popular new sweet shop opening in your neighbourhood. On the first day, everyone rushes to buy, and the sweets might sell out quickly at a slightly higher price due to the initial excitement. But after a month or two, the rush settles down. The shop's prices then reflect what people are regularly willing to pay, based on the quality of the sweets, the service, and how many other sweet shops are around. The IPO listing day is like the grand opening, full of initial buzz, while the price 30-60 days later is closer to the regular, settled market price.

Things people get confused about

Myth → Fact

  • Myth: A big listing gain means it's a great company.

    • Fact: A big listing gain shows strong initial demand from investors, but it doesn't guarantee the company's long-term success or that its share price will continue to rise.

  • Myth: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad company and should be avoided forever.

    • Fact: Listing below the issue price can happen due to various reasons like market sentiment, pricing strategy, or lower-than-expected demand. It doesn't automatically mean the company is "bad." Its future performance will depend on its business and market conditions.

  • Myth: Applying for an IPO is a guaranteed way to make quick money.

    • Fact: While some IPOs do offer good listing gains, others might list flat or even below their issue price. There is no guarantee of profit, and like any market investment, IPOs carry risks.

FAQ

What is an IPO?

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary people to buy a part of the company.

What is a Mainboard IPO?

A Mainboard IPO is for larger, more established companies that list their shares on the main exchanges like NSE and BSE, catering to a wide range of investors.

What is an SME IPO?

An SME IPO is for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) that list on dedicated SME platforms of exchanges like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, typically involving smaller issue sizes and often higher investment lots.

What does "issue price" mean?

The "issue price" is the fixed price per share at which the company offers its shares to the public during the IPO.

What is "current price"?

The "current price" is the latest trading price of the company's shares on the stock exchange, reflecting what buyers and sellers are agreeing upon at that moment.
