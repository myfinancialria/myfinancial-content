BYJU'S: How India's Most Valuable Startup Wiped Out $22 Billion in 18 Months

The year is 2022. Byju Raveendran, the former math tutor turned edtech mogul, stands at the helm of an empire. His company, BYJU'S, India's most valuable startup, has just secured an $800 million funding round, catapulting its valuation to a staggering $22 billion. Raveendran, whose personal net worth once touched $3.6 billion, was a poster child for India's booming startup ecosystem, gracing magazine covers and appearing on global stages. The company’s app boasted over 150 million registered students, a testament to its seemingly unstoppable ascent. It was a moment of triumph, a peak from which the only conceivable direction seemed to be further skyward. Yet, beneath the gleaming facade of aggressive growth and celebrity endorsements, cracks were already forming, hinting at a spectacular collapse that would redefine the perils of unchecked ambition.

The World Before

Before BYJU'S transformed the landscape, India’s education sector was largely traditional, dominated by physical tuition centers and conventional schooling. Online learning existed, but it was often considered a supplement, not a primary mode of instruction. The idea of a digital platform commanding billions in valuation, let alone fundamentally altering how millions of children learned, seemed improbable to many.

Then came BYJU'S, founded in 2011 by Byju Raveendran and Divya Gokulnath. It began as a pioneering online tutoring platform, initially offering courses for competitive exams before expanding into K-12 education. The company tapped into a vast, underserved market, promising personalized learning and leveraging engaging video content. Its early success was driven by a genuine need for accessible, quality education, particularly in a country with a massive student population. As internet penetration grew, BYJU'S positioned itself at the forefront of the digital learning revolution.

The COVID-19 pandemic, while a global tragedy, proved to be an unexpected accelerant for BYJU'S. With schools shut down and parents desperate for learning solutions, demand for online education exploded. BYJU'S heavy marketing investment, including sponsorship of India's national cricket team and the FIFA World Cup, positioned it as a category leader when the market surged. Revenue grew, user acquisition accelerated, and investors poured billions into the company, convinced they were backing a dominant player in a massive, fundamentally shifted market. What seemed impossible just a few years prior—a multi-billion dollar edtech giant—was now a reality, fueled by a narrative of inevitable, sustained growth.

The Crack

The foundation of BYJU'S meteoric rise, however, began to show fissures almost as soon as it reached its zenith. The company's strategy was characterized by aggressive sales tactics, a relentless acquisition spree, and, eventually, opaque financial reporting.

BYJU'S embarked on a massive acquisition spree, spending heavily to expand its offerings and eliminate competitors. Key acquisitions included Aakash Educational Services for nearly $1 billion in 2021, and WhiteHat Jr for $300 million in 2020. While these moves aimed to diversify and solidify its market position, many acquisitions, like WhiteHat Jr, proved to be overpriced and poorly integrated, draining resources and creating operational chaos. WhiteHat Jr, an online coding school for children, quickly became embroiled in controversy over its aggressive advertising claims and accusations of misleading parents, leading to regulatory scrutiny and numerous complaints.

The pursuit of growth-at-all-costs manifested in BYJU'S sales operations. Reports emerged of high-pressure sales tactics, including misleading claims about educational outcomes and exploiting customer vulnerabilities. Sales teams were allegedly pushed to aggressively market expensive educational packages, sometimes encouraging financing arrangements without proper disclosure, leading to parents taking on debt they didn't fully understand. This generated short-term conversions but severely damaged the company's long-term brand value and trust among its customer base.

The financial health of BYJU'S became increasingly murky. The company repeatedly delayed submitting its audited financial statements, a significant red flag for investors and regulators. When the results for the fiscal year ending March 2021 finally came out in late 2022, they revealed a staggering net loss of ₹4,588 crore (approximately $550 million USD at current exchange rates), while revenue actually fell. This contradicted the narrative of booming pandemic-driven growth and exposed a skyrocketing burn rate, where expenditures on marketing and acquisitions far outpaced revenue growth.

Further troubles mounted with a $1.2 billion Term Loan B (TLB) raised in the United States in 2021 for global expansion. Lenders accused BYJU'S of violating debt covenants, delaying financial disclosures, and allegedly transferring hundreds of millions of dollars without adequate disclosure. Specifically, nearly $533 million in loan proceeds were reportedly moved through obscure investment structures and offshore entities, beyond the reach of creditors. This led to protracted legal battles in US courts, with a Delaware court eventually upholding a ruling that BYJU'S was in default of its $1.2 billion loan, allowing lenders to take control of BYJU'S Alpha Inc., an American subsidiary pledged as collateral.

The lack of financial transparency and mounting legal woes triggered a crisis of confidence. In June 2023, Deloitte Haskins & Sells, BYJU'S auditor, resigned, citing significant delays in submitting financial statements for the year ended March 31, 2022. This was followed by the resignation of three key board members representing investors Peak XV Partners (formerly Sequoia Capital India), Prosus, and Chan Zuckerberg Initiative. Prosus explicitly stated that its representative had cited "poor reporting" and "governance structures" as reasons for his exit, claiming that BYJU'S executive leadership "regularly disregarded advice and recommendations."

What Happened Next

The consequences of BYJU'S growth-at-all-costs strategy were swift and brutal. From its peak valuation of $22 billion in March 2022, the company's value began a precipitous decline. By January 2024, BlackRock, a major investor, had slashed its valuation of BYJU'S shares, implying a company valuation of approximately $1 billion—a staggering 95% drop. Other investors like Prosus also significantly marked down their holdings, with one report in November 2023 valuing the company at less than $3 billion.

Founder Byju Raveendran's personal net worth, once $2.1 billion in 2022, plummeted to zero by April 2024, according to Forbes' Billionaire Index. BYJU'S itself entered insolvency proceedings in India by mid-2024, triggered by unpaid dues, including a Rs 158.9 crore payment to the Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI) related to a sponsorship deal. The company also faced widespread layoffs, with reports indicating approximately 500 employees, mainly from sales and marketing, were let go in 2024. Unpaid salaries became a recurring issue, forcing employees to seek other, often lower-paying, jobs.

Legal battles continued to plague the company. In November 2025, a US court issued a default judgment holding Byju Raveendran personally liable for over $1 billion in damages in connection with the alleged disappearance of the $533 million in TLB funds. The ongoing saga also saw BYJU'S lose effective control over its profitable subsidiary Aakash Educational Services, with the Supreme Court declining to halt a rights issue that would dilute BYJU'S holding to under 5% from its original 25.75%. In a candid admission in October 2024, Byju Raveendran himself acknowledged that the company was now "worth zero."

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Growth Must Be Sustainable, Not Just Aggressive: BYJU'S relentless pursuit of user acquisition and revenue growth, fueled by aggressive sales tactics and heavy marketing spend (at one point, 69% of revenue), proved unsustainable. True growth needs to be coupled with positive unit economics (the revenue and costs associated with a single customer) and a clear path to profitability, not just top-line expansion at any cost.
  2. Corporate Governance is Non-Negotiable: The prolonged delay in filing financial statements, the auditor's resignation citing lack of transparency, and the exodus of investor-appointed board members underscore the critical importance of robust corporate governance. Opaque accounting practices and a lack of independent oversight can quickly erode investor trust and lead to regulatory scrutiny, making it impossible to navigate challenging times.
  3. Acquisitions Require Strategic Integration and Due Diligence: While M&A can be a powerful growth lever, BYJU'S experience with WhiteHat Jr and the ongoing disputes around Aakash demonstrate the perils of poorly executed acquisitions. Overpaying, failing to integrate new companies effectively, and acquiring loss-making entities without a clear turnaround strategy can quickly become a significant financial drain.
  4. Distinguish Temporary Booms from Durable Shifts: The pandemic-induced surge in online learning was a unique, temporary market condition. Companies, and their investors, must critically assess whether market tailwinds represent a fundamental, long-term shift or a fleeting opportunity. Failure to adapt spending and strategy when market conditions change, as BYJU'S did, can turn a manageable adjustment into an existential crisis.
  5. Debt Can Be a Double-Edged Sword: Leveraging significant debt, like the $1.2 billion Term Loan B, can fuel rapid expansion during favorable conditions. However, without strong cash flows, transparent financials, and sound corporate governance, it quickly becomes a crippling burden when market sentiment shifts or legal disputes arise. The legal battles over the TLB and the alleged fund transfers highlight the risks of debt in the absence of accountability.
