Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public through an IPO (Initial Public Offering), there's often a lot of excitement. On the "listing day" (the very first day its shares are traded on the stock exchange), prices can jump significantly, leading to what we call a "listing gain" (the percentage difference between the IPO price and the closing price on the listing day).

However, this initial excitement can sometimes be short-lived. Many factors, like the buzz around the IPO, temporary high demand, or even specific rules for large investors (like "anchor investor lock-in," where big institutional investors cannot sell their shares for a certain period), can influence the first-day price.

By looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after its listing, we get a calmer, more settled picture. The initial frenzy has usually faded, and the market has had some time to digest the company's shares. This period often gives a more realistic view of how the market values the company after the initial rush.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's listing day performance is a bit like a new shop opening. If many people want to buy (high "demand") and there aren't many shares available (low "supply"), the price tends to go up, causing a "pop" or listing gain. This demand can be seen in the "subscription levels" (how many times the IPO shares were applied for by investors). A highly subscribed IPO often suggests strong demand.

On the other hand, if fewer people are interested or too many shares are available, the price might drop, leading to a listing loss. This can happen if the "pricing" (the IPO price set by the company) is considered too high by the market, or if the overall "market mood" (the general sentiment in the stock market) is negative. Sometimes, large investors called "anchor investors" buy a significant portion of shares before the public IPO, and their "lock-in" period (when they cannot sell their shares) can also affect the supply and demand dynamics in the initial days.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot. The stock market is dynamic, and prices can show a lot of "volatility" (quick ups and downs) in the initial days and weeks. The "broader market" (how the overall stock market, like the Nifty or Sensex, is performing) also plays a role. A company might list with a big gain, but if the overall market falls, its share price might also come down. Conversely, a stock might list flat or even with a loss, but if the company performs well over time, its share price could rise significantly later. The first day's excitement doesn't always reflect the company's long-term value or performance.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a school fete stall selling unique handmade items. The "IPO price" is like the initial price tag the students put on their items. On the first day of the fete ("listing day"), everyone rushes to buy the most popular items. If an item is very popular, people might even pay more than the tag price, causing its "price" to "pop." This is like a listing gain.

However, after the initial rush, the crowd thins out. Over the next few weeks, people might come back and look at the items more carefully. The "current price" of the item then settles based on how much people really value it, considering its quality, usefulness, and how many are still available. This settled price might be higher or lower than the initial rush price, and even different from the original tag price. This is why looking at the price 30 to 60 days later gives a more realistic picture than just the first day's excitement.

Things people get confused about

Myth → Fact

  • Myth: A big listing gain means it's a great company and a guaranteed profit.

    • * Fact: Listing gains are often short-term market reactions driven by demand and supply on the first day. They are not a guarantee of the company's long-term performance or a sure shot for future profits.

  • Myth: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's always a bad investment and you should avoid it.

    • * Fact: A lower listing can be due to various reasons, including broader market conditions or the IPO being priced too high. The company's business might still be strong, and its share price could recover and perform well over time.

  • Myth: You must apply for every IPO to make money in the stock market.

    • * Fact: Not all IPOs perform well, and some can even lead to losses. It's important to understand the company's business, its financials, and the risks involved before deciding to apply for any IPO.

FAQ

What is an IPO?

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money from investors, allowing its shares to be traded on a stock exchange.

What is a Mainboard IPO?

These are IPOs listed on the main stock exchanges like the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE), typically for larger, more established companies.

What is an SME IPO?

SME IPOs are for Small and Medium Enterprises, which list on dedicated SME platforms of NSE and BSE. They usually have different rules, smaller issue sizes, and are aimed at smaller companies.

What does "issue price" mean?

This is the fixed price per share at which the company offers its shares to the public during the IPO, before they start trading on the stock exchange.

What is "listing gain"?

It's the percentage difference between the IPO issue price and the share's closing price on its first day of trading (listing day), indicating how much the share price increased or decreased on debut.
