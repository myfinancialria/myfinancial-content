In the early 1990s, on dusty, winding roads across rural India, a peculiar sight unfolded on television screens. A rickety, overloaded bus, packed to the brim with passengers clinging to every available surface – the roof, the windows, even the bumpers – lumbered along, defying the laws of physics. Not a single soul fell off. As the bus rattled past, a small, hand-painted advertisement on its rear became visible, revealing the impossible truth: "Fevicol". This wasn't just an ad; it was a cultural moment, a stroke of advertising genius that didn't show a product being used, didn't highlight its features, but implicitly promised an unbreakable bond through pure, unadulterated humour and hyperbole. This particular commercial, often cited as the "Overloaded Bus" ad from 1991, marked a turning point, embedding Fevicol not just into the national consciousness but into its very lexicon.

The World Before

Before Fevicol cemented its place in Indian households, the adhesive market was, to put it mildly, a utilitarian wasteland. The year was 1959. Carpenters and craftsmen, the primary users of adhesives, relied heavily on traditional glues known as 'saresh'. These were animal-based products, often malodorous, requiring time-consuming preparation like heating multiple times to achieve the desired consistency. The quality was inconsistent, the bonding weak, and the entire process messy and inefficient. It was a low-involvement category, a mere commodity that rarely warranted a second thought, let alone creative advertising. Marketing efforts, if any, were confined to dry, technical specifications, appealing only to the functional needs of a niche, professional audience. There was no brand loyalty, only a transactional need for something that would (hopefully) hold things together. The idea of making glue "interesting" or "emotional" would have been considered absurd, a foolish expenditure of resources on a product category deemed inherently boring and unglamorous.

The Crack

Balvant Parekh, the visionary founder of Pidilite Industries, identified this gaping hole in the market. He introduced Fevicol, a synthetic resin adhesive that was ready-to-use, durable, and offered consistent, superior bonding strength without the fuss of traditional glues. Initially, Fevicol focused on the business-to-business (B2B) segment, targeting carpenters and furniture makers directly, building trust through product quality and direct engagement. By the 1970s, Fevicol had largely dominated the industrial adhesive market.

But the real revolution began when Pidilite decided to shift its focus towards direct consumer marketing in the 1970s. The goal was to ensure that even everyday people would ask for Fevicol by name when commissioning work from carpenters. This ambition required a new approach, one that could elevate a mundane product into a household name. In the 1980s, Pidilite forged a partnership with advertising agency Ogilvy & Mather (now Ogilvy) India, a collaboration that has endured for over three decades and is considered one of Indian advertising's longest-standing client-agency relationships.

The creative genius behind Fevicol's transformation was largely spearheaded by Piyush Pandey, who joined Ogilvy Mumbai in the late 1980s. Pandey realized that conventional advertising, focusing on technical properties, would be uninspiring for an adhesive. Instead, he chose to sell the glue based on emotion, humour, and deeply relatable storytelling, communicating the core benefit of "unbreakable bond" through visual hyperbole. The product itself was rarely shown, if ever.

The "Overloaded Bus" ad from 1991, conceptualized by Ogilvy & Mather, perfectly encapsulated this strategy. It visually depicted an impossible situation, yet one that resonated with the common Indian experience of crowded public transport, to convey the adhesive's strength. The tagline, "Yeh Fevicol ka jod hai, tootega nahi" (This is Fevicol's bond, it won't break), became iconic, a promise delivered with a wink.

This wasn't a one-off. Fevicol consistently built on this creative model. Another memorable campaign featured a hen laying unbreakable eggs because it had been eating grain from a Fevicol tin. The "Sofa" ad, released in 2001 and later repurposed for Fevicol's 60th anniversary in 2019, showcased a single wooden sofa enduring through multiple generations of a family, witnessing weddings, births, and the changing landscape of Indian society, all thanks to Fevicol's lasting strength. Even a 2009 ad titled "Moustache" added to the humour-driven portfolio, reinforcing the brand's unique identity.

The genius lay in its cultural grounding. Fevicol's ads embraced earthy, everyday Indian scenarios, abandoning the polished, aspirational advertising common at the time. This approach not only made the brand relatable but also transformed "Fevicol ka jod" into a common idiom, used by politicians and in Bollywood songs to describe unbreakable alliances and strong attachments. The consistency of this creative model, emphasizing humour, cultural relevance, and an indirect yet powerful demonstration of product strength, has remained unchanged for over 30 years.

What Happened Next

Fevicol's unconventional, humour-led advertising strategy propelled Pidilite Industries to unprecedented heights, turning a seemingly unglamorous product into a market leader and a cultural phenomenon. Today, Fevicol commands a staggering approximately 70% market share in the branded glue industry in India, effectively owning its category.

The financial impact on Pidilite Industries has been immense. For the financial year 2024, Pidilite Industries reported consolidated revenues of ₹12,522.6 crore, a significant increase from ₹11,848.7 crore in FY23. The company's market capitalization stands at an impressive ₹1,60,827 crore as of November 5, 2026. Fevicol, as a flagship brand within Pidilite's consumer and bazaar products segment, contributes significantly, with this segment accounting for approximately 80% of the company's total revenues.

Beyond the numbers, Fevicol achieved something far more profound: it became ingrained in the cultural fabric of India. The phrase "Fevicol ka jod" is widely understood and used as a metaphor for an inseparable bond. The brand's success also allowed Pidilite to diversify its portfolio, expanding into a range of consumer products and construction chemicals, further solidifying its market presence. Fevicol's rise demonstrated that even in a low-involvement product category, a powerful, consistent, and culturally resonant advertising strategy could build not just a brand, but a legacy.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Don't Sell the Product, Sell the Benefit (with a Twist): Fevicol rarely showed its white glue. Instead, it focused entirely on the outcome – the unbreakable bond – by depicting impossible, humorous scenarios. This taught that for functional products, the emotional resonance of the benefit, creatively exaggerated, can be far more powerful than a direct product demonstration. It transformed a "boring" product into an exciting idea.
  1. Consistency in Creative Strategy Pays Dividends: For over 30 years, Fevicol's advertising model has remained remarkably consistent: humour, hyperbole, cultural relevance, and an indirect portrayal of strength. This unwavering approach built cumulative brand equity, making Fevicol's style instantly recognizable and reinforcing its core message without needing frequent overhauls.
  1. Cultural Grounding is a Superpower: Fevicol's ads were deeply rooted in everyday Indian life and humour, from overloaded buses to family sofas. This made the brand incredibly relatable and allowed its tagline and name to transcend product marketing, becoming part of common language and cultural references. Authenticity in cultural context fosters deeper connection than generic aspirational messaging.
  1. Humour Breaks Through the Clutter: In a commoditized market, Fevicol used humour as its secret weapon. It made a low-involvement product category memorable and engaging, differentiating itself sharply from competitors. A well-executed, consistent sense of humour can make a brand lovable and unforgettable.
