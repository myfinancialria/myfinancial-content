Navigating Global Investments: RBI Limits, UCITS ETFs, and Your Options

TL;DR

  • The Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) permits resident Indians to invest up to USD 250,000 abroad annually.
  • Many Indian mutual funds previously offered easy access to global equities, but regulatory changes have paused fresh investments into several such schemes.
  • This shift encourages investors to explore direct global investment avenues for portfolio diversification beyond the Indian market.
  • UCITS (Undertakings for the Collective Investment in Transferable Securities) Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs) offer a regulated and cost-effective way to access diverse international markets.
  • Understanding the tax implications, including Tax Collected at Source (TCS) and capital gains, is crucial when investing globally.

What this means in plain terms

For many years, Indian investors keen on diversifying their portfolios globally found a convenient route through Indian mutual funds, specifically those investing in international markets or "Fund of Funds" (FoFs) that invested in underlying foreign funds. This allowed them to gain exposure to global giants like Apple, Amazon, or the broader US or European markets without directly dealing with foreign regulations or currency conversions.

However, the landscape has recently changed. Due to concerns about breaching overall overseas investment limits set by the RBI and SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India), many of these popular global FoFs have temporarily halted fresh investments. This means that while existing Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs) might continue, new lump sum investments or fresh SIPs are often not being accepted. This development has prompted a closer look at alternative methods for global diversification, primarily through the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), which allows individuals to directly send money abroad for various purposes, including investments, and exploring options like UCITS ETFs.

The Shifting Landscape of Global Investing for Indians

Initial Access via Indian Mutual Funds

Historically, Indian investors sought global diversification to mitigate country-specific risks and tap into growth opportunities unavailable domestically. Indian Asset Management Companies (AMCs) responded by launching Fund of Funds (FoFs) that invested in international feeder funds or directly in global equities. These schemes were highly popular due to their simplicity, allowing investors to invest in rupees and benefit from professional management without needing to open international brokerage accounts or deal with foreign exchange complexities. They provided exposure to diverse markets like the US, Europe, and emerging economies, bringing global giants within reach of the common Indian investor.

The Regulatory Shift and Restrictions

In early 2022, SEBI issued a directive asking mutual funds to halt fresh subscriptions to schemes investing in overseas securities. This move was primarily triggered by the industry nearing the overall overseas investment limits set by the RBI for mutual funds (USD 7 billion for the industry and USD 1 billion for gold ETFs). While some funds were later allowed to resume investments as market values fluctuated, many popular global FoFs continue to restrict fresh inflows. This regulatory pause highlighted the inherent limits of the indirect route and pushed investors to consider direct avenues for global exposure, primarily through the RBI's LRS framework.

Navigating the RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS)

The Annual Limit and Its Scope

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), introduced by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), allows all resident individuals, including minors, to freely remit up to USD 250,000 per financial year (April 1 to March 31) for any permissible current or capital account transaction. This limit applies per individual, meaning a family of four could collectively remit USD 1 million. The scope of LRS is broad, covering everything from education and travel expenses to gifts, maintenance of close relatives, and crucially, investments in foreign equities, bonds, and real estate. This scheme is the primary legal route for resident Indians to directly invest in international markets. You can find more details on the RBI website: https://rbi.org.in.

Implications for Direct Investments

For those looking to invest directly in global markets, the LRS is the gateway. To leverage this, an investor typically needs to open an overseas trading account with an international brokerage firm. Funds are then remitted from an Indian bank account to the foreign brokerage account, utilising the LRS limit. This direct approach offers greater control over investment choices and access to a wider range of global securities, including individual stocks, bonds, and international ETFs, which might not be available through Indian mutual funds. However, it also entails managing foreign exchange conversions, understanding foreign market regulations, and directly handling tax compliance in India.

UCITS ETFs: Your Gateway to Diversified Global Exposure

What are UCITS ETFs?

UCITS stands for "Undertakings for the Collective Investment in Transferable Securities." It is a comprehensive regulatory framework established by the European Union (EU) for investment funds. Funds that comply with UCITS regulations are known for their high standards of investor protection, transparency, and liquidity. Most UCITS ETFs are domiciled in Ireland or Luxembourg, which are popular jurisdictions due to their favourable tax treaties and robust regulatory environments. These ETFs offer diversified exposure to various asset classes, sectors, and geographies, tracking indices like the S&P 500, NASDAQ 100, MSCI World, or specific country/sector indices.

Advantages for Indian Investors

UCITS ETFs present several compelling advantages for Indian investors seeking global diversification:

  • Diversification: They provide broad exposure to international markets, reducing reliance on the Indian economy and currency.
  • Regulation and Safety: The UCITS framework ensures stringent investor protection, robust risk management, and transparency, offering peace of mind.
  • Cost-Effectiveness: ETFs generally have lower expense ratios compared to actively managed mutual funds.
  • Liquidity: UCITS ETFs are traded on major stock exchanges, offering good liquidity.
  • Tax Efficiency: For Indian investors, particularly those investing in Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs, the India-Ireland Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement (DTAA) can be beneficial, potentially reducing withholding taxes on dividends. Ireland also does not levy estate duty on non-Irish residents holding Irish-domiciled assets.

How to Invest in UCITS ETFs

Investing in UCITS ETFs typically involves opening an account with an international brokerage platform that facilitates trading on European exchanges. The process generally includes:

  1. Choosing an International Broker: Select a reputable broker that offers access to the desired UCITS ETFs and supports remittances from India.
  2. Account Opening: Complete the Know Your Customer (KYC) process, which often requires identity proof, address proof, and tax residency documents.
  3. Remitting Funds: Transfer funds from your Indian bank account to your international brokerage account using the LRS. Your bank will require a declaration for the purpose of remittance.
  4. Placing Orders: Once funds are credited, you can purchase UCITS ETFs on the relevant exchange. It's advisable to start with broad-market ETFs for diversified exposure.

Taxation and Compliance for Overseas Investments

Income Tax on Dividends and Capital Gains

Investing in global assets through LRS requires careful consideration of Indian tax laws:

  • Dividends: Any dividends received from foreign stocks or ETFs are taxable in India as 'Income from Other Sources' at your applicable income tax slab rates. If tax has been withheld in the foreign country, you can claim a credit for it under the DTAA between India and that country, avoiding double taxation.
  • Capital Gains:

    • * Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG): If you sell foreign equities or ETFs within 24 months of purchase, the gains are considered short-term and are added to your total income, taxed at your applicable slab rates. * Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG): If you sell foreign equities or ETFs after holding them for more than 24 months, the gains are considered long-term. These are taxed at a flat rate of 20% after factoring in indexation benefits, which adjust the purchase cost for inflation. It is mandatory to declare all foreign assets and income in your Income Tax Return (ITR) using Schedule FA (Foreign Assets). More information is available on the Income Tax India website: https://incometax.gov.in.

Tax Collected at Source (TCS) on LRS Remittances

As per government regulations, Tax Collected at Source (TCS) is applicable on remittances made under the LRS.

  • Effective October 1, 2023, a TCS of 20% is applicable on all LRS remittances exceeding ₹7 lakhs in a financial year for purposes other than education or medical treatment. This includes remittances for overseas investments.
  • For example, if you remit ₹10 lakhs for global investments, TCS will be levied on ₹3 lakhs (₹10 lakhs - ₹7 lakhs threshold), amounting to ₹60,000.
  • It's important to note that TCS is not an additional tax but an advance tax. You can claim this TCS amount as a credit against your total tax liability when filing your Income Tax Return. If your actual tax liability is lower, you can claim a refund. Refer to CBDT notifications for the latest updates: https://incometaxindia.gov.in.

A real example

Meet Rahul, 38, ₹28L CTC, Bengaluru. Rahul has a well-diversified Indian portfolio but wants to allocate 15% of his investable surplus to global markets for better diversification and access to international growth stories. He plans to invest ₹15 lakhs into UCITS ETFs over the next year.

Here’s how his global investment journey might look:

  1. LRS Utilisation: Rahul decides to make a single remittance of ₹15 lakhs (approximately USD 18,072 at an exchange rate of ₹83/USD) to his international brokerage account. This amount is well within his annual LRS limit of USD 250,000.
  2. TCS Calculation: Since his remittance for investment purposes exceeds the ₹7 lakh threshold, TCS will be applicable.

    3. * Amount exceeding ₹7 lakhs = ₹15,00,000 - ₹7,00,000 = ₹8,00,000. * TCS @ 20% on ₹8,00,000 = ₹1,60,000. * Rahul's bank will collect ₹1,60,000 as TCS when he remits the ₹15 lakhs. So, he will effectively pay ₹16,60,000 from his Indian bank account, with ₹15 lakhs going towards his investment and ₹1.6 lakhs being the advance tax.

  3. Investment in UCITS ETFs: Rahul uses the ₹15 lakhs to purchase a mix of UCITS ETFs, including one tracking the S&P 500 and another focused on European technology companies, through his international brokerage account.
  4. Dividend Income: After a year, Rahul receives dividends totalling ₹20,000 from his UCITS ETFs. Assume a 15% withholding tax (WHT) of ₹3,000 was deducted in the foreign country.

    5. * In India, this ₹20,000 will be added to his 'Income from Other Sources'. If Rahul is in the 30% tax bracket, his tax liability on this dividend would be ₹6,000. * He can claim a tax credit for the ₹3,000 WHT paid abroad, reducing his Indian tax liability on dividends to ₹3,000 (₹6,000 - ₹3,000).

  5. Capital Gains: After 30 months, Rahul decides to sell a portion of his ETFs, realising a gain of ₹2,00,000.

    6. * Since he held them for more than 24 months, this is considered a Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG). * Assuming an indexation benefit reduces his taxable gain to ₹1,80,000. * His LTCG tax liability would be 20% of ₹1,80,000 = ₹36,000.

  6. ITR Filing: When filing his ITR, Rahul will declare his foreign assets in Schedule FA, report his dividend income and capital gains, and claim the ₹1,60,000 TCS credit and any WHT credit against his total tax liability. If his total tax liability for the year (including salary, dividends, capital gains) is, say, ₹4,00,000, he would have already paid ₹1,60,000 as TCS. He would then pay the remaining ₹2,40,000 or claim a refund if his actual liability is lower than the advance tax paid.

What to do this week

  1. Assess Your Global Exposure: Review your existing investment portfolio to understand your current allocation to international markets, if any, and identify areas for diversification.
  2. Understand Your LRS Capacity: Calculate how much of the USD 250,000 annual LRS limit you have already utilised or plan to use for other purposes (travel, gifts, etc.) to determine your available investment capacity.
  3. Research International Brokerage Platforms: Explore reputable international brokers that facilitate direct investments in global stocks and UCITS ETFs, comparing their fees, platform features, and customer support.
  4. Consult a Financial Planner: Speak with a Certified Financial Planner (CFP) to evaluate if direct global investing aligns with your financial goals, risk tolerance, and to understand the detailed tax implications for your specific situation.
  5. Start Small with UCITS ETFs: Consider making a small, initial investment in a broad-market UCITS ETF (e.g., one tracking a global index) to get accustomed to the process, understand the platform, and gain initial exposure.

FAQ

Why are Indian mutual funds no longer accepting fresh investments in global funds?

Many Indian mutual funds halted fresh investments into global schemes because the industry collectively approached or breached the overseas investment limits set by the RBI and SEBI, necessitating a temporary pause on new inflows.

What is the RBI's LRS limit for global investments?

The RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) allows resident Indian individuals to remit up to USD 250,000 per financial year (April 1 to March 31) for various purposes, including direct investments in foreign assets.

What are UCITS ETFs and why are they relevant for Indian investors?

UCITS ETFs are Exchange Traded Funds regulated under a strict European Union framework, known for investor protection and transparency. They are relevant for Indian investors as they offer a regulated, cost-effective, and diversified way to gain direct exposure to global markets, especially after restrictions on Indian global mutual funds.

How are dividends and capital gains from global investments taxed in India?

Dividends are taxed as 'Income from Other Sources' at your slab rates. Short-Term Capital Gains (held < 24 months) are taxed at slab rates, while Long-Term Capital Gains (held > 24 months) are taxed at 20% with indexation benefits.

Is TCS applicable on remittances for global investments?

Yes, a Tax Collected at Source (TCS) of 20% is applicable on LRS remittances exceeding ₹7 lakhs in a financial year for investment purposes. This is an advance tax that can be claimed as a credit against your total tax liability.

Can Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) invest in UCITS ETFs?

NRIs are generally not subject to LRS limits as they are not resident Indians. They can typically invest in UCITS ETFs through international brokerage accounts, subject to the regulations of their country of residence and the brokerage firm.
