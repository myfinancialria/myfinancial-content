What Happens When a Company Leaves the Stock Market? Understanding Voluntary Delisting

TL;DR

  • Voluntary delisting is when a company removes its shares from public trading on a stock exchange.
  • This means you can no longer easily buy or sell those shares on platforms like NSE or BSE.
  • Companies usually do this to go private, giving promoters more control and saving costs.
  • Public shareholders get a chance to sell their shares back to the promoters through a special bidding process called Reverse Book Building.
  • It's important for investors to understand this process as it affects how they can exit their investment.

What's this about?

Imagine you own a small part of a company by holding its shares. Usually, you can buy or sell these shares easily on a stock exchange like the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). But sometimes, a company decides it no longer wants its shares traded publicly. This process is called voluntary delisting. It means the company will remove its shares from the stock exchange. Once delisted, those shares cannot be bought or sold by the general public on the exchange anymore.

Why it's in the news right now

Delisting is a recurring topic in financial news whenever a company's promoters decide to take it private. With changing market conditions and business strategies, such decisions come up from time to time. This makes understanding the delisting process crucial for any investor, as it directly impacts their ability to trade shares and the value of their holdings. Recently, discussions around corporate restructuring and promoter consolidation have again brought this mechanism into focus, reminding investors of its implications.

How it actually works

Voluntary delisting in India follows a clear process set by SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India), our market regulator. Here’s a simplified breakdown:

  1. Board and Shareholder Approval: First, the company's board of directors approves the delisting proposal. Then, it needs a special resolution from shareholders. For this to pass, at least two-thirds (66.67%) of the public shareholders (those other than the promoters) must vote in favour. This ensures minority shareholders have a say.
  2. Floor Price Calculation: The company calculates a 'floor price' for its shares. This is a minimum price based on specific rules, like the average of recent share prices. It acts as a guide, but the final price can be higher.
  3. Reverse Book Building (RBB) Process: This is the core of voluntary delisting. Instead of the company setting a final price, public shareholders bid to sell their shares.

    4. * Think of it like an auction, but in reverse. Shareholders submit the price at which they are willing to sell their shares back to the promoters. * The promoters then look at all these bids. They need to acquire enough shares to increase their ownership to at least 90% of the total share capital. * The 'discovered price' is the lowest price at which the promoters can acquire enough shares to cross this 90% threshold.

  4. Promoter's Decision: The promoters have a choice:

    5. * Accept the discovered price and buy all shares offered at or below that price, provided they reach the 90% ownership mark. * Reject the discovered price if they find it too high. If they reject it, the delisting offer fails, and the shares remain listed.

  5. Payment and Delisting: If the discovered price is accepted and the 90% threshold is met, shareholders who bid at or below this price receive payment. Once the shares are acquired, they are removed from the stock exchange, and the company becomes private.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a popular 'kirana' (grocery) shop in your neighbourhood that decided to sell small ownership stakes to its regular customers a few years ago. Now, the original family that owns the shop wants to take full control again. They decide to "delist" their shop from being publicly owned.

They tell all their customer-owners, "We want to buy back your shares." Instead of them setting a price, they ask each customer, "What's the lowest price you're willing to sell your share for?" They collect all these offers. Then, they figure out the lowest price at which they can buy enough shares to own 90% of the shop again. If they like that price, they pay everyone who offered to sell at or below that price, and the shop is no longer partly owned by customers – it's fully back with the family. If the customers ask for too high a price, the family might say, "No thanks," and the shop remains partly owned by customers.

Why it matters to you

  1. Exit Opportunity: If you hold shares of a company that announces voluntary delisting, it gives you an opportunity to sell your shares back to the promoters. This is often the primary way to exit your investment once the company goes private.
  2. Price Discovery: The Reverse Book Building process helps discover a fair price for your shares, as it's based on what other shareholders are willing to accept. However, there's no guarantee the final price will be higher than the current market price.
  3. Loss of Liquidity: If you choose not to sell your shares during the delisting offer, your shares become illiquid. This means you can no longer easily sell them on the stock exchange, as they are no longer traded publicly. Finding a buyer for unlisted shares can be very difficult.
  4. Investment Strategy Review: A delisting announcement might prompt you to review your investment in that company. You'll need to decide whether to participate in the offer or hold onto unlisted shares.
  5. Impact on Portfolio: If a significant holding in your portfolio delists, it changes the composition and liquidity of your overall investments.

Common confusions cleared up

  • Myth: Delisting means the company is going bankrupt or shutting down.

    • * Fact: Not at all. Voluntary delisting usually happens because the promoters want more control or strategic flexibility, or to save costs. The company can be financially healthy and continue its operations as a private entity.

  • Myth: The company will force you to sell your shares at a low price.

    • * Fact: SEBI rules ensure a transparent process, especially the Reverse Book Building. You get to decide the price at which you are willing to sell. Promoters must accept a price discovered through this process, or the delisting fails. You are not forced to sell if you don't agree with the price, but your shares will become illiquid.

  • Myth: Delisting is the same as a share buyback.

    • * Fact: While both involve companies buying back their own shares, the goals are different. A buyback is usually to improve shareholder value or return capital, and the shares remain listed. Delisting's primary goal is to take the company private, removing its shares from public trading entirely.

FAQ

What happens if I don't participate in the delisting offer?

If you don't tender your shares during the offer period, and the delisting is successful, your shares will become unlisted. This means they can no longer be traded on the stock exchange, making them highly illiquid and difficult to sell.

Can the delisting offer fail?

Yes, a delisting offer can fail. This typically happens if the promoters are unable to acquire enough shares to reach the 90% ownership threshold, or if they decide the discovered price through the Reverse Book Building process is too high and reject it.

Is the delisting price always higher than the current market price?

Not necessarily. While the Reverse Book Building process aims to discover a fair price, there's no guarantee it will be higher than the current market price. It depends on what price shareholders are willing to sell at and what price the promoters are willing to accept.

How long does a voluntary delisting process take?

The entire process, from board approval to final delisting, can take several months. SEBI regulations prescribe specific timelines for various stages, including public announcements, bidding periods, and settlement.

What is the role of SEBI in delisting?

SEBI plays a crucial role in protecting investor interests by setting clear regulations for the delisting process. These rules ensure transparency, fair price discovery through Reverse Book Building, and adequate opportunities for public shareholders to exit their investments.
