Business Mavericks · 2026-07-16

The year is 2016. India’s bustling electronics markets are a battleground. On one side stand the global titans: Sony, JBL, Sennheiser, with their premium audio gear costing upward of ₹5,000 – a luxury for most. On the other, a deluge of nameless, unbranded Chinese imports, cheap but notoriously unreliable, often failing within weeks. For millions of young, aspirational Indians, there was no middle ground – no stylish, durable, yet affordable option that spoke to their evolving lifestyle. It was in this stark landscape that Aman Gupta and Sameer Mehta, armed with a modest ₹30 lakhs of their own capital, decided to launch boAt. They had an audacious plan: to build an audio brand not by outspending the giants on traditional TV ads, but by leveraging the electrifying appeal of cricketers and digital influencers.

The World Before

Before boAt entered the fray, the Indian audio market was a tale of two extremes. High-fidelity headphones and sleek wireless earbuds from international brands were status symbols, but their price tags alienated the vast majority of consumers. A pair of branded earphones could easily set a young professional back ₹3,000 to ₹4,000, or even more, for marginally better sound quality that most users wouldn't discern. Meanwhile, the budget segment was a Wild West of generic products, often sourced from China, that prioritized low cost over everything else. These products offered poor sound, flimsy build quality, and a complete lack of aesthetic appeal. They were purely functional, and often not for long.

This market dynamic meant that consumers were forced to choose between breaking the bank for a premium experience or settling for a disposable one. There was no brand offering products that were both "good enough" in sound quality, durable, and, crucially, fashionable and affordable. The rise of smartphones and cheap internet data, particularly after 2016 with the entry of Jio, was rapidly increasing multimedia consumption across India, creating a massive, underserved demand for audio accessories. The prevailing marketing strategies were equally conventional. Big brands poured millions into prime-time TV slots, glossy print ads, and large-scale billboards, aiming for broad, generic reach. This left a gaping hole for a brand that could connect with India's youthful demographic, estimated at a median age of 28 in 2016, on their own terms.

The Crack

Aman Gupta, with his background in sales at Harman International (the company behind JBL), had seen this market gap firsthand. In 2016, he teamed up with Sameer Mehta to officially launch boAt as a consumer electronics brand under their parent company, Imagine Marketing. Their initial product wasn't even an audio device; it was a rugged, durable charging cable designed to withstand the daily wear and tear that often destroyed standard phone accessories. This small win gave them confidence. Soon, they shifted their focus to audio, starting with earphones and, eventually, wireless earbuds and speakers.

Their core philosophy was simple: offer "good-looking, affordable audio gear for people who couldn't (or wouldn't) pay international prices." This meant strategically pricing their products, typically between ₹1,000 to ₹3,000, significantly below international competitors but above the unbranded options. The true genius, however, lay in their marketing. While competitors chased mass market visibility through expensive traditional advertising, boAt adopted a digital-first approach, focusing heavily on e-commerce platforms like Amazon and Flipkart, and a groundbreaking influencer strategy.

Instead of shelling out crores for prime-time TV commercials, boAt invested in "micro-priced celebrity endorsements" with cricketers and Bollywood stars, alongside a network of social media influencers. Cricketers like Hardik Pandya, KL Rahul, Shikhar Dhawan, Shreyas Iyer, Rishabh Pant, and Jasprit Bumrah became "boAtheads" – active brand ambassadors who appeared in social media campaigns, in-match activations, and product launches. Bollywood celebrities such as Kartik Aaryan, Kiara Advani, Jacqueline Fernandes, and Ranbir Kapoor also joined the ranks. These weren't just passive endorsements; they were designed to transfer the cultural capital of these beloved figures directly to the boAt brand.

This strategy worked for several reasons. Firstly, it resonated deeply with young, digitally native consumers (Gen Z and millennials) who spent more time on social media than watching traditional television. Seeing their favourite sports heroes and film stars using boAt products, often in casual, lifestyle-oriented content, made the brand aspirational and relatable. Secondly, it was incredibly cost-effective. By focusing on digital platforms and targeted influencers, boAt bypassed the prohibitive costs of mainstream advertising, allowing them to allocate resources more efficiently. Aman Gupta himself articulated this philosophy, stating, "We believe in earning money and not burning money."

boAt didn't just sell electronics; it sold a "cool lifestyle." Their products were designed with bold colours and modern aesthetics, repositioning audio accessories from mere gadgets into fashion statements. This "affordable premium" positioning, where style met budget, made boAt products desirable without being out of reach. They cultivated a community, affectionately terming their customers "boAtheads," fostering a sense of belonging and loyalty that transcended simple product features.

What Happened Next

The results were nothing short of spectacular. From a modest ₹31 crore in sales in FY2017, boAt's revenue surged to ₹701 crore by FY2020. By the financial year 2022-23, the company recorded an impressive ₹4,000 crore in net sales.

Their market dominance grew exponentially. By the third quarter of 2020, boAt had already become India's #1 wearable brand, surpassing global giants like Apple, Samsung, and Xiaomi in its home market. Furthermore, it cemented its position as the fifth-largest wearable brand in the world by shipment volume in the same period, according to IDC.

This rapid rise was recognized with a valuation of $1.32 billion as of December 2022. boAt continued to expand its product portfolio beyond earphones and speakers, venturing successfully into truly wireless stereo (TWS) earbuds and smartwatches. While the smartwatch segment later saw increased competition, boAt still maintains a dominant share of roughly 26% to 28% in the overall Indian wearables market, largely driven by its robust TWS earbuds business. The company also initiated plans for an Initial Public Offering (IPO), first filing for a ₹2,000 crore IPO in January 2022, which was later deferred. It re-filed for a leaner ₹1,500 crore IPO in October 2025, reflecting its continued growth ambitions and financial maturity.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Understand the Local Pulse for "Affordable Premium": boAt didn't just offer cheap products; it identified a sweet spot where Indian consumers desired style, reasonable quality, and affordability. They mastered the "affordable premium" segment, proving that value often trumps brand heritage if the product looks good and performs well enough for the price.
  2. Digital-First, Influencer-Led Marketing Trumps Traditional Ads (for a specific audience): By eschewing expensive TV ads and focusing on digital platforms and relatable celebrity/influencer endorsements, boAt efficiently reached its target demographic of young, social media-savvy consumers. This strategy was not only more cost-effective but also built a deeper, more authentic connection than generic mass advertising.
  3. Build a Lifestyle, Not Just a Product: boAt successfully transformed audio accessories into fashion and lifestyle statements. By associating products with cultural trends, sports, and entertainment, they fostered an emotional connection, creating a loyal "boAthead" community that buys into an identity rather than just features.
  4. Founder as a Brand's Public Face: Aman Gupta's prominent role, particularly through shows like Shark Tank India, humanized the brand and gave it a recognizable, relatable face. This personal connection with the founder often translates into increased trust and affinity for the brand, especially in a D2C (direct-to-consumer) context.
