First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local chai shop that has been doing very well. Now, the owners want to open more branches across the city. To get the money needed for this expansion, they decide to sell a small part of their ownership in the shop to the public.

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is just like that. It's when a private company offers its shares to the public for the very first time. This is done in the primary market (where new shares are sold directly by the company). Once these shares are bought, they can then be traded on stock exchanges like the NSE or BSE. This process is called listing, and after listing, the shares are bought and sold in the secondary market (the regular stock market).

Companies do an IPO mainly to raise money for growth, pay off debts, or allow existing owners to sell some of their shares.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When a company announces an IPO, there are a few key pieces of information you'll always find. Understanding these helps you grasp the basics of the offering.

  1. Price Band: This is the range of prices at which the company is willing to sell its shares. For example, if the price band is ₹118 to ₹124, you can bid for shares anywhere within this range. The highest price in the band (₹124 in this example) is usually called the "cap price."
  2. Lot Size: You cannot buy just one share in an IPO. Instead, you have to apply for shares in fixed bundles called "lots." If the lot size is 100 shares, you must apply for 100 shares, or 200 shares, and so on. This is to manage the large number of applications.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is simply the lowest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO. It's calculated by multiplying the lot size by the highest price in the price band. For example, if the lot size is 100 shares and the cap price is ₹124, your minimum investment would be ₹12,400 (100 shares * ₹124).
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public through the IPO. It tells you how big the offering is in terms of shares. Sometimes, this is also expressed as a total rupee value.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS): An IPO typically consists of two parts.

    6. * Fresh Issue: This is when the company issues new shares, and the money raised from these shares goes directly to the company for its business purposes (like expansion or debt repayment). * Offer-for-Sale (OFS): This is when existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares. The money from these shares goes to the selling shareholders, not to the company itself. The data provided for these IPOs does not specify the breakdown between fresh issue and OFS.

  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the official document filed with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) that contains all the details about the company, its financials, the IPO, risks involved, and how the money will be used. Think of it as the company's detailed report card and business plan for potential investors. The DRHP is the draft version, and the RHP is the final one published before the IPO opens.

The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO might seem complex, but it follows a clear path:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI for approval. This document gives a detailed picture of the company.
  2. SEBI Approval & RHP: Once SEBI reviews and approves the DRHP, the company publishes the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) and announces the IPO dates and price band.
  3. Bidding Period: The IPO opens for a few days (usually 3 working days). During this time, you can place your bid for shares.

    4. * Retail Investors: Ordinary individuals like you and me can apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * HNI (High Net-worth Individuals) / NII (Non-Institutional Investors): These are individuals or entities who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * QIB (Qualified Institutional Buyers): These are large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.

  4. Allotment: After the bidding closes, the company and its bankers decide how to distribute the shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than available shares), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors. If you get shares, this is called allotment.
  5. Refund/Debit: If you are allotted shares, the money for those shares is debited from your bank account. If you are not allotted shares, the blocked money is unblocked and returned to your account.
  6. Listing: Finally, the shares are listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE), usually within a week of the IPO closing. Once listed, you can buy or sell these shares in the open market.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine your favourite neighbourhood bakery, "Sweet Delights." They've been making delicious cakes for years, but now they want to buy a bigger oven and open a new shop across town. To raise the money, they decide to sell small "ownership coupons" for their bakery.

An IPO is just like Sweet Delights selling these ownership coupons to the public for the first time. When you buy these coupons (shares), you become a part-owner of the bakery. The money you pay helps them grow, and if the bakery does well, the value of your coupons might increase.

Things people get confused about

Here are some common misunderstandings about IPOs:

  • Myth: Applying for an IPO guarantees you will get shares.

    • * Fact: If an IPO receives more applications than the shares available (it's "oversubscribed"), shares are allotted through a lottery system, especially for retail investors. There's no guarantee of allotment.

  • Myth: All IPOs give you quick profits on listing day.

    • * Fact: While some IPOs might list at a higher price than their issue price, others may list lower or at the same price. There's no guarantee of "listing gains," and the share price can go up or down after listing based on market conditions and company performance.

  • Myth: You can buy any number of shares you want in an IPO.

    • * Fact: You must apply for shares in fixed bundles called "lots." You can only bid for a multiple of the specified lot size.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility via net banking, or through your stockbroker's platform. You'll need a demat account and a trading account.

What is a lot size?

A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot apply for individual shares; you must apply in multiples of the specified lot.

What is the difference between a Mainboard and an SME IPO?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger companies and list on the main exchanges (NSE/BSE). SME IPOs are for smaller companies and list on dedicated SME platforms, often with different rules and minimum investment amounts.

What happens if I don't get an allotment?

If you don't get an allotment, the money you had blocked for the IPO in your bank account will be unblocked and returned to you.

Can I sell my shares immediately after allotment?

Once the shares are listed on the stock exchange, you are free to buy or sell them just like any other stock in the secondary market.
