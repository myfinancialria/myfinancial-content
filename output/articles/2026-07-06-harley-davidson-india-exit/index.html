In the sweltering August of 2009, Harley-Davidson, the quintessential American motorcycle icon, roared into India with the promise of bringing its legendary cruisers to a nation on the cusp of an economic boom. The air crackled with anticipation as the company announced its official entry, with plans to launch models like the Roadster and Fatboy, initially as Completely Built Units (CBUs). Within a year, its first dealership opened in Hyderabad, a gleaming testament to Harley’s ambition in a market often called the world’s largest two-wheeler playground. Experts and enthusiasts alike imagined a new era of premium biking, fuelled by rising disposable incomes and a burgeoning aspirational class. Yet, just eleven years later, in September 2020, that dream sputtered to a halt. The bikes that once symbolized freedom on American highways found themselves ill-suited for India's chaotic roads, leading to a quiet, almost inevitable, exit after selling a mere 27,000 bikes.

The World Before

For decades, India has been a two-wheeler behemoth, a land where motorcycles and scooters are not just leisure vehicles but the lifeblood of daily commute for millions. Annually, over 17 million two-wheelers roll off assembly lines and onto Indian roads, making it the largest market globally. However, this colossal market was, and largely remains, dominated by utilitarian, low-capacity, and fiercely fuel-efficient bikes, typically under 150cc, priced for the masses. Brands like Hero MotoCorp and Bajaj Auto thrive in this high-volume, low-margin environment.

Harley-Davidson, on the other hand, existed in an entirely different universe. Synonymous with large-displacement V-twin engines, chrome, and the open road, its appeal lay in its distinct culture and powerful, heavy cruisers designed for long-distance touring on wide, uncongested highways. In the late 2000s, as India's economy grew and a visible middle and upper-middle class emerged, many global luxury brands saw immense potential. It was a belief that a segment of Indian consumers, armed with newfound wealth, would aspire to own international symbols of status and adventure. Harley-Davidson saw itself at the forefront of this premium wave, expecting that the demand for high-end motorcycles would naturally follow the country's rising incomes. The initial excitement surrounding its entry, even with bikes priced upwards of Rs. 10 Lakhs, underscored this optimistic outlook.

The Crack

Harley-Davidson’s journey in India began with a significant hurdle: pricing. Initially, all its bikes were imported as Completely Built Units (CBUs), attracting hefty import duties that often pushed their retail prices beyond the reach of even affluent buyers. To address this, the company established an assembly plant in Bawal, Haryana, by 2011, a "complete knock-down" (CKD) facility that assembled kits shipped from its U.S. plants. This move was intended to reduce import duties and make the bikes more affordable.

The strategy bore some fruit with the launch of the Street 750 and Street 500 models in 2014. The Street 750, priced at Rs 4.10 lakh (ex-showroom), was almost half the price of its next most affordable model and was even manufactured in India (with the Street 500 reserved for export). This tactical price reduction led to a surge in sales, and Harley even set an ambitious target of 10,000 units a year by 2016.

However, beneath the surface, fundamental issues persisted. Harley’s core product – big, heavy, air-cooled cruisers – proved to be a poor fit for India's specific conditions. The bikes often struggled with the country's traffic-choked roads, frequent potholes, and the intense heat, which made the air-cooled engines uncomfortable for riders. Even the Street 750, designed to be more compact, faced criticism for excessive heat and low ground clearance. As one former owner noted, "The only issue was the heat generation from the air-cooled engines," a problem often ignored initially due to the brand's allure but which became a significant pain point.

Moreover, Harley-Davidson faced formidable local competition. Royal Enfield, with its deeply entrenched history in India dating back to 1949, offered a range of mid-capacity, more affordable cruisers that resonated deeply with Indian riders. Royal Enfield often sold more bikes in a single month than Harley managed in an entire decade.

Sales figures painted a grim picture. From an initial surge, Harley’s sales volumes steadily declined. From a peak of 11,753 units in 2016, sales plummeted to a mere 2,470 units in the fiscal year ending March 2020. In the first quarter of FY2021 (April-June 2020), a paltry 100 motorcycles were sold. Over its entire 11-year run, Harley-Davidson sold roughly 27,000 bikes, a fraction of its initial aspirations.

This dismal performance wasn't just a sales problem; it created significant financial strain for its dealer network. With 35 dealerships across the country, each requiring an estimated investment of Rs 3-4 crore, the average sales of fewer than 70 units per dealer annually meant many dealerships were bleeding cash. Furthermore, the lack of a robust local vendor base meant spare parts often had to be imported, leading to delays and dissatisfied customers.

By 2020, Harley-Davidson was in the midst of a global struggle, reporting its first quarterly loss in over a decade. New CEO Jochen Zeitz introduced a radical global restructuring plan called "The Rewire," aimed at consolidating operations, focusing on core markets, and exiting unprofitable ventures. India, identified as one of its worst-performing international markets and contributing less than 5% to the company’s total sales volume, became a prime candidate for this strategic overhaul. In September 2020, Harley-Davidson officially announced its decision to cease sales and manufacturing operations in India, shutting down its Bawal assembly unit and laying off approximately 70 employees. The exit incurred $75 million in restructuring costs.

What Happened Next

Harley-Davidson's "exit" from India was not a complete disappearance. A month after its announcement, in October 2020, the company unveiled a strategic partnership with Hero MotoCorp, the world’s largest manufacturer of motorcycles and scooters by volume. Under this distribution agreement, Hero MotoCorp took over the sales and servicing of Harley-Davidson motorcycles, parts, accessories, and general merchandise through a network of brand-exclusive dealerships and its extensive existing network.

Crucially, the partnership extended beyond distribution. A licensing agreement empowered Hero MotoCorp to develop and sell a range of premium motorcycles under the Harley-Davidson brand name. This collaboration soon bore fruit with the launch of models like the Harley-Davidson X440 and Mavrick 440, developed and manufactured by Hero MotoCorp in India. These bikes, with smaller displacements and more competitive pricing, represented a significant shift towards better product-market fit for the Indian consumer.

This strategic pivot, leveraging Hero's local manufacturing prowess and vast distribution network, injected new life into the brand in India. By FY2022, Harley-Davidson had staged a remarkable comeback in the high-end motorcycle segment, regaining the number one position in the 1000cc and above category. The brand sold 531 units in this segment, marking a 37% year-on-year growth. The partnership continues to expand, with plans to introduce more bikes and grow the premium showroom network, emphasizing a long-term commitment to the Indian market, albeit with a significantly revised strategy. India is now seen as a key manufacturing hub for these entry-level performance bikes, not just for local sales but potentially for global markets, aligning with Harley’s broader "Back to Bricks" strategy to make its bikes more accessible to a wider audience.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Market-Specific Product-Market Fit is Non-Negotiable: Harley-Davidson's initial failure in India stemmed from a fundamental mismatch between its traditional large, heavy cruisers and the realities of Indian roads, climate, and consumer preferences. Even with local assembly, simply offering a slightly smaller version of its existing portfolio wasn't enough. Companies expanding internationally must critically assess if their core product genuinely serves the unique needs and conditions of the target market, rather than assuming global appeal.
  1. True Localisation Goes Beyond Assembly: While setting up a CKD assembly plant helped reduce import duties, it didn't solve deeper supply chain inefficiencies or the need for a localized vendor base for spare parts. This led to high maintenance costs and poor service experiences, alienating customers. For price-sensitive markets like India, genuine localization, encompassing manufacturing, supply chains, and after-sales support, is crucial for cost-effectiveness and customer satisfaction.
  1. The Power of Strategic Partnerships: Harley-Davidson’s solo attempt floundered, but its subsequent partnership with Hero MotoCorp proved to be a lifeline. Hero's deep understanding of the Indian market, extensive distribution network, and manufacturing capabilities for smaller-displacement bikes provided Harley with the missing pieces. This highlights that for complex, competitive, and culturally distinct markets, collaborating with a strong local player can be a more effective entry or re-entry strategy than going it alone.
  1. Premium Doesn't Mean Unaffordable: While India's aspirational class is growing, "premium" doesn't equate to "exorbitantly priced" for most segments. Harley's initial CBU models were out of reach, and even its assembled bikes faced stiff competition from more value-for-money offerings. The success of the Hero-developed X440 demonstrates that there is a market for premium experiences, but it must be delivered at a price point that acknowledges local income levels and competitive landscapes.
