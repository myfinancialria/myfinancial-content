Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company offers its shares to the public for the first time, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The "listing day" is the very first day these shares start trading on the stock exchange. Often, there's a lot of excitement, and the share price might jump significantly, leading to a "listing gain" (the percentage difference between the IPO price and the closing price on the first trading day).

However, the listing day can be quite volatile (meaning prices can change very quickly and sharply). It's like the grand opening of a new shop – there's a lot of buzz, special offers, and a crowd. But what happens after the initial excitement fades?

Looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after its listing gives us a calmer, more realistic picture. By this time, the initial frenzy has usually settled down. Also, the "lock-in period" for "anchor investors" (large institutional investors who commit to buying shares before the IPO opens to the public) often ends around 30 days. Once this lock-in period is over, these investors are free to sell their shares, which can impact the supply and demand dynamics in the market. This window helps us see where the market truly values the company's shares after the initial buzz.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

Think of an IPO like a special stall at a school fete selling unique handmade items.

If many people want to buy these items but there are only a few available, the price might go up quickly. This is similar to an IPO "pop" or "listing gain." It happens when there is high "demand" (many investors want to buy shares) and limited "supply" (the number of shares available). High "subscription levels" (when the IPO receives applications for many more shares than it offers) often indicate strong demand. A positive "market mood" (overall optimistic feeling in the stock market) and attractive "pricing" (the IPO price set by the company) also contribute to a listing gain.

On the other hand, if there aren't enough buyers for the items, or if the price seems too high, the items might not sell well. This is like an IPO "drop" or "listing loss." This occurs when demand is low, or the "pricing" of the IPO was considered too high by the market, or the general "market mood" is weak.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot of the market's initial reaction. It's often driven by short-term excitement or speculation. "Volatility" (quick and large price movements) is very common on the first day.

A stock might list with a big gain, but then its price could fall in the following weeks or months. Conversely, a stock might list flat or even with a small loss, but then its price could rise significantly later as more investors recognise its value. The performance of the "broader market" (the overall stock market) can also influence a stock's movement, regardless of its individual company performance. The true test of a company's share price often comes after the initial excitement fades and the market starts to assess its business fundamentals.

A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like booking tickets for a new movie on its opening day. Everyone rushes to get tickets, and they might sell out quickly, sometimes even at higher prices on the black market (this is like the listing day frenzy). But what truly matters is whether the movie is still running successfully and drawing crowds a month or two later, after the initial hype has settled. The "current price" of an IPO reflects that sustained interest (or lack thereof) in the company's shares.

Things people get confused about

Myth → A big listing gain means it's a great company.

Fact: A listing gain only shows initial market demand and sentiment. A company's long-term success depends on its business performance, management quality, and financial health, not just its IPO debut.

Myth → If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment.

Fact: A listing day drop can be due to various factors like overall market conditions or the IPO's pricing. The company's underlying business might still be strong, and its share price could recover or even grow over time.

Myth → All SME IPOs give huge listing gains.

Fact: As our report shows, SME IPOs can be quite volatile. While some offer significant gains, others can list with losses or see their prices fall after listing. They also generally carry higher risks due to their smaller size and often less established business operations.

FAQ

What is an SME IPO?

An SME IPO (Small and Medium Enterprises Initial Public Offering) is for smaller companies looking to raise capital. They list on separate platforms like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, with different rules compared to mainboard IPOs.

What is the 'issue price'?

The 'issue price' is the fixed price per share at which the company offers its shares to investors during the IPO.

What does 'current price' mean?

The 'current price' is the latest available market price of the share on the stock exchange, reflecting what buyers and sellers are willing to trade it for now.

How do I apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility or through your brokerage firm's online platform. Your money remains blocked in your account until shares are allotted.

What is 'allotment'?

'Allotment' is the process where shares are distributed to successful applicants after an IPO closes. If demand is high, shares might be allotted on a pro-rata basis or through a lottery system.
