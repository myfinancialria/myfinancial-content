Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The first day these shares are traded on the stock exchange is called the listing day. On this day, there's often a lot of excitement, and prices can move quite a bit. The listing gain is the percentage difference between the IPO price (what you paid for the shares) and the price the shares close at on their first day of trading.

However, this first-day price doesn't always tell the whole story. Many things can influence it, including short-term demand and market buzz. We look at prices 30 to 60 days after listing because by then, the initial excitement usually settles down. Also, for some big investors called anchor investors (who commit to buying a large chunk of shares before the IPO opens), there's often a 30-day "lock-in" period where they cannot sell their shares. Once this period ends, their selling or holding decisions can also affect the price. So, looking at the price a month or two later gives us a calmer, more reflective picture of how the market values the company's shares.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

Imagine a popular new sweet shop opening in your neighbourhood. If everyone rushes to buy the sweets, and there aren't enough for everyone, the shop might decide to sell them at a slightly higher price. This is similar to how IPOs "pop" or list above their issue price.

  • Demand vs. Supply: If many investors want to buy shares of a company (high demand) but there are limited shares available (low supply), the price tends to go up. This often leads to a "pop" on listing day. If there are more shares than buyers, the price might drop.
  • Subscription Levels: This tells you how many times an IPO was "oversubscribed." For example, if an IPO was subscribed 10 times, it means investors bid for 10 times more shares than the company offered. High subscription levels usually suggest strong demand and can lead to a listing pop.
  • Market Mood: The overall feeling in the stock market can also play a big role. If the broader market is doing well and investors are confident, IPOs tend to do better.
  • Pricing: The company and its bankers decide the issue price (the price at which shares are offered in the IPO). If the shares are priced attractively, meaning they seem a bit cheaper than what the market might value them at, it can create more demand and a listing gain.
  • Anchor Investors and Lock-in: As mentioned, anchor investors are big institutional investors (like mutual funds or insurance companies) who buy a significant portion of the IPO shares before it opens to the public. Their participation often signals confidence in the company. Their shares are typically "locked in" for a certain period (often 30 days), meaning they cannot sell them immediately. This reduces the supply of shares in the market initially.

A "pop" on listing means the shares started trading at a price higher than the IPO price. A "drop" means they started trading lower. Both are simply reflections of market forces on that particular day.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day is just one day in a company's long journey on the stock exchange. The price on that day is influenced by immediate demand and supply, which can sometimes be driven by short-term excitement or speculation.

  • Volatility: Share prices can be very volatile, meaning they can change quickly and sometimes dramatically. A stock might list with a big gain, but then its price could fall in the following weeks or months as the market assesses its long-term value. Conversely, a stock might list flat or even with a small drop, but then grow steadily over time if the company performs well.
  • Broader Market Influence: The overall performance of the stock market can also affect individual stocks. If the market as a whole is going through a tough phase, even good companies might see their share prices affected.
  • Company Performance: Ultimately, a company's share price over the long term is linked to its business performance, earnings, and future prospects. These factors take time to play out and are not fully reflected on listing day.

A simple way to think about it

Think of it like this: A new stall opens at a school fete selling handmade crafts. On the first day, everyone rushes to buy, and some items sell out quickly at a slightly higher price than expected. That's like the listing day pop. But after a month, the initial rush is over. People are now buying based on how much they actually like the crafts, how useful they are, and if the prices are fair compared to other stalls. The price the market settles on after a month or two is usually a calmer reflection of what buyers truly think the crafts are worth, beyond the initial excitement.

Things people get confused about

Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.

Fact: A big listing gain simply means there was high demand for the shares on the first day of trading. It doesn't automatically mean the company is fundamentally strong or that its share price will continue to rise.

Myth → Fact: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad company.

Fact: A listing day drop can happen due to various reasons, including overall market conditions, how the IPO was priced, or simply less-than-expected demand. It doesn't necessarily mean the company itself is bad. Its performance over time will tell the real story.

Myth → Fact: Applying for IPOs is a guaranteed way to make quick money.

Fact: Like any investment in the stock market, IPOs carry risks. While some IPOs do offer good listing gains, others may list flat or even below their issue price. There are no guarantees of profit.

FAQ

What is a Mainboard IPO?

A Mainboard IPO is when a company lists its shares on the main exchanges like the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE), typically for larger, more established companies.

What is an SME IPO?

An SME IPO is for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) that list their shares on dedicated platforms within the NSE or BSE, designed for smaller companies with different listing requirements.

What is "issue price"?

The issue price is the fixed price per share at which the company offers its shares to the public during the IPO.

What is "current price"?

The current price is the latest trading price of the share on the stock exchange at the time this report was compiled.

What does "return vs issue price" mean?

This percentage shows how much the current market price of a share has changed compared to its original IPO issue price. A positive percentage means the current price is higher than the IPO price, while a negative percentage means it's lower.
