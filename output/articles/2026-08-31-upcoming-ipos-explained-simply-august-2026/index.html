First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local shop that has been doing very well. Now, the owners want to expand, maybe open new branches or buy new equipment. To get the money for this, they decide to sell a small part of their ownership to the public. This act of a private company selling its shares for the first time to the public is called an Initial Public Offering (IPO).

When a company does an IPO, it's raising money from the primary market (where new shares are sold directly by the company). Once these shares are sold, they then get listed (start trading) on stock exchanges like the NSE or BSE, where people can buy and sell them among themselves.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, there are a few key pieces of information that help you understand what's on offer:

  1. Price Band: This is the range, like ₹78 to ₹82, within which the company offers its shares. You can bid at any price within this range. Most retail investors (those applying for up to ₹2 lakh worth of shares) choose to bid at the cut-off price, which is usually the highest price in the band (₹82 in our example). This ensures your bid is considered at the final price discovered.
  1. Lot Size: This is the minimum number of shares you must apply for. You cannot apply for just one share; you have to apply in multiples of this lot size. For example, if the lot size is 100 shares, you must apply for 100, 200, 300 shares, and so on. This helps manage the large number of applications.
  1. Minimum Investment: This is calculated by multiplying the lot size by the upper end of the price band. For instance, if the lot size was 100 shares and the upper price band was ₹82, your minimum investment would be ₹8,200 (100 shares * ₹82). This is the smallest amount of money you need to invest to participate in the IPO.
  1. Issue Size: This refers to the total value of shares the company is offering to the public, or the total number of shares being sold. It tells you how much money the company aims to raise from the IPO. A larger issue size often means more shares are available for allotment.
  1. Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS): An IPO can be a mix of two things:

    2. * Fresh Issue: The company issues new shares, and the money raised from these shares goes directly to the company. This money is typically used for business expansion, debt repayment, or other corporate purposes. * Offer-for-Sale (OFS): Existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares. The money raised from these shares goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself. The company does not receive any funds from an OFS.

  1. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the official document filed with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) that contains all the important details about the company, its financials, business operations, risks involved, management team, and how the IPO proceeds will be used. The DRHP is the initial draft, and the RHP is the final version before the IPO opens. It's like the company's detailed report card and business plan for potential investors.

The IPO process, step by step

Understanding the journey of an IPO can make the whole process clearer:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first prepares a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with all its details and files it with SEBI for approval. This document is made public for everyone to review.
  1. Price Band Announcement: Once SEBI gives its nod, the company announces the price band (e.g., ₹78-₹82) and the lot size for the IPO.
  1. Bidding Period: The IPO opens for a few days (typically 3-5 days). During this time, investors can place their bids for shares. Bids are categorised:

    2. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Individuals applying for shares worth up to ₹2 lakh. * Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs) or High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs): Individuals or entities applying for shares worth more than ₹2 lakh. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large institutional investors like mutual funds, foreign institutional investors, and insurance companies.

  1. Allotment: After the bidding closes, the company and its registrars (firms that manage share records) determine the final offer price and decide how to distribute the shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (more demand than shares available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
  1. Listing: Finally, the shares get listed on the stock exchanges (like NSE or BSE) on a specific date. This is when they start trading, and you can buy or sell them like any other stock.

A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a school fete stall. The school (the company) wants to raise money for new books or sports equipment. So, they decide to sell small "ownership coupons" of their fete stall to parents and students (the public). Each coupon represents a tiny part of the stall.

When you buy these coupons, you're giving money to the school. If the fete stall does well, the value of your coupon might go up. If it doesn't do so well, the value might go down. An IPO is simply a company selling these "ownership coupons" (shares) for the very first time to the public to raise funds or allow existing owners to sell their stake.

Things people get confused about

Myth: Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares. Fact: If an IPO is very popular (oversubscribed), there are more applications than shares available. In such cases, shares are allotted through a lucky draw system, especially for retail investors. You might not get any shares even if you apply.

Myth: Listing gain is guaranteed in an IPO. Fact: While some IPOs see their share prices rise on the first day of trading (listing day), this is not guaranteed. Share prices can also fall below the issue price, leading to a listing loss. The market decides the price based on demand and supply.

Myth: IPOs are only for quick profits. Fact: An IPO is just the first step for a company to become publicly traded. Investing in an IPO means becoming a part-owner of the company. Like any investment, it should ideally be viewed with a long-term perspective, considering the company's fundamentals and future prospects, not just short-term gains.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank using the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility, or directly through your demat account broker's platform. Your application amount will be blocked in your bank account until allotment, not debited immediately.

What is a lot size?

The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for when applying in an IPO. It's set by the company to ensure efficient processing of applications and fair distribution of shares.

What is a price band?

The price band is the fixed range (e.g., ₹190 to ₹200) within which you can place your bid for the shares. You can choose any price within this range.

What is the 'cut-off price'?

The cut-off price is typically the highest price within the announced price band. Retail investors often choose to bid at the cut-off price to ensure their application is considered at the final offer price, which is usually discovered at the upper end of the band if there is strong demand.

Can I sell my shares on the listing day?

Yes, once the shares are listed on the stock exchange and trading begins, you can sell them on the same day through your demat account, just like any other listed stock.
