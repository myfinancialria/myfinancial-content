Business Mavericks · 2026-10-06

The year was 2019, and WeWork, the co-working behemoth, was on top of the world. Its charismatic co-founder and CEO, Adam Neumann, had cultivated an image of a visionary, transforming drab office spaces into vibrant "communities." SoftBank, the Japanese investment giant, had poured billions into the company, pushing its private valuation to a staggering $47 billion in January 2019. Neumann, with his flowing hair and grand pronouncements, seemed to embody the limitless ambition of the tech-driven startup era. He envisioned WeWork not merely as an office rental company, but as a global movement, expanding into co-living (WeLive) and even a "conscious entrepreneurial school" for children (WeGrow). The stage was set for one of the most anticipated IPOs of the year, a public debut that promised to cement WeWork's status as a disruptor of the traditional real estate market.

The World Before

Before WeWork burst onto the scene, the commercial real estate market was largely a predictable, staid affair. Businesses typically signed long-term leases, often spanning 10 to 15 years, committing to fixed spaces. The idea of flexible, month-to-month office rentals, particularly for freelancers, startups, and smaller companies, was nascent. There were established players like Regus, offering serviced offices, but they often lacked the hip, community-focused ethos that Neumann and his co-founder Miguel McKelvey would champion.

Neumann and McKelvey's journey began in 2008 with GreenDesk, an "eco-friendly coworking space" in Brooklyn. They saw an opportunity in subdividing office floors and offering shared amenities, earning more than a traditional single-client lease. This early success, rooted in the burgeoning sharing economy, proved that there was demand for flexible workspaces. In 2010, they sold their stake in GreenDesk for approximately $500,000 each and founded WeWork, armed with a similar model but a much grander vision. They would lease large office floors or entire buildings, design and furnish them with a distinctive aesthetic, and then sublet desks and offices on flexible terms, fostering a sense of community among members. This approach resonated, allowing small businesses to avoid large upfront commitments and offering larger companies agile space solutions without the lengthy traditional real estate process.

The Crack

The first sign of trouble, a barely perceptible tremor beneath the gleaming facade, emerged on August 14, 2019, when WeWork's parent company, The We Company, publicly filed its S-1 registration statement for its initial public offering. This 359-page document, intended to woo public investors, instead became an immediate and brutal exposé of the company's precarious financials and questionable corporate governance.

One of the most glaring issues was the colossal mismatch between WeWork's long-term liabilities and its short-term revenue commitments. The S-1 revealed that WeWork had future undiscounted minimum lease obligations totaling an astounding $47.2 billion, primarily for leases averaging around 15 years for its U.S. locations. Against this, its future lease commitments from members only amounted to about $4 billion. This meant WeWork was on the hook for massive, fixed rental payments for years, regardless of whether its flexible-term members stayed or left. As one expert succinctly put it, WeWork was selling flexibility to its customers while carrying immense fixed commitments itself.

Beyond the fundamental business model fragility, the S-1 laid bare a series of alarming self-dealings and corporate governance red flags centered around Adam Neumann.

  • Trademark Sale: The company had paid an entity owned by Neumann and other WeWork founders $5.9 million for the rights to the "We" family of trademarks. This meant Neumann essentially sold a brand name to his own company. Following public outcry, Neumann returned the $5.9 million and transferred all trademark rights to the company in early September 2019.
  • Personal Loans: The S-1 disclosed that WeWork had extended millions in loans to Neumann and other executives. In April 2019, the company issued a $362.1 million loan to Neumann at a remarkably low 2.89% interest rate to help him exercise stock options. This was in addition to a $7 million loan in 2016 at a mere 0.64% interest, which he repaid in 2017 with only $100,000 in interest.
  • Landlord-Tenant Conflict: Neumann personally owned several properties that he then leased back to WeWork, creating a clear conflict of interest. In the first half of 2019, WeWork made cash payments totaling $4.2 million to landlord entities affiliated with Neumann, with future minimum payments of $237 million for these specific leases.
  • Super-Voting Shares: The S-1 revealed a governance structure that granted Neumann disproportionate voting power through super-voting shares, allowing him to control a majority of the company's voting stock even after the IPO.
  • Succession Clause: Perhaps one of the most unusual disclosures was a clause stating that in the event of Neumann's death, his wife, Rebekah Neumann, would have a say in selecting his successor.

The S-1 also highlighted the company's staggering losses, which were growing alongside its impressive revenue figures. In 2018, WeWork lost $1.9 billion on $1.8 billion in revenue, and for the first six months of 2019, it posted a loss of $690 million on $1.5 billion in revenue. While revenue was indeed increasing rapidly, the path to profitability remained elusive, with the company burning through cash at an alarming rate.

The public market's reaction was swift and brutal. Investors, analysts, and journalists scrutinized every page of the S-1, leading to widespread criticism. SoftBank, WeWork's largest investor, grew increasingly concerned and pressured the company to postpone the IPO. The valuation, once a fantastical $47 billion, began to plummet. By early September 2019, reports indicated WeWork was considering cutting its IPO valuation by more than 50%, targeting between $20 billion and $30 billion. This was quickly followed by an even steeper drop, with the reported public valuation falling to around $10 billion.

Amid mounting investor pressure and concerns over his leadership, Adam Neumann resigned as CEO on September 24, 2019, and gave up majority voting control. On September 30, 2019, WeWork formally withdrew its S-1 filing, indefinitely postponing its IPO. The dream of a $47 billion public offering had imploded in just six weeks.

What Happened Next

The aftermath was a stark reminder of the consequences of unchecked ambition and poor governance. WeWork's valuation, once $47 billion, plunged to less than $8 billion after SoftBank's bailout in October 2019, which made SoftBank the largest shareholder. As part of this deal, Neumann received a controversial exit package valued at approximately $1.7 billion, including stock, cash, and consulting fees, a payout that shocked corporate governance experts. As one expert noted, "The captain rammed the ship into a bridge and then was given the value of the ship to leave."

The company underwent significant restructuring, including layoffs of 2,400 employees in November 2019, about 20% of its global headcount. WeWork eventually went public in October 2021 through a merger with a Special Purpose Acquisition Company (SPAC), BowX Acquisition Corp., at a significantly reduced valuation of $9 billion. However, this public listing did not stabilize the business. WeWork's stock continued to decline, and by mid-August 2023, shares were trading at roughly $0.13.

On November 6, 2023, WeWork filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the U.S. and Canada, listing liabilities of approximately $18.7 billion against $15.1 billion in assets. This marked a stunning downfall, with its market cap falling to approximately $44.5 million. The company's lease obligations, which had been highlighted in the infamous S-1, were identified as its biggest turnaround obstacle, with WeWork spending over 80% of its revenue on rent and interest by mid-2023.

WeWork emerged from Chapter 11 bankruptcy on June 11, 2024, as a private company. The restructuring involved slashing $4 billion of debt and cutting future lease obligations by about 50%, saving an estimated $12 billion. Yardi Systems, a real estate technology provider and creditor, became the majority owner with a 60% stake, while SoftBank's stake was heavily diluted to roughly 20%. Adam Neumann, despite his role in the company's troubles, maintained a significant personal fortune, estimated at $2.8 billion by Forbes in 2023, thanks to earlier share sales and settlements.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. A Compelling Narrative Needs a Sound Business Model: WeWork successfully sold a compelling vision of community and flexibility, attracting massive private investment. However, its core business model—signing long-term, fixed-cost leases and subletting on short-term, flexible agreements—created an inherent and unsustainable financial mismatch. Growth alone cannot paper over fundamental unit economics problems.
  2. Governance Matters, Especially for Founders: Adam Neumann's extensive control through super-voting shares, coupled with numerous instances of self-dealing (trademark sales, personal loans from the company, leasing his own properties to WeWork), exposed a severe lack of corporate governance. Public markets demand transparency and accountability, and such practices are significant red flags that can quickly erode investor trust.
  3. Private Valuations Can Be Divorced from Public Reality: The $47 billion private valuation, largely fueled by SoftBank's aggressive investment strategy, proved to be an illusion when exposed to the scrutiny of public markets. The WeWork S-1 filing demonstrated that private market exuberance does not guarantee public market acceptance, and due diligence is paramount, regardless of the hype.
  4. Growth at Any Cost is Not a Sustainable Strategy: WeWork's rapid expansion was undeniably impressive, opening hundreds of locations globally. However, this aggressive growth was pursued without a clear, credible path to profitability, leading to ever-increasing losses and debt. Sustainable growth requires a balance between expansion and financial health.
