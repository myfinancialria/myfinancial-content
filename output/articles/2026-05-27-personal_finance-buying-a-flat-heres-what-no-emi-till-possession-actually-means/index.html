Buying a Flat? Here’s What ‘No EMI Till Possession’ Actually Means

TL;DR

  • 'No EMI till possession' schemes mean the builder pays your home loan interest until you get your flat.
  • While it offers temporary financial relief, the loan is still in your name, and you are ultimately liable.
  • Project delays are a major risk; if the builder stops paying, you bear the burden of both rent and EMIs.
  • The cost of this "free EMI" is often embedded in a higher property price, so you might pay more overall.
  • The RBI has cautioned against pure subvention schemes, advising banks to link disbursements to construction progress.

What this means in plain terms

Imagine you're buying an under-construction flat. Normally, when your home loan is disbursed, you'd start paying Equated Monthly Instalments (EMIs) right away. This can be tough if you're also paying rent for your current home. This is where "no EMI till possession" schemes come in.

Simply put, a "no EMI till possession" scheme is an arrangement where the real estate developer promises to pay the interest portion of your home loan until you physically receive possession of your new flat. This means you get a payment holiday during the construction period, easing your immediate financial burden. It's often marketed as a way to avoid the "double burden" of paying both rent and EMIs simultaneously.

However, it's crucial to understand that this isn't truly "no EMI." Your home loan is still active, and interest is accruing. It's just that the builder is temporarily covering that interest component on your behalf. The full EMI, which includes both principal and interest, only begins once you take possession of your home. These schemes are also known as 'subvention schemes' and typically involve a tripartite agreement between you (the homebuyer), the bank (lender), and the builder (developer).

How 'No EMI Till Possession' Schemes Work

The mechanics of a "no EMI till possession" scheme are designed to provide immediate financial relief to the homebuyer while ensuring the builder receives funding for the project. Here's a breakdown:

  1. Tripartite Agreement: The core of this scheme is a three-way agreement involving you (the buyer), the bank (lender), and the developer (builder). This agreement outlines the responsibilities of each party.
  2. Home Loan Sanction & Disbursement: You apply for and get a home loan sanctioned from a bank. The bank then disburses the loan amount, usually in stages or sometimes a significant portion upfront, directly to the builder based on the construction progress or as per the agreement.
  3. Builder Pays Interest (Pre-EMI): During the construction phase, from the time the loan is disbursed until you get possession of your flat, the builder undertakes to pay the interest component of your home loan to the bank. This period is often fixed, typically ranging from 24 to 36 months, or until possession, whichever is earlier.
  4. Buyer's Payment Holiday: As the buyer, you are not required to pay any EMIs (or only a very small initial amount) during this specified period. This allows you to manage other expenses like rent or existing loan payments without the added stress of a home loan EMI.
  5. Full EMI Starts Post-Possession: Once the construction is complete and you receive possession of your flat, the builder's responsibility to pay the interest ceases. From this point onwards, you begin paying the full EMIs, which include both the principal and interest components of your home loan.

It's important to note that while the builder pays the interest, the home loan is always in your name. This means the legal liability for the loan ultimately rests with you, the borrower.

The Hidden Costs and Interest Implications

While "no EMI till possession" sounds appealing, it's rarely a free lunch. There are hidden costs and interest implications that buyers must be aware of:

  1. Inflated Property Prices: Developers often factor the cost of paying interest on your behalf into the property's selling price. This means you might end up paying a premium for the flat compared to a similar property offered without such a scheme. Essentially, you're indirectly paying for the "free EMI" through a higher base price.
  2. Capitalised Interest: In some variations, the interest paid by the builder might be capitalised, meaning it's added to your principal loan amount. While you don't pay it upfront, your total loan amount increases, leading to higher EMIs and overall interest outgo once you start paying.
  3. Limited Duration of Subvention: The builder's commitment to pay interest is typically for a fixed period (e.g., 2-3 years) or until possession, whichever comes first. If the project gets delayed beyond this period (a common occurrence in Indian real estate), you will have to start paying the EMIs even if you haven't received possession. This can lead to the dreaded "double burden" of paying both rent and EMIs.
  4. Higher Interest Rates/Processing Fees: To compensate for the deferred payments, some lenders might charge slightly higher interest rates or processing fees for schemes associated with these arrangements. Always compare the effective interest rate with a standard home loan.
  5. False Sense of Affordability: The immediate relief of not paying EMIs can sometimes lead buyers to opt for a more expensive property than they can truly afford in the long run. When the full EMIs kick in, the financial shock can be substantial if not planned for adequately.

Risks and Regulatory Scrutiny

The attractive facade of "no EMI till possession" can hide significant risks, which is why regulatory bodies have expressed concerns.

  1. Project Delays and Builder Defaults: This is the biggest pitfall. Indian real estate is infamous for project delays. If the project isn't completed on time and the builder stops paying the pre-EMIs (either due to financial trouble or the expiry of the subvention period), the entire burden falls on you. You'll be forced to pay EMIs for a home you don't possess, potentially along with rent for your current accommodation.
  2. Impact on CIBIL Score: Crucially, the home loan is in your name. If the builder defaults on paying the interest, it's your loan account that becomes irregular, negatively impacting your credit (CIBIL) score. A poor CIBIL score can severely hinder your ability to get future loans or credit cards.
  3. Regulatory Red Flags: The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has recognized the dangers of pure subvention schemes, especially those where banks disburse a large portion of the loan upfront to builders without linking it to construction progress. The RBI has issued strict warnings and guidelines to banks, advising them to stop funding such schemes and to ensure due diligence, linking loan disbursements to the actual stages of construction. This means many reputable banks no longer offer pure "no EMI till possession" schemes, or they are structured with stricter conditions. While Housing Finance Companies (HFCs) might still offer variations, buyers must exercise extreme caution.
  4. Legal Battles: In cases of builder default or project abandonment, buyers often find themselves embroiled in lengthy and expensive legal battles with developers and sometimes even with banks. The Supreme Court has intervened in cases where homebuyers were trapped in such schemes, ordering relief and even CBI probes into alleged collusion.
  5. Lock-in Periods: Some agreements might include lock-in clauses, preventing you from selling the property during the construction phase without incurring heavy penalties. This limits your financial flexibility if your circumstances change.

Tax Benefits on Home Loan Interest (Pre-Possession)

Even though you aren't paying the EMIs during the "no EMI till possession" period, interest is still accruing on your loan. The good news is that this "pre-construction interest" or "pre-EMI interest" can be claimed as a deduction under Section 24(b) of the Income Tax Act, 1961.

Here's how it works:

  • Deduction Post-Possession: You cannot claim this interest during the construction phase. The total pre-construction interest accumulated until the end of the financial year immediately preceding the year of completion of construction can be claimed as a deduction.
  • Five Equal Instalments: This accumulated pre-construction interest is then allowed as a deduction in five equal annual instalments, starting from the financial year in which you take possession of the property.
  • Overall Limit: This deduction is part of the overall limit under Section 24(b), which is ₹2 lakh per financial year for a self-occupied property. For a let-out property, there is no upper limit on the interest deduction, subject to certain set-off rules.
  • New Tax Regime: It's important to note that if you opt for the new tax regime, you generally cannot claim the deduction for pre-construction interest for self-occupied property. However, it can still be claimed for let-out property even under the new regime.
  • Principal Repayment: The principal amount repaid on your home loan is eligible for deduction under Section 80C, up to a limit of ₹1.5 lakh per financial year, but this applies only after you start paying full EMIs post-possession.

Always keep detailed records of all interest certificates from your lender to correctly calculate and claim these deductions.

A Real Example

Let's consider Anjali, a 30-year-old software engineer in Bengaluru, who decides to buy an under-construction flat.

  • Property Value: ₹80 lakhs
  • Home Loan Amount: ₹64 lakhs (80% of property value)
  • Interest Rate: 9% p.a.
  • Loan Tenure: 20 years
  • Construction Period (promised): 3 years
  • Scheme: "No EMI till possession" for 3 years, where the builder pays the interest.

Scenario 1: Ideal Case (Builder delivers on time)

  1. Year 1-3 (Construction Phase): Anjali takes a loan of ₹64 lakhs. For three years, the builder pays the interest on the disbursed loan amount. Let's assume the bank disburses the full ₹64 lakhs upfront.

    2. Monthly interest on ₹64 lakhs at 9% p.a. is (₹64,00,000 0.09) / 12 = ₹48,000. Total interest paid by builder over 3 years = ₹48,000 36 months = ₹17,28,000. * Anjali pays ₹0 in EMIs during this period. She continues to pay her rent of ₹25,000 per month. * Pre-construction interest for tax purposes: ₹17,28,000.

  2. Year 4 Onwards (Post-Possession): Anjali gets possession. Her full EMIs (principal + interest) start.

    3. * EMI for ₹64 lakhs at 9% for 20 years is approximately ₹57,580. * Tax Benefit: From Year 4, Anjali can claim ₹17,28,000 / 5 = ₹3,45,600 as pre-construction interest deduction each year for five years, subject to the ₹2 lakh limit under Section 24(b) for self-occupied property. She can also claim current year's interest (up to ₹2 lakh) and principal under 80C. Hidden Cost:* If the property's base price was inflated by, say, ₹10 lakhs due to the scheme, Anjali effectively paid an extra ₹10 lakhs for the "no EMI" benefit.

Scenario 2: Common Case (Project Delayed by 1 year)

  1. Year 1-3 (Construction Phase): Same as above. Builder pays interest for 3 years.
  2. Year 4 (Delay): The project is delayed, and the builder's subvention period ends. Anjali still doesn't have possession.

    3. * The builder stops paying the interest. * Anjali now has to start paying the full EMI of ₹57,580 per month for a home she doesn't possess. * She also continues to pay her rent of ₹25,000. * Total monthly outflow for Anjali: ₹57,580 (EMI) + ₹25,000 (Rent) = ₹82,580. This is a significant financial strain. * If Anjali fails to pay, her CIBIL score will be impacted.

  3. Year 5 Onwards (Possession): Anjali finally gets possession. Her financial burden eases, but she has endured a year of double payments. The tax benefits would kick in from this year, but the financial stress of the delay remains.

This example highlights that while the immediate benefit of "no EMI" is attractive, the risks associated with delays and the potential for higher overall costs can outweigh the advantages.

What to do this week

  1. Read the Fine Print Thoroughly: Before signing any document, meticulously go through the tripartite agreement between you, the bank, and the builder. Understand the exact duration for which the builder will pay interest, what happens if there are delays, and any lock-in clauses.
  2. Assess Your True Affordability: Don't be swayed by the "no EMI" offer to buy a property beyond your actual means. Calculate what your full EMI will be and ensure you can comfortably afford it along with your other expenses, even if the project is delayed.
  3. Research the Developer's Track Record: Investigate the builder's reputation for project delivery. Check their RERA registration, past project completion timelines, and any existing legal disputes. A reliable builder is crucial for such schemes.
  4. Compare Property Prices: Research similar properties in the same locality that are not under a "no EMI" scheme. This will help you determine if the "no EMI" property is priced higher, effectively nullifying the benefit.
  5. Seek Independent Financial and Legal Advice: Consult a financial advisor and a real estate lawyer who can review the agreement, explain the risks, and help you understand the long-term financial and legal implications before committing.

FAQ

What is a subvention scheme?

A subvention scheme is another name for a "no EMI till possession" scheme. It's a tripartite agreement where the builder pays the interest on your home loan during the construction period, giving you a payment holiday until you receive possession of the property.

Is 'no EMI till possession' truly interest-free?

No, it is not interest-free. Interest is still accruing on your home loan. It's just that the builder is undertaking to pay that interest component on your behalf for a specified period. You are still the borrower, and the loan is in your name.

What happens if the builder defaults on payments?

If the builder defaults on paying the interest, the legal liability falls entirely on you, the homebuyer. The bank will demand payments from you, and your CIBIL score will be negatively impacted if you fail to pay.

Can I claim tax benefits on the interest paid by the builder?

Yes, the interest paid by the builder on your behalf during the pre-possession period (pre-construction interest) can be claimed as a deduction under Section 24(b) of the Income Tax Act. However, this deduction can only be claimed in five equal annual instalments starting from the financial year of possession, subject to the overall limit of ₹2 lakh for self-occupied property.

Has the RBI banned these schemes?

The RBI has not issued an outright ban on all subvention schemes. However, it has issued strict warnings and guidelines to banks, discouraging pure subvention schemes where builders receive large upfront disbursements without linking them to construction progress. Banks are advised to exercise due diligence and ensure disbursements are commensurate with the actual stages of construction.

What are the main risks for homebuyers?

The primary risks include project delays (leading to you paying both rent and EMIs), builder defaults (impacting your CIBIL score), inflated property prices, and limited loan flexibility.

Should NRIs consider 'no EMI till possession' schemes?

NRIs face similar risks to resident Indians, if not more, due to distance and potential difficulties in monitoring project progress or resolving disputes. While the temporary relief might seem attractive, the inherent risks of project delays and builder defaults make it a high-risk proposition that requires even greater due diligence.
