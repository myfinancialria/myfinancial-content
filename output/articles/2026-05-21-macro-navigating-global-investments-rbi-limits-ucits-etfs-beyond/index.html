Navigating Global Investments: RBI Limits, UCITS ETFs & Beyond

TL;DR

  • Indian investors are increasingly looking to diversify their portfolios globally beyond domestic options.
  • The Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) permits resident Indians to send up to USD 250,000 abroad per financial year for various purposes, including investments.
  • Indian mutual funds recently paused fresh inflows into their international feeder funds due to approaching aggregate LRS limits, prompting investors to seek alternatives.
  • UCITS (Undertakings for the Collective Investment in Transferable Securities) ETFs, regulated in Europe, offer a transparent and diversified route to global markets, accessible via direct brokerage accounts.
  • Understanding the LRS limits, tax implications, and available investment avenues is crucial for effective global portfolio diversification.

What this means in plain terms

For a long time, most Indian investors kept their money within India, primarily in local stocks, mutual funds, real estate, and fixed deposits. However, with growing awareness and the desire to reduce risk by not putting all eggs in one basket, many are now looking at investment opportunities outside India. This means buying shares of international companies, investing in global bonds, or participating in overseas real estate markets.

This shift allows investors to tap into growth stories in developed markets like the US or Europe, or emerging economies beyond India. It also helps protect wealth from domestic economic fluctuations, currency risks, and specific market downturns. However, investing globally isn't as straightforward as buying a local mutual fund; it involves navigating specific regulations set by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and understanding different tax rules.

Understanding the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS)

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) is a facility provided by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) that allows resident individuals to freely remit up to USD 250,000 (or its equivalent in other foreign currencies) abroad per financial year. This limit applies to a wide range of current and capital account transactions, including overseas education, medical treatment, gifts, travel, and crucially, overseas investments.

For an Indian resident, any investment made directly into foreign stocks, bonds, or funds through an international brokerage account falls under this LRS limit. The USD 250,000 is a cumulative limit for all remittances made by an individual in a financial year (April 1st to March 31st). It's important to track all remittances made under LRS to avoid exceeding this ceiling. While the scheme aims to liberalise foreign exchange transactions, it also ensures a structured approach to capital outflow from India. You can find detailed guidelines on the RBI website (https://rbi.org.in).

How LRS impacts global investing

When you transfer funds from your Indian bank account to an international brokerage account to invest in global stocks or ETFs, this transaction is counted towards your annual USD 250,000 LRS limit. This means that if you send USD 50,000 for investments, you would have USD 200,000 remaining for other LRS-permitted activities in that financial year. Exceeding this limit without specific RBI permission is not allowed. Financial institutions facilitating these remittances are responsible for ensuring compliance with the LRS guidelines.

The Rise and Retreat of Indian Mutual Funds in Global Investing

For many years, the most accessible route for Indian residents to invest globally was through international feeder funds offered by Indian Asset Management Companies (AMCs). These funds would collect money from Indian investors and then invest it into an underlying global fund (often a US-domiciled ETF or a fund managed by their international partners). This offered a convenient way to diversify globally without the hassle of opening international brokerage accounts or dealing with direct foreign exchange transfers.

These feeder funds gained significant popularity, especially during periods when US technology stocks showed robust growth. They allowed investors to participate in global markets with investments as small as ₹500 via Systematic Investment Plans (SIPs). The aggregate limit for Indian AMCs to invest overseas, however, is capped at USD 7 billion by SEBI (https://sebi.gov.in), with an additional USD 1 billion specifically for investing in overseas ETFs.

The recent pause on fresh inflows

In early 2022, and again in late 2023, many Indian mutual funds paused fresh subscriptions and SIPs into their international feeder funds. This decision was primarily driven by the AMCs approaching or hitting the aggregate overseas investment limits set by SEBI. When the collective investments made by all Indian AMCs reach these limits, they are temporarily prevented from accepting new money into their international funds. This pause highlighted the limitations of relying solely on the mutual fund route for global diversification and pushed investors to explore direct investment options under the LRS. The Association of Mutual Funds in India (AMFI) often issues advisories regarding such developments (https://amfiindia.com).

UCITS ETFs: A New Avenue for Global Diversification

With the restrictions on Indian international mutual funds, many investors are now exploring direct investment options, and UCITS ETFs have emerged as a prominent alternative. UCITS stands for Undertakings for the Collective Investment in Transferable Securities, a regulatory framework established by the European Union (EU). UCITS funds are highly regulated, transparent, and designed to protect investors, making them a popular choice globally.

Why UCITS ETFs are attractive

  1. Diversification: UCITS ETFs offer exposure to a wide range of global markets, sectors, and asset classes, from US equities (like S&P 500 or Nasdaq 100) to European bonds, emerging markets, and specific themes like clean energy or artificial intelligence.
  2. Regulatory Oversight: Being regulated under the EU framework, UCITS funds adhere to strict rules regarding diversification, liquidity, and investor protection, providing a layer of security.
  3. Cost-Efficiency: Like other ETFs, UCITS ETFs generally have lower expense ratios compared to actively managed funds, making them a cost-effective way to gain broad market exposure.
  4. Tax Efficiency (for some): For Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) residing in countries with favourable tax treaties with Ireland or Luxembourg (common domiciles for UCITS funds), there can be tax advantages concerning dividend withholding tax compared to US-domiciled ETFs. For Indian residents, the tax treatment is as per Indian tax laws, but the underlying structure is robust.
  5. Accessibility: Indian residents can access UCITS ETFs by opening an account with an international brokerage firm that facilitates LRS remittances. This allows direct ownership of the ETF units.

Accessing UCITS ETFs for Indian Residents

To invest in UCITS ETFs, an Indian resident needs to open a trading and demat account with an international brokerage firm that supports Indian clients and allows remittances under the LRS. Once the account is set up, funds can be transferred from an Indian bank account to the international brokerage account, up to the USD 250,000 LRS limit per financial year. These funds are then used to purchase UCITS ETFs listed on European exchanges (e.g., London Stock Exchange, Euronext Amsterdam). It's crucial to choose a reputable broker and understand their fee structure, including foreign exchange conversion charges and trading commissions.

Taxation of Global Investments for Indian Residents

Understanding the tax implications of global investments is critical for Indian residents. The income generated from these investments is taxable in India as per the Income Tax Act, 1961 (https://incometax.gov.in).

Capital Gains Tax

  • Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG): If you sell your global investments (e.g., UCITS ETFs, foreign stocks) within 24 months of purchase, any profit is considered STCG. This gain is added to your total income and taxed at your applicable income tax slab rate.
  • Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG): If you sell your global investments after holding them for more than 24 months, any profit is considered LTCG. This gain is taxed at a flat rate of 20% after indexation benefit. Indexation adjusts the purchase price for inflation, thereby reducing the taxable gain.

Dividend Income

Dividends received from global investments are fully taxable in India at your applicable income tax slab rate. These dividends might also be subject to withholding tax in the source country (e.g., Ireland for UCITS funds, or the US for US-domiciled stocks). India has Double Taxation Avoidance Agreements (DTAAs) with many countries, which allow you to claim a credit for the tax already paid abroad against your Indian tax liability, preventing double taxation.

Tax Collected at Source (TCS) on LRS Remittances

As of October 1, 2023, a Tax Collected at Source (TCS) of 20% applies to all remittances made under LRS for purposes other than education or medical treatment, if the aggregate amount exceeds ₹7 lakh in a financial year. This means when you remit funds for global investments exceeding ₹7 lakh, your bank will collect 20% of the amount above ₹7 lakh as TCS. It's important to understand that TCS is not an additional tax; it's an advance tax. You can claim this TCS as a credit against your total tax liability when filing your Income Tax Return (ITR). If your total tax liability is less than the TCS collected, you can claim a refund.

A real example

Meet Rahul, 38, ₹28L CTC, Bengaluru. Rahul wants to diversify his portfolio beyond Indian equities and decides to allocate a portion of his savings to global markets. He plans to invest ₹20 lakh (approximately USD 24,000, assuming ₹83/USD) in UCITS ETFs for long-term growth.

Financial Year 2024-25 Plan:

  1. Initial Remittance: Rahul decides to make an initial remittance of ₹10 lakh (approx. USD 12,000) to his international brokerage account in April 2024.

    2. * Since this amount is above ₹7 lakh, his bank will collect TCS. * Amount exceeding ₹7 lakh = ₹10,00,000 - ₹7,00,000 = ₹3,00,000. * TCS collected = 20% of ₹3,00,000 = ₹60,000. * Total debited from Rahul's Indian account = ₹10,00,000 (remittance) + ₹60,000 (TCS) = ₹10,60,000. * The international brokerage account receives USD 12,000. * Rahul's remaining LRS limit for the financial year is USD 250,000 - USD 12,000 = USD 238,000.

  1. Second Remittance: In September 2024, Rahul plans another remittance of ₹10 lakh (approx. USD 12,000).

    2. This remittance is also above ₹7 lakh in aggregate* for the financial year. * Total remittance so far = ₹10 lakh + ₹10 lakh = ₹20 lakh. * Total amount exceeding ₹7 lakh = ₹20,00,000 - ₹7,00,000 = ₹13,00,000. TCS to be collected on this remittance: The bank will consider the cumulative* remittances. Since ₹3 lakh was already subject to TCS, the current remittance adds another ₹10 lakh to the taxable base above ₹7 lakh. * TCS collected = 20% of ₹10,00,000 = ₹2,00,000. * Total debited from Rahul's Indian account = ₹10,00,000 (remittance) + ₹2,00,000 (TCS) = ₹12,00,000. * The international brokerage account receives another USD 12,000. * Rahul's total LRS usage is now USD 24,000. Total TCS collected is ₹60,000 + ₹2,00,000 = ₹2,60,000.

  1. Investment: Rahul uses the USD 24,000 to buy units of a UCITS ETF tracking the S&P 500 on a European exchange.
  1. Tax Filing: When Rahul files his ITR for AY 2025-26, he will declare the ₹2,60,000 TCS as an advance tax paid. This amount will be adjusted against his total tax liability for the year. If his tax liability is, say, ₹8,00,000, he will effectively only need to pay ₹5,40,000 (₹8,00,000 - ₹2,60,000). If his tax liability is less than ₹2,60,000, he can claim the excess as a refund.

This example illustrates the practical steps and financial considerations, especially regarding LRS limits and TCS, for an Indian resident investing directly in global markets.

What to do this week

  1. Review your current portfolio: Spend 30 minutes to understand what percentage of your investments are currently in Indian assets versus global assets. This helps assess your current diversification level.
  2. Research international brokers: Dedicate 30 minutes to identify 2-3 reputable international brokerage firms that cater to Indian residents for direct global investments. Check their account opening requirements, fee structures, and supported investment products (e.g., UCITS ETFs).
  3. Understand LRS limits: Reconfirm your LRS usage for the current financial year by checking with your bank. Ensure you have a clear picture of your remaining limit before planning any new global remittances. This can be done by reviewing bank statements for past international transfers.
  4. Read up on UCITS ETFs: Spend 30 minutes researching what UCITS ETFs are, their benefits, and how they differ from other global investment vehicles. Websites of major fund houses (e.g., BlackRock, Vanguard) often have educational resources.

FAQ

What is the RBI LRS limit for global investments?

The RBI's Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) allows resident individuals to remit up to USD 250,000 per financial year for various purposes, including direct global investments. This is a cumulative limit across all remittances.

Why did Indian mutual funds stop accepting money for international funds?

Indian mutual funds paused fresh inflows into their international feeder funds because they were nearing or had hit the aggregate overseas investment limits (USD 7 billion + USD 1 billion for ETFs) set by SEBI for all AMCs combined.

What are UCITS ETFs and how do they benefit Indian investors?

UCITS ETFs are investment funds regulated under strict EU guidelines, offering diversification, transparency, and often lower costs. For Indian investors, they provide a regulated and accessible way to invest directly in global markets, especially given the restrictions on Indian international mutual funds.

How are global investment gains taxed in India?

Short-term capital gains (investments held for ≤ 24 months) are added to your income and taxed at slab rates. Long-term capital gains (investments held for > 24 months) are taxed at 20% with indexation benefit. Dividends are taxed at your slab rate, with potential for DTAA credit for foreign taxes paid.

Is the 20% TCS an additional tax on global investments?

No, the 20% Tax Collected at Source (TCS) on LRS remittances above ₹7 lakh for investments is an advance tax, not an additional tax. It can be claimed as a credit against your total tax liability when filing your Income Tax Return.

Can NRIs invest in UCITS ETFs?

Yes, Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) can typically invest in UCITS ETFs through international brokerage accounts, often without being subject to the RBI's LRS limits, as LRS applies to resident individuals. However, they need to comply with regulations of their country of residence.
