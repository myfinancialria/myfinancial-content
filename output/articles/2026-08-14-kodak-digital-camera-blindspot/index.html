Business Mavericks · 2026-08-14

In a dimly lit laboratory at Eastman Kodak in Rochester, New York, a young electrical engineer named Steven Sasson carefully assembled a contraption in December 1975. It was an eight-pound, toaster-sized device, a peculiar amalgam of a Super 8 movie camera lens, an experimental charge-coupled device (CCD) chip, and a portable cassette tape recorder. Powered by 16 AA batteries, it was far from elegant, painted blue simply because a can of that color was on hand. When Sasson finally pointed his invention at a lab assistant, it took 23 seconds to record a single, grainy, black-and-white image onto the cassette tape. The resolution was a minuscule 0.01 megapixels, or 100x100 pixels.

The moment was, in hindsight, a pivot point for an entire industry. Sasson proudly presented his prototype to Kodak executives, expecting excitement. Instead, he was met with a lukewarm response, famously summarized as "That's cute — but don't tell anyone about it". Another account noted a senior executive's blunt assessment: "It needs work!". This invention, a harbinger of the digital age, was not celebrated, but quietly suppressed. For nearly two decades, Kodak, the undisputed king of photography, chose to bury the very technology that would eventually lead to its downfall.

The World Before

To understand Kodak's decision, one must appreciate its monolithic dominance. For over a century, the name Kodak was synonymous with photography itself. Founded by George Eastman in 1888, the company democratized image-making with its simple slogan, "You press the button, we do the rest". This wasn't just about selling cameras; it was about an entire ecosystem. Kodak’s business model was ingeniously built on the "razor and blades" strategy: sell inexpensive cameras and make substantial profits from the recurring sales of film, chemicals, and printing paper.

By the mid-1970s, Kodak was an economic powerhouse. In 1976, it held an astonishing 90% market share in the US film market and 85% of camera sales. Its gross margins on film were exceptionally high, nearing 70%. The company’s annual sales surpassed $10 billion by 1981, and its market capitalization reached $28 billion at its peak in the mid-1990s, employing over 140,000 people. Kodak moments – those cherished memories captured on yellow-boxed film – were woven into the fabric of American life and culture.

In this golden era, the idea of a camera that didn't require film, processing, or chemicals was not just foreign; it was an existential threat to Kodak’s incredibly lucrative core business. Digital photography seemed inferior, expensive, and impractical, producing low-resolution images that could only be viewed on a television set. Executives feared that embracing this nascent technology would cannibalize their immensely profitable film sales, undermining the very foundation of their empire.

The Crack

Steven Sasson, a 24-year-old electrical engineer, had only joined Kodak two years prior when his supervisor gave him a charge-coupled device (CCD) and the vague instruction to "see what he could do" with it. With the help of skilled technicians Bob DeYager and Jim Schueckler, Sasson spent less than a year creating the world's first self-contained digital camera.

The prototype was rudimentary but functional. It used a Fairchild CCD sensor to convert light into electronic signals. These signals were then digitized by an analog-to-digital converter and stored on a Philips-style data cassette. Each black-and-white image, comprising just 10,000 pixels, took 23 seconds to record onto the tape. The camera could store up to 30 photographs, a number deliberately chosen to fall between the common 24 and 36 exposures of a 35mm film roll. In 1977, Kodak even filed a patent application for Sasson's invention, which was granted in 1978 as U.S. Patent No. 4,131,919.

Despite this internal breakthrough and subsequent patent, Kodak chose a path of prolonged hesitation. The company’s leadership, acutely aware of the potential disruption, continuously tracked digital advancements but remained locked into its existing, highly profitable film business model. This internal struggle led to the suppression of the technology for approximately 18 years. While Kodak’s engineers, including Sasson himself, continued to work on digital imaging, even developing the first modern digital single-lens reflex (SLR) camera in 1989, these projects remained largely sidelined.

Kodak’s first commercial digital SLR camera, the DCS 100, finally launched in 1991, nearly two decades after Sasson’s invention. It was a groundbreaking professional product, but its high cost and niche market showed Kodak's continued reluctance to fully commit to a consumer-focused digital future. The company’s actions demonstrated a strategic failure to evolve its business model, prioritizing value capture from its existing film markets over value creation in new digital ones.

What Happened Next

The consequences of Kodak's delayed embrace of digital were catastrophic. While the company held a promising 27% market share in the US digital camera market in 1999, this quickly eroded. By 2003, it had fallen to 15%, and by 2010, Kodak’s share in digital camera sales had plummeted to a mere 7%. Companies like Sony, Canon, and Nikon, which invested aggressively in digital technology, seized the burgeoning market. The rise of smartphones in the late 2000s delivered another devastating blow, making standalone digital cameras increasingly redundant for the average consumer.

Kodak's revenues began a precipitous decline. From $13.3 billion in 2003, annual revenue fell to approximately $6 billion by 2011. The workforce was drastically cut, shrinking from about 64,000 employees in 2003 to around 17,000 in 2011. The company struggled to pivot, even after declaring that over half its revenues in 2005 were from digital products and services. However, profits remained unstable, swinging wildly between gains and losses.

On January 19, 2012, after years of struggle, Eastman Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. At the time, the company listed assets of $5.1 billion against liabilities of $6.75 billion. The once-invaluable digital imaging patent portfolio, which could have been Kodak's saving grace and was once valued at around $2 billion, ultimately sold for $525 million. Kodak exited bankruptcy in September 2013, a shadow of its former self, having shed its consumer photography business and pivoted to commercial printing and enterprise services.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Innovation isn't just invention; it's execution and belief: Kodak invented the digital camera and patented it, demonstrating its technical prowess. However, the real failure wasn't in invention but in the belief and follow-through necessary to commercialize and aggressively lead the market it had created. A brilliant invention buried internally is a lost opportunity.
  1. Protecting the present can kill the future: Kodak’s leadership made a conscious decision to protect its highly profitable film business, fearing cannibalization from digital. This short-sighted view prevented them from disrupting themselves, leaving the door open for competitors to seize the future. The allure of current high margins can blind a company to inevitable shifts.
  1. Understand your true business, not just your product: Kodak defined itself as a "film business" rather than a "storytelling" or "memory-making" business. This narrow self-perception kept them from seeing digital photography as an evolution of their core mission, instead viewing it as a threat. Companies must continuously re-evaluate their fundamental purpose in a changing world.
  1. The pace of disruption can be underestimated: While Kodak was aware of digital technology, it underestimated the speed and scale at which consumers would abandon film for the convenience of digital. Even if it had transitioned earlier, the shift from high-margin film to lower-margin digital products still presented a difficult challenge. The cost of delay amplified this problem.
