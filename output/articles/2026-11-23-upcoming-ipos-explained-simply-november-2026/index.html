First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local shop that has been doing very well. Now, the owners want to expand, maybe open new branches or buy new equipment. To get the money for this expansion, they decide to sell a small part of their shop's ownership to the public.

This act of a private company selling its shares for the first time to the public is called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). It's the moment a company transitions from being privately owned to publicly owned. When you buy shares in an IPO, you are buying them directly from the company (or its existing owners) in what's known as the primary market. Once these shares are successfully sold and listed on a stock exchange like NSE or BSE, they begin listing – meaning they can be traded by anyone in the open market.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, there are a few key pieces of information that help you understand what's on offer:

  1. Price Band: This is the range of prices within which investors can bid for the shares. For example, if a price band is ₹129 to ₹136, you can bid at any price within this range. The company usually sets a "cut-off price" within this band after the bidding closes, typically at the higher end, based on demand.
  2. Lot Size: This is the minimum number of shares an investor must apply for. You cannot apply for just one share; you have to apply for shares in multiples of the lot size. For example, if the lot size is 100 shares, you must bid for 100, 200, 300 shares, and so on.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is the smallest amount of money you need to invest in an IPO. You calculate it by multiplying the lot size by the lower end of the price band. For instance, if the lot size is 100 shares and the lower price band is ₹129, your minimum investment would be ₹12,900 (100 shares * ₹129).
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total number of shares (or the total value of shares) the company is offering to the public through the IPO. A larger issue size means more shares are available.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS): An IPO can be a combination of these two.

    6. * Fresh Issue: When a company issues new shares to the public. The money raised from these new shares goes directly to the company for its business operations, expansion, or debt repayment. * Offer-for-Sale (OFS): When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell their shares to the public. The money raised from an OFS goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself.

  6. RHP/DRHP: These stand for Red Herring Prospectus and Draft Red Herring Prospectus. These are legal documents that companies file with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) before an IPO. They contain all the crucial information about the company, its financials, risks, management, and the IPO details. Reading the RHP helps you understand the company thoroughly.

The IPO process, step by step

Here’s how an IPO typically unfolds:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files its Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI. This document contains all the initial details about the company and its IPO plans.
  2. SEBI Approval: SEBI reviews the DRHP to ensure all regulations are met and investor interests are protected. Once approved, the company files the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP).
  3. Price Band & Dates Announced: The company, along with its investment bankers, announces the IPO's price band, lot size, and the dates when the IPO will open and close for applications.
  4. Bidding (Application): During the application window, investors can bid for shares. Different categories of investors participate:

    5. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Ordinary investors like you and me, who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs): Investors who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large financial institutions like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.

  5. Allotment: After the IPO closes, if the demand for shares is higher than the supply (called oversubscription), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors. If the IPO is undersubscribed, all applicants get shares.
  6. Listing: Finally, the shares are listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and/or BSE) on a specified date. Once listed, they can be bought and sold freely in the secondary market (the regular stock market).

A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a school fete stall. The school wants to raise money for new sports equipment. So, they decide to sell "ownership tickets" in their new venture (say, a school garden project). They announce how many tickets are available, what price range they're selling them for, and when you can buy them. You decide how many tickets you want and pay for them. If too many people want tickets, they might draw names from a hat (like a lottery) to decide who gets them. Once the tickets are distributed, people can then trade them among themselves if they wish.

Things people get confused about

Here are some common misunderstandings about IPOs:

  • Myth: Listing gain is guaranteed in every IPO.

    • * Fact: While some IPOs do list at a higher price than their issue price, this is not guaranteed. Share prices can go up or down on listing day, just like any other stock in the market.

  • Myth: Applying for more applications (using multiple PAN cards) or bidding for a very high number of lots in the retail category increases your chances of allotment.

    • * Fact: For retail investors, only one application per PAN card is considered. If an IPO is oversubscribed, allotment is typically done through a lottery system, where each valid retail application has an equal chance, regardless of the number of lots applied for (as long as it's within the retail limit).

  • Myth: IPOs are always a "safe" investment because they are new companies.

    • * Fact: All investments in the stock market carry risks, and IPOs are no exception. Companies coming out with an IPO are often at an early stage of public scrutiny, and their future performance can be uncertain. It's important to understand the business and its risks.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility or through your demat account provider/broker's online platform. The funds are blocked in your bank account and only debited if you are allotted shares.

What is a lot size?

The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for in an IPO. It ensures that shares are distributed in manageable blocks.

What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME IPOs are for smaller companies, listed on dedicated SME platforms (like NSE Emerge or BSE SME), with slightly different regulatory requirements and trading rules.

What is oversubscription?

Oversubscription happens when the total demand for shares in an IPO (the number of shares applied for) is greater than the total number of shares offered by the company.

Can I sell my shares on the listing day?

Yes, once your allotted shares are credited to your demat account and the company's shares start trading on the stock exchange, you are free to sell them on the listing day itself.
