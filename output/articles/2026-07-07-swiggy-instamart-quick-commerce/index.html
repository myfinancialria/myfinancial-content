Business Mavericks · 2026-07-07

On a sweltering August afternoon in 2020, as India grappled with the unprecedented restrictions of the COVID-19 pandemic, Sriharsha Majety, co-founder and CEO of Swiggy, was preparing to unveil a bold gamble. His food delivery giant, synonymous with bringing hot meals to millions, was about to step into a new, complex, and notoriously unforgiving arena: instant grocery. This wasn't just another service; it was Instamart, and it represented a monumental pivot, a conscious defiance of the prevailing asset-light startup playbook, that would redefine Swiggy's future and establish a new benchmark for quick commerce in India. Majety himself had a clear conviction: the grocery business in India held a potential 25 times larger than the restaurant industry, anticipating higher growth rates than food delivery itself.

The World Before

For years, the Indian startup ecosystem had championed the "asset-light" model. Companies aimed to be platforms, connecting buyers and sellers without owning the underlying inventory or infrastructure. This strategy minimized capital expenditure and focused on rapid scaling through network effects. In food delivery, Swiggy and its rival Zomato had perfected this, becoming a duopoly by leveraging vast networks of delivery partners and restaurant tie-ups.

Grocery delivery, however, was a different beast. Prior attempts by various players had often stumbled, battling logistical nightmares in congested urban landscapes, high operational costs, and the delicate dance of managing perishable inventory. Delivering a hot biryani was one thing; ensuring fresh vegetables and milk arrived within minutes, consistently, was another. Traditional e-grocery platforms typically offered scheduled deliveries, often taking hours or even a day, unable to meet the immediate, impulse-driven needs of urban consumers. The idea of a company building out its own network of mini-warehouses, or "dark stores" — dedicated retail fulfillment centers optimized solely for online order picking and rapid dispatch, inaccessible to walk-in customers — was seen by many as capital-intensive, risky, and a stark departure from the lean startup mantra.

The Crack

The pandemic, however, provided an unexpected catalyst. Lockdowns and safety concerns around physical store visits accelerated online grocery adoption, creating a sudden, urgent demand for doorstep delivery of essentials. Sensing this seismic shift, Majety and his team launched Swiggy Instamart in August 2020. It wasn't just a side project; it was a deliberate, strategic move to capture this emerging market.

Instamart's core was its network of dark stores. These weren't sprawling warehouses on city outskirts but smaller, densely stocked facilities strategically placed within crowded urban neighborhoods. Each dark store, typically ranging from 2,500 to 4,000 square feet, was designed for rapid order picking, with inventory arranged for operational efficiency, not customer browsing. They stocked thousands of fast-moving products, from daily essentials and fresh produce to snacks and even electronics, tailored to local demand patterns.

The genius lay in the synergy. Swiggy didn't start from scratch. It leveraged its pre-existing, robust logistics network of 5.4 lakh delivery partners and a massive customer base already accustomed to on-demand convenience. This gave Instamart a significant head start, reducing customer acquisition costs and allowing for rapid expansion. A customer who trusted Swiggy for their food was more likely to trust it for their groceries. The initial promise was audacious: groceries delivered in 10-15 minutes.

Swiggy invested heavily, defying the conventional wisdom of being asset-light. Establishing and stocking each dark store cost approximately ₹50-70 lakh ($60,000-$85,000). But the company committed. From operating in 28 cities by April 2022, Instamart expanded aggressively, reaching 100 cities by April 2025 and covering over 120 cities by November 2025. By March 2025, Instamart operated over 1,021 dark stores across the country, a testament to its rapid infrastructure build-out.

The product catalog also expanded beyond mere groceries. By Q2 FY26, non-grocery items like electronics, beauty products, and toys accounted for 26% of Instamart's total sales mix, showcasing a strategic diversification to increase average order values and attract a wider consumer base. This was not just about speed; it was about convenience across a broader spectrum of needs.

What Happened Next

The pivot was nothing short of transformative. By November 2024, Instamart, initially a side experiment, contributed approximately 40% of Swiggy's consolidated gross order value (GOV), a significant leap from the 20% mentioned in the initial context, highlighting its rapid ascent. This cemented its position as a crucial moat against competitors like Zomato, which later acquired Blinkit, and new entrants like Zepto.

Instamart’s financial performance reflected this growth, albeit with significant investment. In Q3 FY26 (December 2025), Instamart reported a Gross Order Value of ₹7,938 crore, marking a 103.2% year-on-year increase, with revenue standing at ₹1,016 crore. The business, however, was still in investment mode, with adjusted EBITDA losses widening to ₹908 crore in the same quarter. Swiggy, however, aimed to bring Instamart to contribution breakeven by Q1 FY27 (March-June 2026), demonstrating a clear path towards profitability.

Swiggy, as a whole, went public in November 2024 with an Initial Public Offering (IPO) at ₹390 per share, valuing the company at $11.3 billion. This market validation underscored the success of its multi-vertical strategy, with Instamart playing a pivotal role.

While Instamart's absolute growth was impressive, the quick commerce market quickly became a battleground. By mid-2025, Instamart held an estimated 23-25% market share in India's quick commerce sector, competing fiercely with Blinkit (around 44-46%) and Zepto (around 29-30%). By FY26, Instamart's share of total orders among the top three players had declined to 20.9% from 34.3% in FY24, indicating that while it continued to grow, Blinkit and Zepto were capturing a larger share of the incremental market growth. This intense competition meant Swiggy had to decide between aggressive discounting to gain market share or focusing on sustainable growth with loyal, profitable users. Majety indicated a preference for the latter, focusing on differentiation rather than simply matching competitor incentives.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Strategic Vertical Integration Can Be a Differentiator: In an era obsessed with asset-light models, Swiggy's decision to invest heavily in a dense network of dark stores was a bold, counter-intuitive move. This asset-heavy approach created a significant operational moat, enabling ultra-fast delivery that competitors struggled to replicate without similar capital commitments.
  2. Leverage Existing Ecosystems for New Ventures: Instamart’s rapid scale was significantly aided by Swiggy's existing brand recognition, extensive delivery fleet, and established customer base. This synergy allowed for lower customer acquisition costs and faster market penetration than a standalone quick commerce startup could achieve.
  3. Timeliness and Adaptability to Market Shifts are Critical: The pandemic fundamentally altered consumer behavior, creating an overnight surge in demand for online groceries. Swiggy's swift launch of Instamart, precisely when this demand peaked, allowed it to capture significant market share and establish an early lead in a nascent category.
  4. Long-Term Vision May Require Short-Term Profit Sacrifices: Building a quick commerce network is inherently capital-intensive and often unprofitable in its early stages. Swiggy's willingness to incur substantial losses for several years demonstrates a commitment to building a category leader, understanding that profitability would follow scale and market dominance.
  5. Diversification Enhances Platform Value: By expanding beyond food into a wide array of grocery and non-grocery items, Swiggy transformed from a single-purpose app into a multi-category convenience platform. This increased user engagement, order frequency, and average order values, making the platform more sticky and valuable to consumers.
