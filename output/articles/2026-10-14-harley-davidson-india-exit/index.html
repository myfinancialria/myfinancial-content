Why Harley-Davidson Exited India in 2020 After 11 Years and 27,000 Bikes Sold

The announcement landed like a gut punch in September 2020. Harley-Davidson, the quintessential American motorcycle icon, declared it was ceasing sales and manufacturing operations in India, drawing a definitive curtain on an 11-year saga in the world's largest two-wheeler market. The news, part of a global restructuring effort dubbed "The Rewire," meant the closure of its Bawal assembly plant in Haryana and the elimination of approximately 70 jobs. It was a stark reversal from the grand ambitions of 2009, when the company first roared into India, promising to cultivate a premium motorcycle culture among a rapidly urbanizing and increasingly affluent population. The dream was potent: the open road, the thrumming V-twin engine, the unmistakable silhouette of a Hog against an Indian sunrise. But the reality proved far more challenging, leading to a quiet retreat for a brand once synonymous with freedom and power.

The World Before

In 2009, India’s two-wheeler market was a behemoth, selling millions of motorcycles and scooters annually. Yet, it was a landscape overwhelmingly dominated by affordable, fuel-efficient commuter bikes, primarily in the 100-150cc segment. Brands like Hero Honda and Bajaj Pulsar reigned supreme, offering practicality and accessibility to the masses. Premium motorcycles, as understood in the West, were a nascent, almost non-existent category. Before Harley's arrival, the idea of a significant market for large, expensive cruisers seemed almost fanciful, given the country's developing road infrastructure and price-sensitive consumer base.

Harley-Davidson, however, saw immense potential. Its entry was driven by the belief that India's rising disposable incomes and burgeoning middle class would eventually fuel demand for luxury lifestyle products. This wasn't merely a business decision; it was a diplomatic one. India's market access for Harley-Davidson motorcycles was famously part of a 2007 "mango diplomacy" deal, where the U.S. agreed to lift a ban on Indian mango imports in exchange.

Despite this high-level agreement, the path was not smooth. Harley-Davidson faced significant hurdles even before selling its first bike. India's import duties on completely built-up (CBU) motorcycles were exorbitant, often exceeding 100%, effectively doubling the retail price. Additionally, the country lacked specific emission standards for motorcycles over 500cc, which initially presented regulatory barriers. Undeterred, Harley-Davidson officially commenced operations in August 2009, opening its first dealership in Hyderabad in July 2010. The initial lineup consisted of 12 models from five motorcycle families, all imported as CBUs, with prices ranging from ₹6.95 lakh to ₹34.95 lakh (ex-showroom).

The Crack

Harley-Davidson's strategy in India began with high hopes but quickly encountered the sharp edges of market reality. Recognizing the prohibitive cost of imports, the company took a significant step in 2011 by establishing an assembly plant in Bawal, Haryana. This facility allowed Harley to import "complete knock-down" (CKD) kits and assemble bikes locally, reducing import duties and making its products relatively more affordable. The Bawal plant wasn't just for the domestic market; it also served as a hub for exports.

The most ambitious move to crack the Indian market came in 2014 with the launch of the Street 750. Priced at a more accessible ₹4.1 lakh (ex-showroom, Delhi), the Street 750 was designed specifically for urban Indian conditions. It featured a liquid-cooled Revolution X V-twin engine, higher ground clearance, and a more manageable size – a stark departure from the brand's traditional heavyweight cruisers. This was Harley's first all-new platform in 14 years, signaling a serious intent to capture a younger, urban demographic. Indeed, the Street 750 quickly became Harley's best-selling model in India.

However, even the Street 750 couldn't turn the tide of declining sales and mounting challenges. Over its entire 11-year tenure, Harley-Davidson sold fewer than 30,000 bikes in India, with approximately 27,000 units sold over a decade. To put this in perspective, its primary competitor in the mid-capacity segment, Royal Enfield, sold more than double that amount every month in India. Harley's annual sales figures tell a stark story: production volume in India plummeted from 11,753 units in FY16 to a mere 4,533 units in FY20, while retail sales dropped from 4,708 units to 2,470 units in the same period. Between April and June 2020, just before the exit, only about 100 Harleys were sold in the country.

Several factors contributed to this fall. Despite the Bawal plant, significant import duties (ranging from 44% to 110%) still applied to many CBU models, keeping prices stratospherically high for the majority of Indian consumers. The inherent design of traditional Harleys—heavy, expensive, and fuel-guzzling—was fundamentally ill-suited for India's congested city traffic, hot climate, and often poor road infrastructure, where 40% of roads were still dirt or gravel. Furthermore, the Bawal plant, while assembling bikes, wasn't a full manufacturing facility, leading to long delays and high costs for spare parts that had to be imported.

The dealership network also struggled. With 35 dealerships across the country, each reportedly requiring an investment of ₹3-4 crore, many were bleeding cash, selling fewer than 70 bikes a year per outlet. The company's global leadership was criticized for focusing on 35-50-year-old riders and relying too heavily on marketing without sufficient product innovation, allowing rivals like BMW, KTM, and Yamaha to introduce lighter, more technologically advanced bikes.

Globally, Harley-Davidson was already facing headwinds, with sales declining for years. The COVID-19 pandemic served as the final catalyst, exacerbating financial pressures and leading CEO Jochen Zeitz to implement "The Rewire" strategy: a plan to reduce the product portfolio by 30% and exit loss-making markets to refocus on core regions like North America, Europe, and parts of Asia Pacific. India, despite its potential, simply wasn't making the cut.

What Happened Next

The official exit in September 2020 was abrupt. Harley-Davidson discontinued its sales and manufacturing operations, closed its Bawal plant, and laid off approximately 70 employees. The move left its 35 dealer partners in India blindsided, with many reportedly exploring legal action due to significant losses and lack of prior communication.

However, Harley-Davidson's story in India wasn't entirely over. Just a month later, in October 2020, the company announced a strategic partnership with Hero MotoCorp, the world's largest manufacturer of motorcycles and scooters by unit volume. This deal was multifaceted: Hero MotoCorp would take over the distribution, sales, and servicing of Harley-Davidson motorcycles in India through both dedicated dealerships and its own extensive network. Crucially, the agreement also included a licensing pact for Hero MotoCorp to develop and sell a range of premium motorcycles under the Harley-Davidson brand name.

This partnership bore its first fruit with the launch of the Harley-Davidson X440 in 2023 (or 2024). Locally manufactured at Hero MotoCorp’s Neemrana facility in Rajasthan, the X440 was positioned as a more accessible entry point to the brand, priced around ₹2.35-2.6 lakh (ex-showroom). Hero also launched its own variant, the Mavrick. This new strategy has shown some positive signs; in the last fiscal year, the X440 and Mavrick combined sold 14,375 units, making Hero the third-largest player in India's 350-500cc segment with an 11% share. The partnership is now expanding to co-develop and build entry-level performance motorcycles and cruisers in India for global sales.

In a more recent development in February 2026, India agreed to scrap import duties on fully-built Harley-Davidson motorcycles with engine capacities exceeding 800cc as part of an interim trade pact with the US. This move could significantly lower prices for high-end imported Harleys, which currently retail between ₹14.5 lakh and ₹45.8 lakh. While it's expected to have a limited impact on overall volumes due to the niche nature of the segment, it could intensify competition at the premium end of the market and signals a more favorable trade environment.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Market Fit Trumps Brand Mystique (Initially): Harley-Davidson arrived with an unparalleled brand legacy, but it underestimated the fundamental differences of the Indian market. Its core product—heavy, expensive cruisers built for open American highways—was a poor fit for India's dense traffic, varied roads, and price-sensitive consumer base. Even attempts at localization like the Street 750, while a step in the right direction, couldn't fully overcome the initial disconnect in product design and positioning.
  2. The Perils of "Assembly" Without Deeper Integration: While setting up the Bawal assembly plant reduced import duties on CKD units, it wasn't a full manufacturing facility. This meant critical components and spare parts still had to be imported, leading to delays, high maintenance costs, and ultimately, a less competitive offering. True localization requires a robust local supply chain and manufacturing ecosystem, not just final assembly.
  3. Dealerships are Partners, Not Just Distributors: The abrupt exit in 2020, without adequate prior notice or compensation to its dealer network, highlighted a critical flaw in partner relations. Dealerships make substantial investments, and their financial health and loyalty are crucial for market penetration and customer service. Alienating them can severely damage a brand's reputation and future re-entry efforts.
  4. Patience Has its Limits, but Strategy Can Evolve: Harley-Davidson spent 11 years trying to make its original model work, pouring significant resources into a market that wasn't responding as anticipated. The "Rewire" strategy acknowledged these failures and led to a pragmatic pivot: partnering with a local giant, Hero MotoCorp. This new approach, focusing on co-development of more market-appropriate models and leveraging Hero's vast distribution, demonstrates that sometimes, exiting a direct operational model is a strategic retreat that can pave the way for a more sustainable, indirect presence.
