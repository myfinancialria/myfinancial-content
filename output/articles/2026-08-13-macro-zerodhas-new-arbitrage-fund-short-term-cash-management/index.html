Zerodha's New Arbitrage Fund: Short-Term Cash Management

TL;DR

  • Zerodha Fund House has launched an arbitrage fund, designed for managing short-term surplus cash.
  • This fund aims to generate returns by exploiting price differences between the cash and futures markets for the same security.
  • It seeks to offer lower volatility compared to pure equity funds, making it suitable for conservative investors.
  • The minimum investment for this New Fund Offer (NFO) starts at ₹5,000.
  • Arbitrage funds are taxed as equity-oriented mutual funds, which can be advantageous for holding periods over one year.

What this means in plain terms

Zerodha Fund House has introduced a new mutual fund category called an arbitrage fund. Think of it as a smart way to park your extra money for a few months or even a year, aiming for returns that are potentially better than a savings account or short-term fixed deposit, but with significantly lower risk than investing directly in the stock market. The fund manager tries to profit from small, temporary price differences of the same stock or index across different market segments – for instance, buying a share in the cash market and simultaneously selling its future contract at a slightly higher price.

This strategy is designed to capture these small, predictable profits repeatedly, leading to steady, albeit moderate, returns. Because the fund takes offsetting positions (buying and selling simultaneously), the overall market risk is substantially reduced. For investors who have a lump sum sitting idle, perhaps for an upcoming expense or just waiting for a better investment opportunity, this fund offers a relatively stable option that also enjoys favourable tax treatment if held for more than a year, similar to equity investments.

Understanding Arbitrage Funds

An arbitrage fund is a type of equity-oriented mutual fund that capitalises on price discrepancies between the cash (spot) market and the derivatives (futures and options) market for the same underlying asset. For example, if a particular stock is trading at ₹100 in the cash market and its one-month future contract is trading at ₹100.50, the fund manager would simultaneously buy the stock in the cash market and sell its future contract. This locks in a profit of ₹0.50 per share, minus transaction costs. When the future contract expires, the prices converge, and the fund profits from this difference.

This strategy is considered low-risk because the fund takes offsetting positions, meaning it is largely insulated from general market movements. If the stock price goes up, the gain in the cash market is offset by a loss in the futures market, and vice-versa. The profit comes from the spread between the cash and futures prices, not from the direction of the market. These spreads typically exist due to factors like interest rates, dividends, and market inefficiencies. The fund continuously looks for such opportunities across various stocks and indices to generate consistent returns.

Why Arbitrage Funds for Short-Term Cash Management?

For investors looking to park surplus funds for a relatively short duration, typically ranging from a few months to a year or slightly more, arbitrage funds present a compelling option. Unlike pure equity funds which are subject to significant market volatility, arbitrage funds aim to provide more stable returns due to their hedged strategy. This makes them suitable for those who prioritise capital preservation and moderate, consistent gains over high-risk, high-reward equity exposure.

Compared to traditional debt instruments like bank fixed deposits (FDs) or ultra-short duration debt funds, arbitrage funds offer a distinct advantage in terms of taxation. Since they are classified as equity-oriented mutual funds (holding at least 65% in equities, even if hedged), gains held for more than one year are treated as Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG) and taxed at 10% on gains exceeding ₹1 lakh in a financial year, without indexation benefit. Short Term Capital Gains (STCG) on units held for less than one year are taxed at a flat rate of 15%. This can be significantly more tax-efficient than debt funds or FDs, where interest income and short-term capital gains are taxed at your marginal income tax slab rate.

Taxation Advantages of Arbitrage Funds

The tax treatment of arbitrage funds is a key differentiator, especially for high-income earners. As per Indian income tax laws, mutual funds are broadly categorised as equity-oriented or non-equity (debt) funds for taxation purposes. Arbitrage funds, by maintaining an average of at least 65% of their corpus in equity and equity-related instruments, qualify as equity-oriented funds.

Here's how their taxation works:

  • Short Term Capital Gains (STCG): If you redeem your units within one year of purchase, any gains are considered STCG and are taxed at a flat rate of 15% (plus cess and surcharge, if applicable).
  • Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG): If you hold your units for more than one year, any gains are treated as LTCG. LTCG from equity-oriented mutual funds are exempt up to ₹1 lakh in a financial year. Gains exceeding ₹1 lakh are taxed at a rate of 10% (plus cess and surcharge, if applicable), without the benefit of indexation.

This contrasts sharply with debt funds or bank FDs. Interest earned on FDs is added to your total income and taxed at your applicable slab rate, which can be as high as 30% for higher income brackets. Similarly, short-term capital gains from debt funds are taxed at your slab rate. Long-term capital gains from debt funds (held for more than 3 years) are taxed at 20% with indexation benefit. For holding periods between one and three years, arbitrage funds often offer a more tax-efficient outcome for those in higher tax brackets.

A real example

Let's consider Anjali, 42, a self-employed professional with an annual income of ₹35 lakhs, based in Mumbai. She has ₹5 lakhs in surplus cash that she anticipates needing in about 15 months for her child's school admission fee. She is currently in the 30% income tax bracket.

Scenario 1: Parking in a Bank Fixed Deposit (FD) If Anjali invests ₹5 lakhs in a bank FD for 15 months, assuming an annual interest rate of 7.0%.

  • Interest earned over 15 months: ₹5,00,000 7.0% (15/12) = ₹43,750.
  • Taxable income from FD: ₹43,750.
  • Tax liability (at 30% slab rate): ₹43,750 * 30% = ₹13,125.
  • Net Post-Tax Return: ₹43,750 - ₹13,125 = ₹30,625.

Scenario 2: Investing in an Arbitrage Fund If Anjali invests ₹5 lakhs in an arbitrage fund for 15 months, assuming an annualised return of 6.5% (post-expense ratio).

  • Gains over 15 months: ₹5,00,000 6.5% (15/12) = ₹40,625.
  • Since the holding period is more than one year, this is considered Long Term Capital Gain (LTCG).
  • LTCG up to ₹1 lakh is exempt.
  • Taxable LTCG: ₹40,625 - ₹0 (since it's below ₹1 lakh exemption) = ₹0.
  • Tax liability: ₹0.
  • Net Post-Tax Return: ₹40,625.

In this example, the arbitrage fund provides Anjali with a significantly higher post-tax return (₹40,625 vs ₹30,625) due to its favourable equity-oriented taxation, even with a slightly lower pre-tax return compared to the FD. This illustrates the tax efficiency for investors in higher income tax brackets, especially for holding periods exceeding one year.

FAQ

What is the primary objective of an arbitrage fund?

The primary objective is to generate returns by exploiting price differentials of the same asset in different markets (cash and derivatives) simultaneously, aiming for low volatility and consistent, moderate returns.

Are arbitrage funds completely risk-free?

No investment is completely risk-free. While arbitrage funds are considered low-risk due to their hedged strategy, they are subject to residual risks such as liquidity risk (difficulty in executing trades at desired prices), expense ratio impact, and the possibility of spreads narrowing significantly.

What is the ideal holding period for an arbitrage fund?

While they are suitable for short-term cash management, holding arbitrage funds for more than one year is generally recommended to benefit from the more favourable Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG) tax treatment applicable to equity-oriented funds.

How do arbitrage funds compare to liquid funds?

Liquid funds invest in very short-term money market instruments and typically offer slightly lower returns than arbitrage funds, but with even lower volatility. Arbitrage funds offer potentially higher post-tax returns for holding periods over one year due to their equity-oriented taxation, which liquid funds do not.

Can NRIs invest in arbitrage funds?

Yes, Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) can generally invest in Indian mutual funds, including arbitrage funds, subject to FEMA regulations and KYC compliance. They should consult their tax advisor regarding taxation in their country of residence.

Is there an exit load for arbitrage funds?

Many arbitrage funds have an exit load, typically 0.25% to 0.50%, if units are redeemed within a very short period, often 7 to 30 days. It's crucial to check the specific fund's Scheme Information Document (SID) for exact details.

How does the expense ratio impact returns?

The expense ratio is the annual fee charged by the fund house to manage the fund. Since arbitrage funds aim for relatively small spreads, a higher expense ratio can significantly eat into the net returns for investors. Lower expense ratios are generally preferable.
