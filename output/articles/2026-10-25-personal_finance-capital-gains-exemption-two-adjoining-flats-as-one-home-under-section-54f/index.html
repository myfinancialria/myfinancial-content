Capital Gains Exemption: Two Adjoining Flats as One Home Under Section 54F

TL;DR

  • The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) recently ruled on claiming capital gains tax exemption for buying two adjoining flats.
  • This ruling clarifies that two physically connected flats used as a single residence can be treated as one house.
  • The exemption applies under Section 54F of the Income Tax Act, 1961, for long-term capital gains from selling assets other than a residential house.
  • Taxpayers must ensure the flats are genuinely integrated and function as a single dwelling unit.
  • The Income Tax Department retains the right to verify such claims to prevent misuse.

What this means in plain terms

When you sell certain long-term capital assets, like land, shares, or mutual funds, and make a profit, this profit is subject to capital gains tax. To encourage investment in housing, the government offers exemptions under Section 54F if you reinvest these gains into a new residential house. Traditionally, there was ambiguity about whether buying two adjacent flats and combining them would qualify as "one residential house" for this exemption.

The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) has now provided clarity, ruling that if you purchase two adjoining flats, physically connect them, and use them as a single, integrated home, they can indeed be considered one residential house for the purpose of claiming the Section 54F capital gains tax exemption. This is a significant relief for taxpayers who wish to design larger living spaces by merging properties. However, it's crucial that the integration is genuine and verifiable, as the Income Tax Department will scrutinise such claims to ensure they meet the spirit of the law.

Understanding Capital Gains Exemption under Section 54F

Section 54F of the Income Tax Act, 1961, is a beneficial provision designed to exempt long-term capital gains (LTCG) arising from the sale of any long-term capital asset other than a residential house. This exemption is available if the net consideration from the sale of the original asset is reinvested within a specified timeframe into purchasing or constructing a new residential house in India. The capital asset could be anything from commercial property, land, shares, or even gold.

To qualify for this exemption, the taxpayer must purchase a new residential house within one year before or two years after the date of sale of the original asset, or construct a new residential house within three years after the date of sale. The amount of exemption depends on the proportion of the net sale consideration reinvested. If the entire net consideration is reinvested, the entire capital gain is exempt. If only a part is reinvested, the exemption is proportionate to the reinvested amount. Importantly, the taxpayer should not own more than one residential house, other than the new house, on the date of sale of the original asset. Additionally, they should not purchase any other residential house within two years or construct another one within three years from the date of sale of the original asset.

The ITAT Ruling: Two Adjoining Flats as One House

The recent ruling by the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) addresses a long-standing question for taxpayers: can two adjoining flats, purchased and then combined, be treated as a single residential unit for claiming capital gains tax exemption under Section 54F? The ITAT has affirmed that they can, provided certain conditions are met. This decision aligns with previous rulings by various high courts that have adopted a pragmatic view on what constitutes a "residential house," focusing on the functional aspect rather than mere architectural or legal divisions.

The core of the ruling is that if two adjacent flats are physically connected and used as a single, unified residence, they should be considered one residential house. This means breaking down a wall to create a larger living space, having a common kitchen, living area, and bedrooms that flow seamlessly from one unit to the other. The intent and actual usage of the property as a single dwelling unit are paramount. The ITAT's stance emphasises that the substance of the transaction – creating a larger, integrated home – should prevail over the form of having two separate property deeds. This provides much-needed relief and clarity for individuals looking to leverage capital gains for creating bespoke, larger homes in urban areas.

Key Conditions and Practical Considerations

While the ITAT ruling provides a favourable interpretation, taxpayers must be meticulous in meeting the underlying conditions and maintaining proper documentation. The primary condition is the physical integration and single usage of the two adjoining flats. This implies:

  • Physical Connection: There must be a clear, demonstrable physical connection between the two flats, such as a doorway or an archway, making them accessible as a single unit. Simply owning two adjacent flats without any physical modification to merge them will not suffice.
  • Single Residential Usage: The combined unit must function as one residential house. This means having common facilities like a single kitchen, living room, and dining area, rather than maintaining two separate, fully functional households within the combined space. The intention should be to create a larger single home, not to operate two independent units.
  • Documentation: It is crucial to maintain robust documentation. This includes proof of purchase of both flats, architectural plans showing the modifications made to connect them, utility bills (if possible, consolidated or clearly indicating single usage), and any other evidence that supports the claim of it being a single residential unit. Photographs of the integrated space can also be helpful.
  • Verification by Income Tax Department: The ruling explicitly states that the benefit remains subject to verification by the Income Tax Department. This means taxpayers should be prepared for potential scrutiny during assessment proceedings. Any claim that appears to be a mere attempt to circumvent tax laws without genuine integration could be challenged.

Taxpayers should also remember the other conditions of Section 54F, such as not owning more than one residential house (other than the new one) on the date of sale of the original asset, and adhering to the timelines for purchase or construction. Consulting a Certified Financial Planner or a tax advisor before making such investments is advisable to ensure full compliance.

A real example

Let's consider Meera, 45, a self-employed software consultant in Gurugram, with an annual income of ₹35 lakhs.

Meera sold a plot of non-agricultural land in December 2025 for ₹1.80 crores. She had purchased this land 12 years ago for ₹40 lakhs. Her long-term capital gain (after indexation) was calculated to be ₹1.10 crores. To save on capital gains tax under Section 54F, she decided to invest in a new residential property.

In July 2026, she identified two adjoining flats in a new residential complex in Gurugram.

  • Flat A: Purchased for ₹90 lakhs.
  • Flat B: Purchased for ₹80 lakhs.
  • Total investment in flats: ₹90 lakhs + ₹80 lakhs = ₹1.70 crores.

Upon taking possession, Meera engaged an interior designer to physically connect the two flats by breaking down a common wall and integrating the living spaces to create a single, spacious 4BHK home. She ensured that the layout functioned as one large family dwelling, with a single main kitchen and combined living areas.

Calculation of Section 54F Exemption:

  1. Net Sale Consideration of Original Asset (Land): ₹1.80 crores
  2. Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG) from Land: ₹1.10 crores
  3. Investment in New Residential House (Two Adjoining Flats): ₹1.70 crores

Since the entire net sale consideration of ₹1.80 crores was not invested, the exemption will be proportionate. The formula for exemption under Section 54F is: Exempt Capital Gain = (LTCG * Amount Invested in New House) / Net Sale Consideration Exempt Capital Gain = (₹1.10 crores * ₹1.70 crores) / ₹1.80 crores Exempt Capital Gain = ₹1,87,00,00,00,00,000 / ₹1,80,00,000 Exempt Capital Gain = ₹1,03,88,888.89 (approximately ₹1.04 crores)

Therefore, out of her capital gain of ₹1.10 crores, Meera can claim an exemption of approximately ₹1.04 crores. The remaining capital gain of ₹1.10 crores - ₹1.04 crores = ₹6 lakhs will be subject to long-term capital gains tax. If Meera had invested the full ₹1.80 crores (or more) into the two flats, her entire capital gain of ₹1.10 crores would have been exempt.

Meera will need to keep all documentation related to the purchase of both flats, the expenses incurred in combining them, and photographs of the integrated home to support her claim during income tax assessment.

What to do this week

  1. Review your property deeds: If you own adjoining properties or are considering buying them, ensure the titles are clear and understand the implications of combining them from a legal standpoint.
  2. Consult a tax advisor: Discuss your specific capital gains scenario with a Certified Financial Planner or a tax consultant to understand how this ITAT ruling applies to your situation and to plan your investment effectively.
  3. Document physical integration: If you have already combined two flats or plan to, start gathering evidence like architectural plans, contractor invoices for modifications, and photographs that clearly show the physical connection and single usage.
  4. Understand Section 54F timelines: Familiarise yourself with the one-year before or two-years after purchase/three-years after construction timelines for reinvestment to ensure you meet the criteria.
  5. Check other Section 54F conditions: Verify that you do not own more than one residential house (excluding the new one) on the date of sale of your original asset, as this is a critical condition for claiming the exemption.

FAQ

Can I claim Section 54F exemption if I buy two separate flats in different buildings?

No, the ITAT ruling specifically refers to "adjoining" flats that are physically connected and used as a single residence. Buying separate flats in different buildings would not qualify.

What if I don't physically connect the two adjoining flats?

If the flats are not physically connected and continue to function as two separate, independent units, they will likely not be considered a "single residential house" for Section 54F exemption purposes. Physical integration is key.

Is this ITAT ruling a new law?

No, ITAT rulings are interpretations of existing law. They provide clarity on how the Income Tax Act should be applied in specific situations. While not a new law, it sets a precedent for similar cases.

What kind of documentation do I need to prove single usage?

You should gather architectural plans showing the merged layout, invoices for renovation work, utility bills (if they reflect combined usage), and photographs of the integrated living space.

Can NRIs also claim this exemption?

Yes, Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) are also eligible to claim capital gains exemptions under Section 54F, provided they meet all the specified conditions, including the reinvestment in a residential house in India.

Does this ruling apply to Section 54 as well?

While the ruling specifically addresses Section 54F, the principle of treating physically integrated adjoining flats as one residential unit has been upheld in some cases for Section 54 as well by various courts. However, it's always best to consult a tax expert for specific advice.
