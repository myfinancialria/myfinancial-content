First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine your favourite local chai shop has become very popular. It wants to open more branches, buy new equipment, and grow bigger. To do this, it needs money. One way to get this money is to invite the public to become part-owners by selling them a small piece of the business.

This is what an IPO (Initial Public Offering) is. It's when a private company sells its shares to the public for the very first time. This initial sale happens in the primary market (think of it as the first-hand market). Once these shares are sold, the company's stock gets listed (meaning it becomes available for trading) on a stock exchange like NSE or BSE. After listing, you can buy or sell these shares in the secondary market (the regular stock market).

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, you'll see a few key terms. Understanding them helps you make sense of the offering:

  1. Price Band: This is the range within which you can bid for shares. For example, if the price band is ₹78 to ₹82, you can bid at any price within this range. The company usually sets a "cut-off price" within this band after the bidding closes, which is often the highest price.
  2. Lot Size: Shares in an IPO are not sold individually, but in fixed groups called "lots". If the lot size is 100 shares, you must apply for at least 100 shares, or multiples of 100 (like 200, 300, etc.). You cannot apply for 50 or 120 shares.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is the smallest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO. It's calculated by multiplying the lot size by the lowest price in the price band. For instance, if the lot size is 100 shares and the lowest price is ₹78, your minimum investment would be ₹7,800.
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total value or number of shares the company plans to offer to the public. It tells you how much money the company aims to raise from the IPO.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer For Sale (OFS):

    6. * Fresh Issue: When a company issues new shares to the public. The money raised from these shares goes directly to the company for its business needs (like expanding operations or paying off debt). * Offer For Sale (OFS): When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares to the public. The money from these shares goes to the selling shareholders, not to the company itself.

  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the main legal document that a company files with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Commission of India) before an IPO. It contains all the detailed information about the company, its business, financials, risks, management, and the IPO itself. Think of it as the company's detailed report card and business plan for potential investors.

The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO might seem complicated, but it follows a clear path:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first prepares a detailed Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) and submits it to SEBI for approval. This document has all the company's information.
  2. SEBI Approval & RHP: Once SEBI reviews and approves the DRHP, it becomes the Red Herring Prospectus (RHP). The company then announces the IPO dates and the price band.
  3. Bidding Period: This is when you can apply for shares. You place your bid (how many shares you want and at what price within the band) through your bank or broker.

    4. * Retail Investors: Ordinary individuals like you and me can apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs): These are individuals who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): These are large institutions like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.

  4. Allotment: After the bidding closes, the company and its bankers decide how to distribute the shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more people applied for shares than available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
  5. Refunds/Debit: If you are not allotted shares, your blocked money is refunded. If you are allotted shares, the money is debited from your account.
  6. Listing: Finally, the shares are listed on the stock exchange (like NSE or BSE). This is when they start trading in the open market, and their price can go up or down based on demand and supply.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine your local grocery store, 'Sharma Kirana', wants to expand into a bigger supermarket. To do this, Mr. Sharma decides to sell small 'ownership coupons' for his business. Each coupon represents a tiny part of the store. He announces that these coupons will be sold for the first time next week. This is his IPO.

When you buy one of these coupons, you become a tiny part-owner of Sharma Kirana. The money you pay helps Mr. Sharma build his new supermarket. Once all the coupons are sold, they can then be bought and sold among customers in the market. This is similar to how a company sells parts of itself (shares) to the public to raise money, and then these shares are traded on the stock market.

Things people get confused about

Here are a few common misunderstandings about IPOs:

  • Myth: Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares.

    • * Fact: If an IPO is very popular and gets more applications than available shares (oversubscribed), shares are allotted through a lottery system, especially for retail investors. So, you might not get any shares even if you apply.

  • Myth: All IPOs give quick listing gains.

    • * Fact: While some IPOs might list at a higher price than their issue price, this is not guaranteed. The listing price depends on market conditions, investor sentiment, and the company's fundamentals. Shares can also list at a discount or trade below their issue price.

  • Myth: You must apply at the highest price in the band to get shares.

    • * Fact: You can bid at any price within the price band. However, to maximise your chances of allotment in an oversubscribed IPO, many retail investors choose to bid at the "cut-off price," which usually means bidding at the highest price in the band. If the final price is set lower, you will still get shares at that lower price.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility via net banking, or through your stockbroker's platform. You will need a demat account and a trading account.

What is a lot size?

A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. Shares are not sold individually but in fixed groups or "lots."

What is the difference between a Mainboard IPO and an SME IPO?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger companies and list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE), while SME IPOs are for smaller companies and list on dedicated SME platforms of the exchanges. SME IPOs typically have higher minimum investment amounts (generally above ₹2 lakh for individual investors, requiring a minimum of 2 lots).

Can I apply for more than one lot?

Yes, you can apply for multiple lots, but only in multiples of the specified lot size. For retail investors, the total application value cannot exceed ₹2 lakhs.

What happens if I don't get shares in an IPO?

If you are not allotted any shares, the money that was blocked in your bank account (under the ASBA facility) will be unblocked and available for use.
