The roar of a Harley-Davidson engine, usually a triumphant anthem, became a muffled concession in September 2020. After 11 years and selling a modest 27,000 bikes, the American motorcycle icon announced its complete withdrawal from India, shutting down its manufacturing and scaling back sales operations. The decision was a stark admission of defeat in the world's largest two-wheeler market, a place where the famed "hog" simply couldn't find its stride.

The World Before

When Harley-Davidson first set its sights on India in 2009, the landscape looked tantalizingly promising. India was, and still is, a two-wheeler behemoth, with an annual market of approximately 17 million bikes and scooters. A burgeoning middle class with rising disposable incomes seemed poised to embrace premium lifestyle products. The thinking was simple: as incomes grew, so too would the demand for aspirational, high-end motorcycles.

However, beneath the surface of this vast market lay a crucial reality: India was predominantly a "high-volumes, low-margins" territory, dominated by fuel-efficient, small-capacity commuter bikes under 150cc. Brands like Hero MotoCorp, Bajaj, and Honda thrived by catering to this segment, producing millions of affordable units annually. The idea of a luxury cruiser, a weekend machine built for open highways, was almost an alien concept. Even the premium motorcycle segment (above 500cc) was minuscule, accounting for only about 25,000 units a year across all brands when Harley exited.

Adding to the challenge were India's formidable import duties. Initially, Completely Built Units (CBUs) – fully assembled bikes imported from abroad – faced tariffs exceeding 100%. This meant a Harley-Davidson, already an expensive proposition, saw its price tag effectively double upon reaching Indian shores, placing it far out of reach for all but the ultra-rich. Despite this, Harley-Davidson entered, buoyed by "mango diplomacy" where access for their bikes was traded for Indian mango exports to the US, and a belief that demand would overcome these tariff hurdles.

The Crack

Harley-Davidson officially commenced operations in India in August 2009, opening its first dealership in Hyderabad in July 2010. Their initial portfolio included 12 models from five families (Sportster, Dyna, VRSC, Softail, and CVO), all imported as CBUs. The high prices, however, quickly became a significant barrier. To mitigate this, in the first half of 2011, Harley-Davidson established a Complete Knock-Down (CKD) assembly plant in Bawal, Haryana. This plant assembled motorcycles from component kits shipped from its US facilities, a move designed to reduce the tariff burden and make bikes more accessible. Models like the Street 750 and Street 500 were specifically introduced and assembled there, with the Street 750 even being exported to European and Asian markets.

Despite these efforts, the "Made in India" Street models, priced from ₹4.7 lakh (approximately $6,100 at 2020 exchange rates), still struggled. While assembly helped, the level of local component sourcing (indigenisation) remained low, meaning many parts were still imported and subject to duties, keeping prices elevated. Moreover, the fundamental design of Harley's cruisers—heavy, air-cooled engines and low ground clearance—proved ill-suited for India's often uneven roads, dense traffic, and hot climate, leading to issues like excessive heat for riders.

The competition was also fierce. While Harley aimed for the premium segment, it found itself outmaneuvered by Royal Enfield, an Indian brand with a long history and a strong emotional connection in the country. Royal Enfield offered more affordable, mid-capacity motorcycles that resonated better with Indian tastes and road conditions, selling upwards of 50,000 units monthly in 2020, compared to Harley-Davidson's total sales of 27,000 bikes over a decade.

Dealerships, which typically required an investment of ₹50 million to ₹80 million (around $670,000 to $1 million), struggled to remain profitable, selling fewer than 70 bikes annually per outlet. Harley-Davidson managed to open only 35 dealerships across the country. The high cost of ownership, including expensive maintenance and spare parts that often had to be imported, further deterred potential buyers.

Globally, Harley-Davidson was also facing headwinds. The company reported its lowest net income in nine years in 2019 ($423 million) and its first quarterly loss in over a decade ($96 million between April and June 2020). In 2019, it shipped only 213,939 motorcycles to dealerships, the lowest in nine years. This period saw a global restructuring plan dubbed "The Rewire," aimed at focusing on more profitable markets and consolidating operations. India, with its persistent sales challenges and a demand outlook further dented by the COVID-19 pandemic, became a casualty of this strategy.

What Happened Next

On September 24, 2020, Harley-Davidson officially announced its decision to cease sales and manufacturing operations in India. The exit involved approximately 70 job redundancies, the closure of its Bawal manufacturing plant, and restructuring costs estimated at $75 million, with overall restructuring charges expected to hit $169 million in 2020.

However, Harley-Davidson did not entirely abandon the market. Just a month later, in October 2020, it announced a strategic partnership with Hero MotoCorp, the world's largest manufacturer of motorcycles and scooters by unit volume. This licensing and distribution agreement was a lifeline, tasking Hero MotoCorp with selling and servicing Harley-Davidson motorcycles, parts, accessories, and merchandise through a network of brand-exclusive dealerships and Hero's vast existing network. More significantly, the deal also included Hero MotoCorp developing and selling a new range of premium motorcycles under the Harley-Davidson brand name, specifically tailored for the Indian market.

This collaboration bore its first fruit in July 2023 with the launch of the Harley-Davidson X440, a single-cylinder, 440cc motorcycle produced by Hero MotoCorp at its Neemrana plant. Priced from ₹2.29 lakh (approximately $2,700), the X440 was designed to be more accessible and better suited to Indian conditions, directly challenging Royal Enfield in the mid-capacity premium segment. While initial sales showed promise, with 1,197 units sold in June 2026, marking a 69% year-on-year growth, the premium segment remains niche. Discussions are ongoing to potentially expand this collaboration to include more Harley-Davidson models and even manufacturing for export.

Meanwhile, the initial exit left some of Harley's former dealers in a lurch, with reports of them exploring legal action against the company over inadequate compensation for their investments. In a twist of irony, recent developments suggest that India might reduce import duties on certain American CBU motorcycles (800cc-1600cc) to zero by March 2026, a policy change that could have significantly benefited Harley-Davidson's earlier strategy, though it would not apply to its largest cruisers.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Market-Fit Trumps Brand Aura: Harley-Davidson learned that even an iconic brand, revered globally, cannot succeed without adapting its core product to local market realities. Their large, expensive cruisers were fundamentally mismatched with India's road conditions, climate, and consumer preferences for affordability and practicality. The "dream" they sold was not the one Indian riders were buying.
  2. Tariff Barriers Are Real Costs: While aspirational demand exists, exorbitant import duties create an insurmountable price barrier for mass adoption in price-sensitive markets. Harley's attempts with CKD assembly were a step, but insufficient without deeper local manufacturing and supply chain integration to truly drive down costs.
  3. Local Competition is Fierce and Agile: Royal Enfield demonstrated the power of a deeply entrenched local brand that understood the pulse of the Indian rider, offering relevant products at competitive prices. Foreign entrants must be prepared to contend with, or even learn from, indigenous players who have built decades of trust and infrastructure.
  4. Dealership Economics Matter: A healthy dealership network is the backbone of any automotive business. When unit sales are low and margins are squeezed by high product costs, dealer profitability suffers, leading to a weak sales and service ecosystem. Harley's struggle to maintain profitable dealerships underscored this critical point.
  5. Partnerships Can Be a Second Chance: Harley-Davidson's return via Hero MotoCorp illustrates that strategic alliances with local giants can be a viable path to re-entry and long-term sustainability. By leveraging Hero's manufacturing prowess and distribution network, Harley can now offer tailor-made, affordable products, demonstrating a more grounded approach to the market.
