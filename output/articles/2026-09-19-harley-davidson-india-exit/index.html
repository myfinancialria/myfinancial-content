September 24, 2020. The announcement landed with the quiet finality of a gavel strike: Harley-Davidson, the iconic American motorcycle manufacturer, was pulling the plug on its independent sales and manufacturing operations in India. It was a stark admission of defeat, a retreat from the world's largest two-wheeler market after an 11-year, often tumultuous, ride. The decision, part of a global "Rewire" strategy aimed at streamlining the company, meant shuttering its Bawal plant in Haryana, laying off approximately 70 employees, and incurring $75 million in restructuring costs. For a brand synonymous with freedom and the open road, this wasn't just a business decision; it was the end of a grand, yet ultimately miscalculated, ambition.

The World Before

When Harley-Davidson first set its sights on India in the late 2000s, the market was a world apart from its home turf. India was (and largely remains) a land of utilitarian two-wheelers, where fuel efficiency, affordability, and robust practicality dictated sales. Commuter bikes, typically under 150cc, dominated the landscape, accounting for the vast majority of the over 10 million units sold annually by 2009-2010. The idea of a premium motorcycle segment was nascent, almost an oxymoron in a country where a bike was primarily a means of transport, not a weekend indulgence.

Yet, Harley-Davidson saw potential. India's economy was booming, disposable incomes were rising, and a new generation of aspirational buyers was emerging. The company believed that this growing affluence would inevitably lead to a demand for luxury goods, including its legendary "hogs." Its entry into India in August 2009 was met with considerable fanfare, even preceded by a unique "mango diplomacy" agreement in 2007, where India allowed Harley imports in exchange for mango exports to the US.

The initial business model was straightforward: import completely built units (CBUs) from the USA. This, however, came with a heavy price tag. Indian import duties, sometimes exceeding 100%, effectively doubled the sale price of these already premium motorcycles. Despite this, Harley-Davidson launched 12 models in 2010, with prices ranging from INR 6.95 lakh to INR 34.95 lakh (ex-showroom). They quickly established a network, with five dealerships initially, aiming for over 20 within five years. The brand cultivated a strong aspirational value, and its Harley Owners Group (H.O.G.) programme fostered a unique riding culture, quickly making it a leader in the nascent premium two-wheeler space.

The Crack

Harley's journey in India, while starting with a bang, soon revealed deep cracks in its strategy. The initial success, driven by the sheer novelty and aspirational appeal of the brand, proved difficult to sustain at scale. To address the exorbitant pricing of CBUs, Harley-Davidson commenced Completely Knocked Down (CKD) operations in India for some models, like the Fat Boy, which helped bring prices down by a few lakhs.

A pivotal moment came with the introduction of the Harley-Davidson Street 750 in 2014, specifically designed with feedback from urban riders and manufactured in India. Launched at a significantly more accessible price point of INR 4.10 lakh (ex-showroom), it was almost half the price of the next most affordable Harley. This local assembly and targeted product did boost sales significantly, and by 2016, Harley even set an ambitious target of 10,000 units a year.

However, the tide began to turn. Despite these efforts, Harley-Davidson struggled to crack the high-volume Indian market. Over 11 years, the company sold approximately 27,000 bikes in India. In the financial year ending March 2020, sales plummeted to less than 2,500 units, a 22% drop from the previous year. India, despite being the world's largest two-wheeler market with 17 million units sold annually, contributed a mere 5% to Harley's global sales.

Several factors chipped away at Harley's Indian dream:

  1. Exorbitant Pricing & Fierce Competition: Even with local assembly, Harley-Davidson bikes remained premium. A typical Harley-Davidson "hog" retailed for around $6,105, while a rival like Royal Enfield offered motorcycles at approximately $2,714. The launch of Royal Enfield's 650 twins, offering similar performance to the Street 750 at less than half the price, delivered a significant blow to Harley's market share. The Indian consumer, even in the premium segment, proved to be highly value-conscious.
  2. High Import Duties: Despite years of lobbying, India's high import duties on fully-built large-capacity motorcycles continued to be a major hurdle, sometimes exceeding 40-50% for high-end models. This kept Harley's prices out of reach for a broader customer base.
  3. A Niche Within a Niche: The premium motorcycle segment (bikes above 350cc) remained tiny, accounting for only about 1.4% of total two-wheeler sales in India as recently as 2025-26. Harley was targeting an extremely small segment of the population, mostly affluent weekend riders, in a market driven by daily commuting needs.
  4. Road Conditions and Practicality: India's varied road infrastructure and heavy urban traffic were not ideal for large, heavy cruisers designed for long, open highways. Many riders found it challenging to navigate city congestion or even drive at high speeds on many Indian highways.
  5. Dealership Profitability: With low sales volumes, maintaining Harley-Davidson's expansive dealerships, which required significant investment, became a struggle for many partners, bleeding cash.

Globally, Harley-Davidson was also facing headwinds. In July 2020, under new CEO Jochen Zeitz, the company unveiled "The Rewire" strategy – a major overhaul to boost profits by reducing its product portfolio by 30% and focusing on 50 key markets with growth potential in North America, Europe, and parts of Asia Pacific. India, with its low volumes and profitability challenges, simply didn't make the cut. The exit was a calculated, albeit painful, decision to prune unprofitable ventures and focus resources elsewhere.

What Happened Next

Harley-Davidson's departure from independent operations left a void, but not for long. Within a month of its exit announcement, in October 2020, the company forged a strategic partnership with Hero MotoCorp, the world's largest manufacturer of motorcycles and scooters by unit volumes. This alliance was a lifeline for Harley-Davidson in India and a significant boost for Hero MotoCorp's premium segment ambitions.

Under the distribution agreement, Hero MotoCorp took over the sales and servicing of Harley-Davidson motorcycles, parts, accessories, and riding gear through a network of exclusive Harley-Davidson dealers and its own extensive dealership network. More significantly, a licensing agreement allowed Hero MotoCorp to develop and sell a range of premium motorcycles under the Harley-Davidson brand name.

The most tangible outcome of this partnership was the launch of the Harley-Davidson X440 in 2023. This motorcycle, designed by Harley-Davidson but manufactured by Hero MotoCorp in India, marked a significant shift. Positioned at a much more accessible price point (starting around INR 2.35 lakh), the X440 aimed directly at the growing mid-capacity premium segment, competing with the likes of Royal Enfield. The X440 saw enthusiastic reception, with over 25,000 bookings within a month of its launch. This partnership has since expanded, with plans for new variants and even new motorcycle development for both domestic and potential international markets, leveraging India as a manufacturing base.

In a separate development, India has also made recent strides in reducing import duties on high-capacity motorcycles, including some Harley-Davidson models, as part of trade pacts with the US. For bikes between 800cc and 1600cc, duties are slated to drop significantly, potentially to zero, which could make larger Harleys cheaper in the future. This change, however, came years after Harley's independent exit, highlighting the persistent regulatory challenges it faced.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Market Nuance Trumps Brand Power: Even an iconic global brand like Harley-Davidson cannot simply transplant its business model into a vastly different market. India's unique blend of price sensitivity, infrastructure challenges, and a dominant commuter culture required a deeper understanding than initial premium aspirations suggested. The "weekend rider niche" was simply too small to sustain an ambitious direct-to-consumer strategy.
  2. The Cost of "Premium" in a Value Market: High import duties combined with the brand's premium positioning created a price barrier that even rising incomes couldn't overcome for sufficient volumes. When domestic or locally assembled competitors offered comparable products at a fraction of the cost, the value proposition for many Harley models became untenable.
  3. Localisation is Key, but Not a Panacea: Harley's efforts to localise with the Street 750 and CKD operations were steps in the right direction, proving that reducing price points could boost sales. However, this wasn't enough to counteract the deeper systemic issues of market size and intense competition from entrenched players. Localisation must be integrated into a fundamentally sound market-entry strategy, not merely a reactive measure.
  4. Strategic Pruning for Global Health: Harley-Davidson's "Rewire" strategy underscores the importance of regularly evaluating market performance and having the courage to exit unprofitable ventures. While painful in the short term, shedding underperforming assets can free up resources and focus for markets where a business has a clearer path to profitability and sustainable growth.
  5. Partnerships Can Unlock Markets: The eventual collaboration with Hero MotoCorp highlights that sometimes, the best way to succeed in a complex market is not to go it alone. Partnering with a local giant, which possesses deep distribution networks, manufacturing capabilities, and an inherent understanding of the domestic consumer, can be a more effective path to long-term presence and growth, even if it means a different business model.
