First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine your favourite local shop, say "Sharma Ji ki Mithai," has been doing really well for years. Now, Sharma Ji wants to open more branches across the city and maybe even buy a bigger kitchen. To do this, he needs a lot of money. Instead of borrowing from a bank, he decides to sell a small part of his shop to the public. This way, ordinary people like you and me can become part-owners of Sharma Ji ki Mithai.

This "selling a part of the company to the public for the first time" is called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). When a company does an IPO, it's entering the primary market (where new shares are sold for the first time). After the IPO, its shares will be available for trading on a stock exchange (like NSE or BSE), which is called listing. Once listed, these shares can be bought and sold by anyone in the secondary market (the regular stock market).

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, you'll see a few standard terms. Understanding them helps you make sense of the offering:

  1. Price Band: This is the range within which investors can bid for shares. For example, if a price band is ₹402 to ₹424, you can bid at any price within this range. The company usually decides on a final "cut-off" price within this band after all bids are in.
  2. Lot Size: This is the minimum number of shares you must apply for. You cannot apply for just one or two shares; you have to apply in multiples of this lot size. For instance, if the lot size is 35 shares, you must apply for 35, 70, 105 shares, and so on.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is the smallest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO. It's calculated by multiplying the lot size by the lowest price in the price band. For example, if the lot size is 35 shares and the lower price band is ₹402, your minimum investment would be 35 shares * ₹402 = ₹14,070.
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public through the IPO. It gives you an idea of how large the offering is. Sometimes, it's also expressed as the total value in rupees (e.g., ₹500 crores).
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS):

    6. * Fresh Issue: When a company issues "fresh" shares, the money raised from selling these shares goes directly to the company. The company uses this money for its business needs, like expanding operations, paying off debt, or funding new projects. * Offer-for-Sale (OFS): In an OFS, existing shareholders (like the promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares to the public. The money raised from an OFS goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself.

  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the official document filed by the company with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) before an IPO. It contains detailed information about the company's business, financials, risks, management, how the money will be used, and the IPO details. Think of it as the company's detailed report card and business plan for potential investors. The DRHP is the draft version, and the RHP is the final version before the IPO opens.

The IPO process, step by step

Here's how an IPO generally unfolds:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI. This document has all the details about the company and its plans for the IPO. SEBI reviews this.
  2. RHP Approval & Price Band Announcement: Once SEBI approves, the company files the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) and announces the price band and other details like the lot size and opening/closing dates.
  3. Bidding Period: The IPO opens for a few days (usually 3-5 working days). During this time, investors can place their bids for shares through their bank or broker.

    4. * Retail Investors: These are individual investors who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs) / Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs): These are individuals or entities who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): These are large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.

  4. Allotment: After the bidding closes, the company and its bankers decide how to distribute the shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than shares available), shares are allotted on a lottery basis for retail investors. If it's undersubscribed (fewer applications than shares), everyone who applied gets shares.
  5. Refunds/Share Credit: If you don't get shares, your blocked money is refunded. If you do get shares, they are credited to your Demat account (an electronic account where your shares are held).
  6. Listing: Finally, the shares get listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE) on a specified date. This is when they start trading in the secondary market, and you can buy or sell them like any other stock.

A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a school fete. The school (the company) wants to raise money for new sports equipment (business expansion). So, it decides to sell "ownership tickets" (shares) for its stalls (parts of the company). These tickets are sold for the first time at a fixed price range (price band) for a few days (bidding period). If many people want tickets, they might be given out by lottery (allotment). Once the fete is over, these tickets can then be traded among students (listing on the stock market).

Things people get confused about

Here are some common myths and facts about IPOs:

  • Myth: Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares.

    • * Fact: Not always. If an IPO is very popular and receives many more applications than there are shares available (oversubscribed), shares are often allotted through a lottery system, especially for retail investors. You might not get any shares.

  • Myth: You will always make a profit on the day an IPO lists (listing gain).

    • * Fact: Listing gains are not guaranteed. While some IPOs do list at a higher price than their issue price, others might list at or below the issue price. The market conditions and the company's fundamentals play a big role.

  • Myth: All IPOs are good investments.

    • * Fact: Every investment carries risk, and IPOs are no exception. It's important to research the company, understand its business, financials, and future prospects before deciding to apply.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's net banking portal using the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility, or through your stockbroker's platform.

What is a lot size?

The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for in an IPO. You cannot apply for fewer shares than the specified lot size.

What is a Demat account?

A Demat account is an electronic account that holds your shares and other securities in a digital format, similar to how a bank account holds your money.

What does "oversubscribed" mean?

An IPO is oversubscribed when the total number of shares applied for by investors is more than the total number of shares the company is offering.

What is the difference between primary and secondary markets?

The primary market is where new securities are issued for the first time (like an IPO). The secondary market is where existing securities are traded among investors (like buying and selling shares on the stock exchange after listing).
