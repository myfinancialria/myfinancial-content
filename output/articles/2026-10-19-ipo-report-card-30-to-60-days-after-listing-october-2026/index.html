Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an initial public offer (IPO). The first day these shares start trading on the stock exchange is known as the listing day. Often, there's a lot of excitement around new listings, which can cause the share price to jump significantly. This jump from the IPO price to the listing day closing price is called a listing gain.

However, the listing day price doesn't always tell the full story. The initial frenzy can fade, and the market starts to evaluate the company more calmly. Also, certain large investors, known as anchor investors (who subscribe to a portion of the IPO before it opens to the public), often have a lock-in period (a time during which they cannot sell their shares), which typically ends around 30 days after listing. Once this lock-in ends, these investors are free to sell, which can affect the share price.

Looking at the share price 30 to 60 days after listing gives us a more settled picture. It helps us understand how the market is valuing the company after the initial excitement has passed and some trading restrictions have lifted.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

The initial movement of an IPO share price, whether it "pops" (rises) or "drops" (falls), is mainly due to the basic principles of demand and supply.

Imagine a popular stall at a school fete selling a limited number of special handmade crafts. If many people want to buy these crafts (high demand) but there are only a few available (limited supply), the price they are willing to pay might go up. This is similar to an IPO with high subscription levels (when many times more bids are received than the shares offered). High demand often leads to a "pop" on listing day.

On the other hand, if there are not many buyers, or if the company's shares were priced (the IPO price was set) too high, or if the overall market mood (the general sentiment of investors towards the stock market) is negative, the share price might drop.

Sometimes, large institutional investors called anchor investors buy a significant portion of shares before the IPO opens to the public. They usually have a lock-in period, meaning they cannot sell their shares for a certain time (often 30 days). Once this lock-in period ends, if many of them decide to sell, it increases the supply of shares in the market, which can also influence the price.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just the starting point. The stock market is dynamic, and prices can experience volatility (quick and sometimes large ups and downs) over time. A stock might list with a big gain, but then its price could fall in the following weeks or months if the company's performance doesn't meet expectations, or if the broader market experiences a downturn.

Conversely, a stock might list flat or even below its IPO price, but then gradually rise as the company shows good results or the market's perception improves. The broader market (the overall performance of the stock market) also plays a big role. Even a good company's share price can be affected by general market trends, economic news, or sector-specific events.

A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a grand opening sale for a new shop. On the first day, there might be a huge crowd, special offers, and a lot of buzz. Prices might seem to fly off the shelves. But after a month or two, the initial excitement settles down. The shop's true value is then reflected by how many regular customers it attracts, how good its products are, and how it performs day-to-day. Similarly, an IPO's true market value often becomes clearer after the initial listing excitement fades.

Things people get confused about

Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.

Fact: A large listing gain primarily reflects high initial demand for the shares, not necessarily the long-term quality or future performance of the company.

Myth → Fact: A low subscription level means the IPO is bad.

Fact: While high subscription often indicates strong investor interest, some fundamentally strong companies might have lower subscription numbers but still perform well over time as their business grows.

Myth → Fact: The IPO price is the "right" price for the company.

Fact: The IPO price is a price set by the company and its bankers to attract investors and raise capital. The market then decides the "right" price through trading activity after listing.

FAQ

What is an IPO?

An IPO is when a private company offers its shares to the public for the first time, allowing anyone to buy a part of the company.

How do I apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your stockbroker using the ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility, which blocks the application amount in your bank account until shares are allotted.

What is a lot size in an IPO?

A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot bid for fewer shares than the specified lot size.

What is the difference between a Mainboard IPO and an SME IPO?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger companies and list on the main exchanges (NSE and BSE), while SME IPOs are for Small and Medium Enterprises and list on dedicated SME platforms like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, with different regulations and market capitalisation requirements.

What is the "issue price"?

The issue price, also known as the IPO price, is the fixed price at which the company offers its shares to the public during the IPO process.
