First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local shop that has been doing very well. The owner wants to expand, maybe open new branches or buy new machines. To do this, they need a lot of money. Instead of borrowing from a bank, they decide to sell a small part of their shop's ownership to the public. This is what an Initial Public Offering (IPO) is.

An IPO is when a private company offers its shares to the public for the first time. It's their way of raising money directly from you and me. When a company does an IPO, it enters the primary market (where new shares are sold for the first time). Once these shares are sold, they then get listed on a stock exchange like NSE or BSE. This means anyone can buy or sell these shares in the secondary market (the regular stock market) after the IPO is complete.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, there are a few key numbers and terms you will always see. Understanding these helps you make sense of the offering.

  1. Price Band: This is the range within which you can bid for shares. For example, if a price band is ₹100-₹105, you can bid at any price between ₹100 and ₹105. Most people bid at the "cut-off price" or "upper end of the price band" (₹105 in this example) to increase their chances of allotment.
  2. Lot Size: This is the minimum number of shares you must apply for. You cannot apply for just one share; you have to apply in multiples of the lot size. If the lot size is 100 shares, you must bid for 100 shares, 200 shares, 300 shares, and so on. This is because IPOs are often designed for wider participation and to manage the application process efficiently.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is directly linked to the price band and lot size. It's the minimum amount of money you need to invest to apply for one lot of shares. If the upper price band is ₹105 and the lot size is 100 shares, your minimum investment would be ₹105 x 100 = ₹10,500.
  4. Issue Size: This is the total value of shares the company plans to sell through the IPO. It tells you how much money the company aims to raise. For example, an issue size of ₹500 crores means the company is looking to raise ₹500 crores from the public. This number helps you understand the scale of the IPO.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS): An IPO can be made up of two parts:

    6. * Fresh Issue: This is when the company issues brand new shares. The money raised from these shares goes directly to the company for its business needs (like expansion, debt repayment, etc.). * Offer-for-Sale (OFS): This is when existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares. The money raised from an OFS goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself. It's important to know the split, as a large fresh issue means more capital for the company's growth.

  6. Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) / Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP): This is the official document filed with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India), India's market regulator. Think of it as the company's detailed report card and business plan for the IPO. It contains almost everything you need to know: the company's business, its financials (profits, losses, assets), risks, management details, how it plans to use the money, and much more. The DRHP is the draft version, and the RHP is the final version filed just before the IPO opens. Always read the RHP if you're considering an IPO.

The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO might seem complicated, but it follows a clear path:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI. This document contains all the initial details about the company and its IPO plans. SEBI reviews this document to ensure everything is in order.
  2. SEBI Approval & RHP: Once SEBI gives its approval, the company files the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP). This RHP includes the price band and other final details.
  3. Bidding Period: The IPO opens for a few days (usually 3-5 working days). During this time, you can place your bids for shares. There are different categories of investors:

    4. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Ordinary investors like you and me, who can apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs) / High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs): Investors who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large institutions like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.

  4. Allotment: After the bidding closes, the company and its bankers determine how to allot shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than available shares), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors. If it's undersubscribed, everyone who applied usually gets shares.
  5. Refunds/Debit: If you don't get an allotment, your blocked money (via ASBA) is released. If you get an allotment, the money for your allotted shares is debited from your account.
  6. Listing: Finally, the shares get listed on the stock exchange (like NSE or BSE). This is when trading begins, and you can buy or sell your shares in the open market.

A simple way to think about it

Think of a school fete where a popular stall is selling delicious homemade sweets. The stall owner wants to expand and open a proper shop. To raise money, they decide to sell "ownership coupons" for their future shop.

The IPO is like the first time they sell these coupons to the public. The price band is the suggested price range for each coupon. The lot size is the minimum number of coupons you must buy (e.g., you can't buy just one, you must buy a pack of 10 coupons). The issue size is the total value of all coupons they hope to sell.

Once you buy these coupons, you become a part-owner. When the shop opens, these coupons become tradable "shares" on the stock exchange, and you can sell them to others or buy more.

Things people get confused about

Here are a few common misconceptions about IPOs:

  • Myth: Listing gain is guaranteed in every IPO.

    • * Fact: While some IPOs do list at a higher price than the issue price, there is no guarantee. Shares can also list at a discount, meaning below the issue price. The performance depends on market conditions, company fundamentals, and investor demand.

  • Myth: You must apply for an IPO through a broker.

    • * Fact: You can apply for an IPO directly through your bank using the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility, which is available in most net banking portals. You can also apply through your stockbroker's platform, which uses ASBA.

  • Myth: All IPOs are for large, well-known companies.

    • * Fact: While many large companies do IPOs, there are also SME IPOs, like the IC Electricals Company Limited one, which are for smaller companies. These list on dedicated SME platforms of the stock exchanges.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's net banking portal using the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility, or through your stockbroker's online platform.

What is a lot size?

The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for in an IPO. You cannot apply for fewer than the specified lot size.

What does "oversubscribed" mean?

An IPO is oversubscribed when the total demand for shares (the number of applications received) is more than the number of shares the company is offering.

What is the difference between a mainboard IPO and an SME IPO?

Mainboard IPOs are typically for larger companies and list on the main platforms of NSE and BSE. SME IPOs are for Small and Medium Enterprises and list on dedicated SME platforms (like NSE Emerge or BSE SME).

What is ASBA?

ASBA stands for Application Supported by Blocked Amount. When you apply for an IPO using ASBA, the application money is blocked in your bank account but not debited until shares are allotted to you. If you don't receive an allotment, the money is simply unblocked.
