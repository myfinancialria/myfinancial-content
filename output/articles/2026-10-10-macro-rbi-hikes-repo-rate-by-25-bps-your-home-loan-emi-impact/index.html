RBI Hikes Repo Rate by 25 bps: Your Home Loan EMI Impact

TL;DR

  • The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) increased the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% on October 7, 2026.
  • This hike directly impacts home loan interest rates, especially those linked to external benchmarks.
  • Borrowers may see their Equated Monthly Instalments (EMIs) increase or their loan tenure extended.
  • Understanding your loan's interest rate type (EBLR or MCLR) is crucial to assess the immediate impact.
  • Reviewing your home loan statement and considering options like prepayment or balance transfer is advisable.

What this means in plain terms

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) recently announced an increase in its key lending rate, known as the repo rate, by 25 basis points (bps). This means the rate has moved from 5.25% to 5.50% as of October 7, 2026. In simple terms, the repo rate is the interest rate at which commercial banks borrow money from the RBI. When this rate goes up, it typically signals to commercial banks that their cost of funds has increased.

For you, as a home loan borrower, this change is significant because most home loan interest rates in India are directly or indirectly linked to the repo rate. Banks usually pass on this increased cost of borrowing to their customers. This means that if you have a floating rate home loan, your bank will likely adjust your interest rate upwards, leading to either a higher EMI (Equated Monthly Instalment) or an extension of your loan repayment tenure, or sometimes a combination of both.

Understanding the Repo Rate and RBI's Mandate

What is the Repo Rate?

The repo rate, short for repurchase option rate, is the interest rate at which commercial banks borrow money from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) by selling government securities with an agreement to repurchase them at a predetermined future date. It's a critical tool in the RBI's monetary policy arsenal. A lower repo rate makes it cheaper for banks to borrow, encouraging lending and economic activity, while a higher repo rate makes borrowing more expensive, aiming to curb inflation and tighten money supply.

Why Does RBI Change It?

The primary mandate of the RBI's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) is to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth. When inflation, or the general increase in prices, is high and persistent, the RBI often raises the repo rate to reduce the amount of money circulating in the economy. By making borrowing more expensive for banks, and subsequently for consumers and businesses, the RBI aims to cool down demand, thereby bringing inflation under control. Conversely, in times of economic slowdown, the RBI might lower the repo rate to stimulate borrowing and investment. The recent 25 bps hike to 5.50% reflects the RBI's ongoing efforts to manage inflationary pressures in the Indian economy.

How Home Loan Rates are Linked

External Benchmark Lending Rate (EBLR)

Since October 1, 2019, the RBI mandated that all new floating rate retail loans, including home loans, must be linked to external benchmarks. The most common external benchmark chosen by banks is the RBI's repo rate. This means that changes in the repo rate are transmitted directly and relatively quickly to your home loan interest rate. When the RBI hikes the repo rate by 25 bps, banks typically adjust their EBLR-linked home loan rates upwards by a similar margin, after factoring in their spread. This direct linkage ensures greater transparency and faster transmission of monetary policy changes to borrowers.

Marginal Cost of Funds Based Lending Rate (MCLR)

Before the EBLR regime, most floating rate home loans were linked to the Marginal Cost of Funds Based Lending Rate (MCLR). Loans sanctioned before October 2019 might still be linked to MCLR. MCLR is an internal benchmark determined by individual banks based on their marginal cost of funds, negative carry on CRR, operating costs, and tenor premium. While MCLR is not directly linked to the repo rate, it is indirectly influenced by it. When the repo rate changes, banks' cost of funds (a component of MCLR) is affected, leading to a subsequent adjustment in MCLR. However, the transmission of policy rates to MCLR-linked loans can be slower and less direct compared to EBLR-linked loans. Your loan agreement will specify whether your loan is linked to EBLR or MCLR.

Your Options: EMI Increase or Tenure Extension

When your home loan interest rate increases due to a repo rate hike, banks typically offer two primary ways to adjust your loan:

Opting for Higher EMIs

This is the most straightforward adjustment. Your bank will calculate the new EMI based on the increased interest rate while keeping your original loan tenure constant. A higher EMI means you pay more each month, but your loan will still be repaid within the initially agreed timeframe. This option is generally preferred by borrowers who have sufficient disposable income to absorb the increased monthly outflow and wish to close their loan sooner. It ensures that the total interest paid over the life of the loan doesn't increase as dramatically as it would with a tenure extension.

Extending Your Loan Tenure

If you prefer to keep your monthly EMI amount relatively stable, your bank might extend your loan repayment tenure. This means you will continue paying roughly the same EMI, but you will be paying it for a longer period than originally planned. While this keeps your immediate financial burden in check, it significantly increases the total interest paid over the entire life of the loan. For example, a loan that was supposed to end in 15 years might now extend to 17 or 18 years, leading to substantial additional interest costs. Banks often automatically opt for tenure extension if the EMI increase pushes the new EMI beyond a certain percentage of the original or if it impacts the borrower's debt-to-income ratio significantly.

It's important to note that some banks might offer a combination of both – a slight increase in EMI along with a modest extension in tenure – to balance the impact on the borrower. You should proactively communicate with your bank to understand the options available and choose the one that best suits your financial situation.

A real example

Let's consider Priya, 35, earning ₹22L CTC, based in Gurugram. Priya took a home loan of ₹50 lakhs for 20 years (240 months) at an interest rate of 8.50% per annum.

Her current EMI calculation: Loan Amount (P) = ₹50,00,000 Annual Interest Rate (R) = 8.50% Monthly Interest Rate (r) = 8.50% / 12 / 100 = 0.0070833 Loan Tenure (N) = 240 months

Using the EMI formula: EMI = P r (1 + r)^N / ((1 + r)^N - 1) Priya's current EMI = ₹50,00,000 0.0070833 (1 + 0.0070833)^240 / ((1 + 0.0070833)^240 - 1) Current EMI ≈ ₹43,622

Now, with the RBI repo rate hike of 25 bps, let's assume Priya's bank increases her home loan interest rate by 0.25%, making it 8.75% per annum.

New Monthly Interest Rate (r') = 8.75% / 12 / 100 = 0.0072916

Scenario 1: EMI increases, Tenure remains same (240 months) New EMI = ₹50,00,000 0.0072916 (1 + 0.0072916)^240 / ((1 + 0.0072916)^240 - 1) New EMI ≈ ₹44,300

In this scenario, Priya's EMI increases by ₹44,300 - ₹43,622 = ₹678 per month.

Scenario 2: Tenure increases, EMI remains same (₹43,622) If Priya wants to keep her EMI at ₹43,622, the bank will extend her loan tenure. We need to calculate the new N (number of months) with the new interest rate. Using a loan amortisation calculator or financial software, with P = ₹50,00,000, EMI = ₹43,622, and r' = 0.0072916: New Tenure ≈ 255 months (approximately 21 years and 3 months).

In this scenario, Priya's loan tenure increases by 255 - 240 = 15 months, meaning she will be paying EMIs for over a year longer than originally planned, incurring more overall interest.

What to do this week

  1. Check Your Loan Statement: Locate your latest home loan statement to identify your current interest rate, the benchmark your loan is linked to (EBLR or MCLR), and your remaining loan tenure.
  2. Contact Your Bank: Reach out to your bank's customer service or loan department to understand how the repo rate hike will specifically impact your loan. Ask about the revised interest rate, the new EMI amount, or the extended tenure.
  3. Evaluate Your Cash Flow: Assess your monthly budget to see if you can comfortably absorb a higher EMI. If not, discuss tenure extension options with your bank, but be aware of the increased total interest cost.
  4. Consider Partial Prepayment: If you have surplus funds, even a small partial prepayment can help reduce your principal outstanding, thereby mitigating the impact of the rate hike on your EMI or tenure.
  5. Explore Balance Transfer: If your current bank's revised interest rate is significantly higher than what other banks are offering, research the possibility of a home loan balance transfer to a lender with a more competitive rate. Factor in processing fees and other charges.

FAQ

What is a basis point (bps)?

A basis point is a unit of measure used in finance to denote a percentage change. One basis point is equal to one-hundredth of one percent (0.01%), so 25 basis points equals 0.25%.

Will my home loan interest rate change immediately?

For EBLR-linked loans, the change is usually quite swift, often within the next interest reset cycle. For MCLR-linked loans, the change might take longer, as it depends on your loan's specific reset clause and the bank's internal MCLR review cycle.

Does this hike affect fixed-rate home loans?

No, fixed-rate home loans are not directly impacted by changes in the repo rate as their interest rate is fixed for the entire tenure or a specified period. However, new fixed-rate loans offered by banks may see higher rates.

Can I switch from a floating rate to a fixed-rate loan?

Yes, many banks offer the option to convert your floating rate loan to a fixed-rate loan. However, fixed rates are typically higher than current floating rates, and there might be conversion charges.

Is a home loan balance transfer always a good idea after a rate hike?

A balance transfer can be beneficial if the new bank offers a significantly lower interest rate. However, you must factor in processing fees, legal charges, and the time involved to ensure the savings outweigh these costs.

How often does the RBI change the repo rate?

The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee meets at least six times a year, typically every two months, to review the economic situation and decide on policy rates. Changes can occur at any of these meetings or even off-cycle if conditions warrant.
