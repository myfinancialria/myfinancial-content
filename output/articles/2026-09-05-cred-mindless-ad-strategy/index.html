Business Mavericks · 2026-09-05

The usually composed Rahul Dravid, India's cricketing legend, stood snarling from the sunroof of his car, bat in hand, yelling "Indiranagar ka gunda hoon main!" ("I am a goon from Indiranagar!"). It was April 2021, and the advertisement for CRED, a credit card bill payment app, had just dropped. The internet exploded. Within an hour, the video had garnered a million views, trending far beyond the ongoing IPL matches it was aired during. The sight of "The Wall" losing his cool, smashing a car's rearview mirror, and engaging in road rage was so utterly against his public persona that it was both shocking and instantly viral.

Yet, for many, the ad raised a fundamental question: What exactly was CRED selling? Was this expensive, absurd celebrity endorsement a stroke of genius or a colossal waste of money? The ad, much like CRED's entire marketing strategy, was deliberately designed to be exclusionary and thought-provoking, a calculated gamble that would ultimately redefine premium distribution in India.

The world before

Before CRED's audacious entry, the Indian financial technology (fintech) landscape, particularly concerning credit cards, was largely functional and feature-driven. Banks and payment apps focused on straightforward messaging: convenience, security, cashback, or interest rates. Advertisements typically featured celebrities speaking directly to the camera, explaining product benefits in a clear, often uninspiring manner, ending with a call to action or a product demo. The market was dominated by mass-market platforms vying for volume, with little emphasis on aspirational branding for credit-worthy individuals.

Enter Kunal Shah, the founder of CRED, in 2018. His premise was unique: a significant segment of India's financially responsible consumers—those who consistently paid their bills on time and maintained healthy credit profiles—received no recognition or reward for their discipline. CRED was founded to address this gap, but with a crucial twist: it was a members-only community. To join, users needed a credit score of 750 or higher, a deliberate move that instantly limited access to a thin slice of India's credit-using population. This restriction wasn't a limitation; it was the brand's most powerful differentiator, positioning CRED as an exclusive, premium ecosystem rather than just another utility app. Kunal Shah articulated this vision, aiming to build a "trust brand" rather than a "product brand," emphasizing distinctive brand recall and aspirational positioning over mere product features.

The crack

With its exclusive user base established, CRED needed a marketing strategy that mirrored its audacious product philosophy. In September 2020, as the Indian Premier League (IPL) kicked off, CRED launched its first major campaign: "CRED: Not Everyone Gets It." This series of ads featured iconic Bollywood stars from the 90s, including Anil Kapoor, Madhuri Dixit, and Bappi Lahiri, in hilariously awkward "auditions" for a CRED commercial. The humor lay in their over-the-top, often cringeworthy performances, with CRED employees subtly rejecting them or questioning the need for such celebrity antics. Anil Kapoor famously huffed out of a room, exclaiming he was "too big for these guys," while Madhuri Dixit danced to a nonsensical jingle about OTPs. The campaign poked fun at the very obsession with celebrity advertising that many brands embraced, while subtly reinforcing CRED's "not everyone gets it" tagline.

This unconventional approach escalated in the 2021 IPL season. Following the success of the 2020 campaign, CRED went bigger, featuring a roster of celebrities like Rahul Dravid, Jackie Shroff, Kapil Dev, and Javagal Srinath. The Rahul Dravid "Indiranagar ka Gunda" ad, where the cricketer showcased an uncharacteristic fit of rage, became an instant cultural phenomenon. Jackie Shroff was seen enthusiastically doing Zumba, and former cricketers transformed into a retro boy band called 'Venkaboys'. The common thread was simple: take beloved, often serious, public figures and have them perform activities completely at odds with their established personas. This "absurdist" humor was crafted by a team including stand-up comedian Tanmay Bhat, and was designed to be shareable and break through the clutter of conventional IPL advertising.

The cost of this marketing blitz was substantial. CRED invested approximately ₹120 crore for a three-year affiliation with the IPL, making it an official league sponsor. Beyond this, estimates for overall IPL ad spend ran into hundreds of crores. Total marketing and promotion expenses for CRED were ₹418 crore in FY21 and a staggering ₹976 crore in FY22. Many critics questioned the heavy expenditure, especially given the significant losses the company was incurring.

However, the strategy worked. The virality of the ads was undeniable, largely due to their unexpected nature and the strong nostalgia factor they leveraged by featuring icons from the 90s. The timing of the ad drops, coinciding with the peak viewership of the IPL, ensured maximum eyeballs. The deliberate choice to make the ads confusing or "useless" in explaining the product was a feature, not a bug. It sparked conversations, memes, and an undeniable curiosity about the brand. Kunal Shah himself argued that CRED was building enduring brand equity and trust, comparing the approach to luxury brand building rather than direct-response marketing.

What happened next

The immediate aftermath of these campaigns was a significant surge in user acquisition. Daily sign-ups for the CRED app jumped six to seven times, and app downloads spiked by eight times. The brand recall was exceptional.

Financially, the market responded positively to this growth. By April 2021, shortly after the Rahul Dravid campaign, CRED's valuation hit $2.2 billion, making it India's fastest unicorn at the time. By the end of 2021 or early 2022, its valuation further soared to $6.4 billion.

While marketing expenses were high (₹418 crore in FY21 against ₹95.5 crore revenue, and ₹976 crore in FY22 against ₹394 crore revenue, leading to substantial losses), investors bought into the long-term vision. The bet was that the strong brand equity and the rapidly growing user base of affluent, creditworthy individuals would eventually monetize through a diverse ecosystem of lending, insurance, and commerce. By April 2021, CRED was processing approximately 20% of all credit card bill payments in India, a testament to its penetration into the premium segment.

Lessons to take away

  1. Exclusivity as a Potent Differentiator: In a crowded market, CRED carved a niche by deliberately restricting its user base to those with high credit scores. This wasn't about mass appeal but about cultivating an aspirational, premium identity that made membership a status symbol.
  2. The Power of Absurdist, Shareable Content: CRED proved that ads don't always need to explicitly explain a product's features. By focusing on humor, unexpected celebrity portrayals, and cultural resonance, they created content that viewers wanted to share, generating organic buzz and curiosity far beyond traditional advertising reach.
  3. Strategic Subversion of Celebrity Endorsement: Instead of using celebrities as talking heads, CRED leveraged their established personas by having them act completely out of character. This "playing against type" created memorable, talkable campaigns that resonated deeply due to the inherent humor and surprise.
  4. Long-term Brand Building Over Short-term ROI: Kunal Shah's philosophy of building a "trust brand" akin to luxury brands, rather than chasing immediate transactional returns, underpinned the heavy investment in seemingly "useless" ads. This long-game approach focused on creating deep brand equity that could be leveraged for diverse monetization avenues later.
  5. Mastering Cultural Moments for Distribution: Aligning massive advertising spends with high-visibility events like the IPL allowed CRED's viral campaigns to reach an unparalleled audience. The timing ensured that the conversation around the ads became an integral part of the broader cultural moment.
