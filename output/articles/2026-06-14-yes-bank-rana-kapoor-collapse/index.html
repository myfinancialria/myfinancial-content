Yes Bank: How Rana Kapoor Built India's Fastest-Growing Bank Then Personally Destroyed It

The morning of March 8, 2020, dawned with a jolt across India's financial landscape. Rana Kapoor, the flamboyant co-founder and former CEO of Yes Bank, was arrested by the Enforcement Directorate (ED) in Mumbai after over 20 hours of questioning. The arrest, under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), sent tremors through an already panicking depositor base. Just days before, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) had placed Yes Bank under a moratorium, capping withdrawals at ₹50,000 per account and superseding its board. The queues outside Yes Bank branches were a stark visual of shattered trust, a dramatic collapse for a bank that had once epitomized rapid private sector growth.

The World Before: A Cautious Banking Landscape

In the early 2000s, India's banking sector was a different beast. Public sector banks dominated, often perceived as slow-moving and bureaucratic. The entry of new private banks was a measured affair, requiring careful navigation of regulatory frameworks and significant capital. It was a landscape where trust was paramount, and aggressive, high-risk strategies were viewed with suspicion. Starting a new private bank was an ambitious, almost audacious, undertaking. Existing private players like HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank had grown steadily, but a newcomer needed a distinct vision and unwavering execution to carve out a niche.

Rana Kapoor, a former Bank of America executive, harbored such an ambition. In 2003, along with his brother-in-law Ashok Kapur, he received a banking license from the RBI to establish Yes Bank. Their vision was to build a "Knowledge Bank," leveraging sectoral expertise alongside capital, particularly for emerging industries often underserved by the larger incumbents. Yes Bank commenced operations in 2004, opening its first branch in Mumbai, and quickly followed with an initial public offering (IPO).

The Crack: Ambition, Aggression, and Accumulation of Risk

Under Rana Kapoor's leadership as Managing Director and CEO, Yes Bank embarked on an extraordinary growth trajectory. In just 14 years, it transformed from a fledgling entity to India's fifth-largest private lender by 2017, with its balance sheet expanding at an impressive pace. Kapoor's net worth even crossed $1 billion in January 2017, largely due to his stake in the rapidly surging bank. This was the era of "Yes Bank saying Yes," a slogan that reflected its willingness to lend where others hesitated.

However, this aggressive growth was not without its shadows. The RBI, in its supervisory role, soon began to notice what it termed "unnatural" banking practices. Kapoor, keen on rapidly expanding the bank's loan book, allegedly began extending credit to borrowers that other, more conservative banks wouldn't touch – often companies in stressed sectors like real estate or those already defaulting on loans from other lenders. The bank's gross Non-Performing Assets (NPAs), essentially bad loans, began to mount.

A key concern was the alleged "evergreening" of loans. This practice involved extending fresh loans to struggling borrowers to enable them to repay older debts, thus masking the true extent of the bank's stressed assets. This allowed Yes Bank to maintain an artificially healthy balance sheet and report profits, keeping shareholders happy.

The rot became increasingly evident through its exposure to several high-profile corporate groups that were themselves teetering on the brink. By March 2020, major defaulters included Anil Ambani's Reliance Group, which owed Yes Bank approximately ₹12,800 crore; Subhash Chandra's Essel Group, with dues of around ₹8,400 crore; and Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Ltd (DHFL), which accounted for ₹4,735 crore in bad loans. These three groups alone constituted over ₹20,000 crore of Yes Bank's stressed assets. Loans were also extended to other struggling entities like IL&FS, Jet Airways, and Cafe Coffee Day, further exacerbating the bank's risk profile.

The RBI's asset quality reviews in 2017 and 2018 exposed significant under-reporting of NPAs by Yes Bank. The divergence between the bank's reported bad loans and the RBI's assessment widened to almost $1 billion a year later. Recognizing severe governance lapses and accumulating risk, the RBI intervened, refusing to extend Kapoor's term as CEO beyond January 2019.

The investigations that followed painted a grim picture of alleged quid pro quo arrangements. The ED and CBI alleged that Kapoor and his family members received kickbacks, in some cases amounting to ₹43 billion (US$450 million), for sanctioning huge loans that subsequently turned into NPAs. A notable instance involved Yes Bank's investment in DHFL's debentures, allegedly followed by DHFL providing a ₹600 crore loan to Do It Urban Ventures Pvt. Ltd., a company beneficially owned by Kapoor and his daughters, against "substandard properties". This intricate web of transactions, spanning over 100 firms, was allegedly used to siphon off funds and acquire properties overseas.

What Happened Next: Rescue, Repercussions, and Rebuilding

The unraveling culminated in the RBI's drastic action on March 5, 2020, imposing a moratorium on Yes Bank and limiting withdrawals to ₹50,000, triggering widespread panic. The bank's stock price, which had reached a lifetime high of ₹393.85 in August 2018, plummeted, losing over 92% of its value by October 2019, and crashing further during the crisis.

To prevent a systemic crisis, the Indian government and RBI orchestrated a rescue plan. On March 14, 2020, the State Bank of India (SBI) led a ₹10,000 crore (approximately US$1.34 billion) bailout, acquiring a 49% stake in Yes Bank. Other private sector banks, including HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank, and Kotak Mahindra Bank, also participated in the capital infusion. A new management team, led by Prashant Kumar as MD & CEO, was brought in to stabilize the bank.

Rana Kapoor was arrested on March 8, 2020, on charges of money laundering and fraud. He was subsequently charged in multiple cases related to fraud and corruption at the bank. After four years in custody, Kapoor was granted bail in April 2024.

The rescue operation included a controversial write-down of Yes Bank's Additional Tier-1 (AT-1) bonds worth ₹8,415 crore, impacting numerous mutual funds and retail investors. However, the new management focused on cleaning up the balance sheet, selling stressed loan portfolios worth ₹480 billion to JC Flowers Asset Reconstruction Company and recovering over ₹5,000 crore of bad loans. By March 2022, Yes Bank reported full-year profitability for the first time since the restructuring. The bank's gross NPA ratio dropped significantly from 16-17% in 2020 to 1.6% by March 2025, with net NPA falling to 0.3%.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Growth at All Costs is a Red Flag: Yes Bank's meteoric rise was fueled by aggressive lending, particularly to high-risk borrowers and stressed sectors. While rapid growth can be enticing, it often masks underlying vulnerabilities when not accompanied by robust risk management and due diligence. The pursuit of ever-higher numbers without a sound foundation proved to be the bank's undoing.
  2. Governance is the Bedrock of Trust: The story of Yes Bank under Rana Kapoor highlights a catastrophic failure of corporate governance. The alleged misuse of official position, personal enrichment through kickbacks, and the creation of a complex web of shell companies corroded the bank's integrity. In finance, where trust is currency, such breaches can lead to rapid and irreversible collapse.
  3. Regulatory Scrutiny is Crucial, Yet Challenging: The RBI's repeated asset quality reviews eventually exposed Yes Bank's hidden NPAs, and its intervention forced Kapoor's exit. This underscores the critical role of regulators in maintaining financial stability. However, the extended period during which these practices allegedly continued also highlights the challenges regulators face in detecting and acting upon complex financial misconduct, especially when it is actively concealed.
  4. Concentrated Exposures Magnify Risk: Yes Bank's significant concentration of loans to a few large, struggling corporate groups proved to be a fatal flaw. When these major borrowers defaulted, the impact on the bank's overall health was devastating. Diversification across sectors and borrowers is a fundamental principle of sound banking, designed to mitigate such systemic risks.
