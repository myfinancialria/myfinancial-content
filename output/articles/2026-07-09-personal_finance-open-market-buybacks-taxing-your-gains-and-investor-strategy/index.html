Open-Market Buybacks: Taxing Your Gains and Investor Strategy

TL;DR

  • SEBI has reintroduced open-market share buybacks, effective August 1, 2026, allowing companies to repurchase their shares via stock exchanges.
  • Unlike tender offer buybacks, gains from open-market buybacks are taxed as capital gains for the investor, not as a Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT) for the company.
  • Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG) are taxed at your applicable slab rate, while Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) over ₹1 lakh are taxed at 10% without indexation.
  • Investors should weigh the buyback price against their acquisition cost, holding period, and future prospects of the company before selling.
  • The reintroduction aims to improve market efficiency and provide companies with more flexibility in capital allocation.

What this means in plain terms

Imagine a company wants to buy back its own shares from the market. Until recently, SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) had restricted this to a "tender offer" route, where the company directly makes an offer to shareholders at a fixed price. Now, SEBI has brought back the "open market" route, effective August 1, 2026. This means companies can simply buy their shares directly from the stock exchange, just like any other investor would.

For you, as an investor, this change primarily impacts how your profits from selling shares in such a buyback are taxed. When a company buys back shares through a tender offer, the company itself pays a tax (previously Dividend Distribution Tax, now a specific tax under Section 115QA of the Income Tax Act) on the amount paid to shareholders. However, with open-market buybacks, any profit you make by selling your shares to the company through the exchange will be treated as a capital gain in your hands, subject to your individual tax rates.

Understanding Open Market Buybacks vs. Tender Offers

Historically, companies in India had two primary methods for share buybacks: the tender offer route and the open market route. SEBI had previously restricted the open market route due to concerns about price manipulation and transparency. The recent notification by SEBI reinstates the open market mechanism, providing companies with greater flexibility.

Tender Offer Buybacks

In a tender offer, a company announces a specific price at which it intends to buy back shares and invites shareholders to "tender" or offer their shares for sale. This process usually involves a fixed window and a pro-rata acceptance ratio, meaning if more shares are offered than the company intends to buy, only a proportion of each shareholder's tendered shares will be accepted. For investors, the gains from tender offer buybacks are exempt from tax in their hands, as the company is liable to pay tax under Section 115QA of the Income Tax Act, 1961. This tax is levied on the distributed income (the difference between the buyback price and the issue price of the shares).

Open Market Buybacks

With the reintroduction of the open market route, companies can now buy back their shares directly from the stock exchange. This is similar to how a regular investor buys shares. The company places orders on the exchange, and any shareholder willing to sell at the prevailing market price can do so. This method offers flexibility to the company in terms of timing and price, as it can accumulate shares over a period. However, for investors, the key difference lies in taxation. Any profit realised from selling shares in an open market buyback is treated as a capital gain for the individual investor.

Taxation of Gains from Open Market Buybacks

The tax implications for an investor selling shares in an open market buyback are straightforward: they are treated as capital gains. The type of capital gain – short-term or long-term – depends on your holding period of the shares.

Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG)

If you sell shares within 12 months of acquiring them, any profit you make is considered a Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG). This STCG is added to your total income and taxed at your applicable income tax slab rate. For instance, if you are in the 30% tax bracket, your STCG will be taxed at 30% (plus surcharge and cess, if applicable).

Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG)

If you sell shares after holding them for more than 12 months, any profit is considered a Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG). Under current income tax laws (Section 112A of the Income Tax Act, 1961), LTCG from the sale of equity shares listed on a recognised stock exchange is taxed at a concessional rate. The first ₹1 lakh of LTCG in a financial year is exempt from tax. Any LTCG exceeding ₹1 lakh is taxed at a flat rate of 10% (plus surcharge and cess, if applicable), without the benefit of indexation.

It's important to note that the Securities Transaction Tax (STT) would have already been paid at the time of purchase and sale of these shares on the stock exchange, which is a prerequisite for availing the concessional LTCG tax rate.

Investor Considerations Before Participating

Deciding whether to sell your shares in an open market buyback requires careful thought. It's not just about the immediate profit but also about your long-term investment strategy and the company's future prospects.

Evaluating the Buyback Price

The most immediate factor is the price at which the company is buying back shares. In an open market buyback, this will be the prevailing market price. You need to compare this price against your original acquisition cost. If the market price offers a significant premium over your cost, it might be an attractive exit point, especially if you have held the shares for a long time and accumulated substantial gains. Conversely, if the market price is close to or below your cost, participating might not be beneficial unless you have specific reasons to exit.

Your Holding Period and Tax Implications

As discussed, your holding period directly impacts the tax treatment. If you have held shares for less than 12 months, your gains will be taxed at your slab rate. If you have held them for over 12 months, you benefit from the ₹1 lakh exemption and the 10% LTCG tax rate. Consider the net-of-tax return before making a decision. Sometimes, deferring the sale to qualify for LTCG can be more beneficial than an immediate STCG.

Company Fundamentals and Future Outlook

A buyback can signal that the company believes its shares are undervalued or that it has excess cash it wishes to return to shareholders. However, it's crucial to assess the underlying fundamentals of the company. Is the buyback a one-off event, or does it reflect a sustainable capital allocation strategy? Are there better alternative investment opportunities, or do you believe the company's shares will appreciate further in the long run? Selling shares means you forgo future potential gains and dividends from that company.

Liquidity and Portfolio Rebalancing

Open market buybacks can offer enhanced liquidity for shareholders looking to exit their positions. This can be particularly useful for large shareholders or those holding illiquid stocks. It also presents an opportunity to rebalance your portfolio. If a particular stock has grown to represent an oversized portion of your portfolio, a buyback can be a good way to trim your exposure and diversify into other assets that align better with your risk profile and financial goals.

A real example

Let's consider Priya, 42, ₹35L CTC, Mumbai. Priya purchased 500 shares of 'Tech Innovations Ltd.' at ₹1,200 per share on January 10, 2024. The total investment was ₹6,00,000.

On September 15, 2026, Tech Innovations Ltd. announces an open-market buyback program. Priya decides to sell all her 500 shares at the prevailing market price of ₹1,800 per share.

Calculation of Capital Gain:

  1. Sale Value: 500 shares * ₹1,800/share = ₹9,00,000
  2. Acquisition Cost: 500 shares * ₹1,200/share = ₹6,00,000
  3. Gross Capital Gain: ₹9,00,000 - ₹6,00,000 = ₹3,00,000

Holding Period: Priya bought shares on January 10, 2024, and sold them on September 15, 2026. This is more than 12 months, so it qualifies as a Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG).

Tax Calculation (LTCG):

  1. Exempt LTCG: The first ₹1,00,000 of LTCG in a financial year is exempt.
  2. Taxable LTCG: ₹3,00,000 (Gross Gain) - ₹1,00,000 (Exemption) = ₹2,00,000
  3. LTCG Tax Rate: 10% (under Section 112A of the Income Tax Act, 1961).
  4. Tax Payable: 10% of ₹2,00,000 = ₹20,000

Additionally, a 4% health and education cess would apply to the tax amount. Total Tax Payable: ₹20,000 + (4% of ₹20,000) = ₹20,000 + ₹800 = ₹20,800.

Priya would need to report this LTCG in her Income Tax Return and pay the calculated tax.

What to do this week

  1. Identify your holdings: Review your stock portfolio to identify any companies that might be candidates for future buybacks or have announced open-market buyback plans.
  2. Track your acquisition costs: Ensure you have accurate records of the purchase price and date for all your equity holdings, as this is crucial for calculating capital gains and determining the holding period.
  3. Understand your tax slab: Familiarise yourself with your current income tax slab rate to estimate the potential tax liability on Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG) from any share sales.
  4. Review company announcements: Keep an eye on news and corporate announcements from companies you hold shares in, especially regarding any buyback intentions or programmes.
  5. Consult a tax professional: If you anticipate significant gains from selling shares in a buyback, consider speaking with a financial advisor or tax consultant to understand the specific implications for your tax situation.

FAQ

What is an open-market share buyback?

An open-market share buyback is when a company repurchases its own shares directly from the stock exchange, similar to how a regular investor buys shares. This contrasts with a tender offer, where the company makes a direct offer to shareholders.

How does SEBI's reintroduction of open-market buybacks affect me?

The reintroduction, effective August 1, 2026, primarily means that if you sell shares in such a buyback, any gains will be taxed as capital gains in your hands, either short-term or long-term, depending on your holding period.

What are the tax implications of selling shares in an open-market buyback?

Gains from selling shares in an open-market buyback are treated as capital gains. Short-Term Capital Gains (held for ≤ 12 months) are taxed at your slab rate. Long-Term Capital Gains (held for > 12 months) over ₹1 lakh are taxed at 10% without indexation.

Is there a difference in taxation between open-market and tender offer buybacks?

Yes. In tender offer buybacks, the company pays a tax (under Section 115QA), making the gains tax-exempt for the investor. In open-market buybacks, the investor is liable for capital gains tax.

Should I sell my shares if a company announces an open-market buyback?

The decision depends on factors like the buyback price relative to your cost, your holding period, your tax situation, and your long-term view of the company's prospects. It's essential to evaluate these aspects before making a choice.

Will open-market buybacks lead to market manipulation?

SEBI has put in place regulations to prevent market manipulation, such as limiting the daily purchase quantity and the overall duration of the buyback program. These measures aim to ensure fair play and transparency.
