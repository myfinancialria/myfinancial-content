First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local chai shop that has been doing very well. Now, the owners want to expand, maybe open more branches or buy new equipment. To raise money for this growth, they decide to sell a small part of their shop's ownership to the public.

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is exactly this – it's when a private company offers its shares to the public for the very first time. This is called the primary market because it's the first sale of these shares. Once these shares are bought by the public, they get listed on a stock exchange (like NSE or BSE), meaning they can then be bought and sold by anyone in the open market.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, there are a few key pieces of information to understand:

  1. Price Band: This is the range of prices at which the company is willing to sell its shares. For example, if the price band is ₹130-₹140, you can bid at any price within this range. The company usually picks a final price within this band after the bidding closes.
  2. Lot Size: This is the minimum number of shares you must apply for. You cannot apply for just one share; you have to apply in multiples of the lot size. For example, if the lot size is 100 shares, you must apply for 100, 200, 300 shares, and so on. (Note: Lot size is not yet announced for the current IPOs).
  3. Minimum Investment: This is the smallest amount of money you need to invest to apply for an IPO. It's calculated by multiplying the lot size by the lowest price in the price band. For instance, if the lot size was 100 shares and the lowest price was ₹130, your minimum investment would be ₹13,000 (100 shares * ₹130).
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public through the IPO. It tells you how many shares are available for subscription.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS):

    6. * Fresh Issue: When a company issues new shares, and the money raised goes directly to the company. This money is typically used for expansion, debt repayment, or other business needs. * Offer-for-Sale (OFS): When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares. The money from an OFS goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself.

  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the detailed official document that a company files with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) before an IPO. It contains comprehensive information about the company, its business, financials, risks, management, and how the IPO proceeds will be used. Reading this document helps you understand the company thoroughly.

The IPO process, step by step

Here's how a company goes from being private to publicly traded:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI. This document has all the details about the company and its IPO plans.
  2. SEBI Approval & RHP: After SEBI reviews the DRHP and gives its approval, the company releases the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP). This document includes the confirmed price band and other final details.
  3. Bidding Period: This is when the IPO is "open" to the public. Investors can place their bids for shares within the specified price band and lot size. There are different categories of investors:

    4. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Ordinary individual investors who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs): Investors who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.

  4. Allotment: After the bidding closes, the company and its bankers decide on the final share price and how to distribute the shares among the applicants. If the IPO is oversubscribed (more demand than available shares), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
  5. Listing: Once shares are allotted, they are listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE). This means they can now be freely bought and sold by anyone in the open market, and their price will fluctuate based on demand and supply.

A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a school fete stall. The school (company) wants to raise money for new books (expansion). They decide to sell "ownership tickets" (shares) for their stall to parents and students (the public). They set a price range for these tickets (price band) and say you must buy at least a small bundle of tickets (lot size). Once all the tickets are sold and distributed (allotment), anyone who bought a ticket can then trade it with others at the fete (listing), and the price might go up or down depending on how popular the stall is.

Things people get confused about

  • Myth: Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares.

    • * Fact: If an IPO is very popular and receives many applications (oversubscribed), the demand for shares is higher than the supply. In such cases, shares are allotted through a lottery system, especially for retail investors. So, allotment is not guaranteed.

  • Myth: Listing gain (shares immediately going up in price) is guaranteed.

    • * Fact: While some IPOs do see their share prices rise on the first day of listing, this is not always the case. Share prices can also fall below the IPO price, leading to a loss. Market conditions and company performance play a big role.

  • Myth: Applying for many lots increases your chances of getting more shares as a retail investor.

    • * Fact: In the retail category, if an IPO is oversubscribed, each successful applicant typically gets a minimum of one lot. Applying for multiple lots in the same name does not increase your chances of getting more than one lot; it might even lead to your application being rejected.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's net banking facility using the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) process, or through your stockbroker's platform.

What is a lot size?

The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for in an IPO. You can only apply in multiples of this number.

What is a price band?

The price band is the fixed range (e.g., ₹130 to ₹140) within which you can place your bid for the shares during the IPO subscription period.

What is an RHP?

An RHP (Red Herring Prospectus) is a detailed legal document filed by a company with SEBI, providing comprehensive information about the company, its financials, risks, and the IPO itself.

Can I sell my shares on the first day of listing?

Yes, once your allotted shares are listed on the stock exchange, you are free to sell them at the prevailing market price on the very first day of trading.
