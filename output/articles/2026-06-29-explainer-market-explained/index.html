Title: "Now, More Foreigners Can Directly Invest in Indian Shares: What Changed?"

TL;DR:

  • India has opened its stock market wider to individual investors living abroad.
  • Earlier, only NRIs and OCIs could directly buy listed Indian shares easily.
  • Now, any individual person living outside India can invest, not just those of Indian origin.
  • This change, via amended FEMA rules, could bring more foreign money into Indian companies.
  • Limits for individual foreign holdings have also been increased.

What's this about? The Indian government has recently made a big change to how people living outside India can invest in our stock market. Until now, if you were an individual living abroad and wanted to directly buy shares of Indian companies listed on our stock exchanges, it was mostly limited to Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs). But with new rules, this door has opened much wider.

Now, any "individual person resident outside India" can invest in listed Indian equity instruments. This means foreigners, who are not of Indian origin, can also directly participate in the Indian stock market, subject to certain conditions.

Why it's in the news right now This is big news because the Central Government, through the Department of Economic Affairs, notified amendments to the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019, on June 12, 2026. These new rules came into effect immediately upon their publication. This move broadens access to Indian equities, potentially attracting a larger pool of global retail investors and high-net-worth individuals, and reducing the market's reliance on only institutional foreign flows. The Finance Minister had also announced this in the Union Budget FY2026-27.

How it actually works Here's a breakdown of how this new system allows more foreign individuals to invest:

  1. Who can invest? Earlier, the specific rules for individual investment in listed Indian companies through the portfolio investment scheme were mainly for Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs). Now, this has been expanded to include any "individual person resident outside India." This means a person of any nationality living abroad can now invest directly.
  2. Which investments? These individuals can purchase or sell equity instruments (like shares) of listed Indian companies and other securities on a repatriation basis. "Repatriation basis" means they can take their investment money, and any profits earned, back to their home country.
  3. Investment Limits: The new rules also increased the maximum holding an individual person resident outside India can have in a listed Indian company. It has been raised to less than 10% of the total paid-up equity capital of that company, up from 5% earlier. The total holding by all such individual persons resident outside India put together in an Indian company cannot exceed 24% of its total paid-up equity capital, up from 10% previously.
  4. Regulatory Framework: These changes are part of amendments to the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 (FEMA NDI Rules). FEMA is a law in India that manages foreign exchange and foreign investments.
  5. Conditions Apply: While access is broadened, safeguards remain. For instance, investments that could lead to a transfer of ownership or control to entities or citizens of countries sharing a land border with India will still require prior government approval. Also, if an individual's holding breaches the 10% limit, they will need to sell the excess shares within five trading days.

A simple way to think about it Imagine a popular sweet shop in your town. Earlier, only family members (NRIs/OCIs) and very big, established sweet distributors (institutional foreign investors) could buy sweets directly from the shop owner. Other people (foreign individuals) had to buy through these distributors, which was a bit indirect.

Now, the shop owner has put up a new sign saying, "Anyone can buy directly from us!" This means more customers can come directly to the shop, increasing sales and making the shop more vibrant. The shop owner has also increased how many sweets each new customer can buy, and how many all new customers together can buy. This makes it easier for more people to enjoy the sweets and for the shop to grow.

Why it matters to you

  1. More Money for Indian Companies: With more foreign individuals able to invest, Indian companies could find it easier to raise capital. This money can be used for expansion, creating jobs, and boosting economic growth.
  2. Potentially Stronger Markets: A broader base of foreign investors can lead to more stable and liquid stock markets. This means shares might be easier to buy and sell, and the market might be less dependent on a few large institutional investors.
  3. Increased Global Interest in India: This move signals India's commitment to opening its capital markets, potentially enhancing its image as an attractive investment destination globally.
  4. No Direct Impact on Your Ability to Invest: As an Indian resident, your ability to invest in Indian stocks remains the same. This change is specifically for individuals residing outside India.
  5. Indirect Economic Benefits: If foreign investment increases and companies grow, it can indirectly lead to more job opportunities and a stronger economy, which benefits everyone.

Common confusions cleared up

  • Myth: This means Indian citizens can now directly invest in foreign stocks.

    • Fact: No, this change is for individuals residing outside India to invest in listed Indian companies*. It does not allow Indian residents to directly invest in foreign stocks under this specific amendment.

  • Myth: All foreign investment in India is now completely free with no limits.

    • * Fact: While access is broadened and limits are increased, there are still important conditions and caps. For example, an individual foreign investor cannot hold 10% or more of a company's paid-up capital, and collective individual foreign holdings are capped at 24%. Also, investments from countries sharing a land border with India still require prior government approval.

  • Myth: This will only benefit very large companies.

    • * Fact: While large-cap stocks might attract significant interest, the broader access can potentially benefit a range of listed Indian companies by diversifying their investor base and increasing liquidity.

FAQ

Can Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) still invest in Indian stocks?

Yes, NRIs and Overseas Citizens of India (OCIs) were already permitted to invest in listed Indian companies, and they continue to be able to do so. This new rule simply expands the definition to include all individuals residing outside India.

What are "equity instruments"?

Equity instruments primarily refer to shares of a company, which represent ownership. When you buy equity instruments, you become a part-owner of the company.

What does "repatriation basis" mean?

"Repatriation basis" means that the foreign investor is allowed to take their initial investment amount and any profits or capital gains earned from that investment back to their home country in foreign currency.

Are there any restrictions on which Indian companies foreign individuals can invest in?

Yes, investments are subject to prohibited activities and sectoral caps as prescribed under the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-Debt Instruments) Rules. For instance, certain sectors might have limits on foreign investment.

Will this affect the value of the Indian Rupee?

The government hopes that increased foreign capital inflows due to these changes will help strengthen the Indian Rupee and stabilize the capital account, especially during times of global economic volatility.
