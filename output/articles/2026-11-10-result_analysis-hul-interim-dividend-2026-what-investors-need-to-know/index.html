What this means in plain terms

When a company like Hindustan Unilever (HUL) performs well, it often shares a portion of its profits with its shareholders in the form of a dividend. This is essentially a reward for owning a part of the company. An "interim dividend" means it's paid out during the financial year, usually before the final annual results are declared. HUL announced this specific interim dividend alongside its September-quarter results on October 28.

For investors, understanding the dividend announcement involves a few key dates. The "record date" is a specific date set by the company, and anyone whose name appears in the company's records as a shareholder on that date is eligible to receive the dividend. However, due to stock market settlement cycles (T+1 in India), you typically need to buy the shares at least one trading day before the "ex-dividend date" to ensure they are credited to your demat account by the record date. The ex-dividend date is usually one business day before the record date.

The declared dividend of ₹19 per share means that for every share of HUL stock you own, the company will pay you ₹19. This is a significant payout, representing 1,900% of the share's face value of ₹1. Such payouts are attractive to investors looking for regular income from their stock holdings, alongside potential capital appreciation.

Understanding Dividend Yield and HUL's History

Dividends are a key component of total returns for many long-term investors. Understanding dividend yield and a company's dividend history can provide valuable insights into its financial health and investor-friendly policies.

Calculating Dividend Yield

Dividend yield is a financial ratio that shows how much a company pays out in dividends each year relative to its stock price. It's calculated as:

Dividend Yield = (Annual Dividend Per Share / Current Share Price) * 100

For example, if HUL's annual dividend (including interim and final) were ₹25 and its share price was ₹2,500, the dividend yield would be (₹25 / ₹2,500) * 100 = 1%. A higher dividend yield generally means a larger income stream relative to the stock price, though it's important to consider the sustainability of such payouts.

Taxation of Dividends in India

For Indian residents, dividends received from domestic companies like HUL are taxable in the hands of the shareholder. This is a significant change from previous regulations where companies paid Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT).

How Dividends are Taxed

As per current income tax laws in India, dividends are added to the investor's total income and taxed at their applicable income tax slab rates. This means if you are in the 30% tax bracket, your dividend income will also be taxed at 30%.

There are no specific exemptions for dividend income for individual investors, except for certain situations like dividends received from specific international funds or within certain thresholds for non-residents, which are subject to different rules.

TDS on Dividends

Companies are required to deduct Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) on dividend payments if the aggregate dividend paid to a resident shareholder exceeds ₹5,000 in a financial year. The TDS rate is typically 10% under Section 194 of the Income Tax Act. If the shareholder does not provide a PAN, the TDS rate can be higher, generally 20%.

Investors can claim credit for the TDS deducted by the company while filing their income tax returns. It's crucial for shareholders to ensure their PAN is updated with their demat account provider to avoid higher TDS deductions. For specific details and the latest updates, investors should always refer to the official Income Tax Department website.

Ex-Dividend Date vs. Record Date: Why it Matters

Understanding the difference between the ex-dividend date and the record date is crucial for investors who wish to receive a dividend. Missing these dates can mean missing out on the payout.

The Record Date

The record date is the date on which a company's registrar checks its records to determine who the shareholders are. Only those shareholders whose names appear on the company's register on this specific date are eligible to receive the declared dividend. This is the official cut-off for dividend entitlement.

The Ex-Dividend Date

The ex-dividend date, often referred to as the "ex-date," is typically one business day before the record date in India due to the T+1 settlement cycle for equities. If you buy a stock on or after its ex-dividend date, you will generally not be entitled to the upcoming dividend payment. Conversely, if you buy the stock before the ex-dividend date, you will be eligible for the dividend.

The stock price often adjusts downwards by roughly the dividend amount on the ex-dividend date, reflecting that new buyers will not receive the dividend. Therefore, the "last day to buy" shares to be eligible for a dividend is the trading day immediately preceding the ex-dividend date. This distinction is vital for timing investment decisions around dividend announcements.

A real example

Let's consider Anjali, 42, a software professional with an annual CTC of ₹35 lakhs, residing in Gurugram. Anjali holds 500 shares of HUL in her demat account.

When HUL announced its interim dividend of ₹19 per share, Anjali wanted to calculate her expected dividend income.

Here's the calculation:

  • Number of HUL shares held: 500 shares
  • Dividend per share: ₹19
  • Total dividend income: 500 shares * ₹19/share = ₹9,500

Now, let's consider the tax implications for Anjali. Assuming Anjali falls into the highest income tax bracket (30% plus cess and surcharge, if applicable), her dividend income of ₹9,500 will be added to her total taxable income.

If HUL deducts TDS at 10% (assuming PAN is updated), they would deduct ₹950 (10% of ₹9,500) and pay Anjali ₹8,550. When Anjali files her income tax return, she would declare the full ₹9,500 as dividend income. The actual tax liability on this income, considering her slab, would be higher than the TDS deducted. For instance, if her effective tax rate is 31.2% (30% + 4% cess), the tax on ₹9,500 would be approximately ₹2,964. She would then pay the balance tax amount (₹2,964 - ₹950 = ₹2,014) when filing her returns.

This example illustrates that while dividends provide income, their net value is influenced by an individual's tax slab and applicable TDS.

FAQ

What is an interim dividend?

An interim dividend is a dividend paid by a company during its financial year, before the final annual results are declared. It's usually based on the company's performance in the first half or three quarters of the year.

How is HUL's dividend of 1,900% of face value calculated?

The face value of HUL's equity share is ₹1. A dividend of 1,900% means 19 times the face value. So, 19 * ₹1 = ₹19 per equity share.

Do I need to pay tax on HUL's dividend?

Yes, for resident Indian individuals, dividend income is added to your total income and taxed at your applicable income tax slab rates. TDS may also be deducted by the company if the dividend exceeds ₹5,000 in a financial year.

What happens if I buy HUL shares on the ex-dividend date?

If you buy HUL shares on or after the ex-dividend date, you will not be eligible to receive the announced interim dividend. You must purchase the shares before the ex-dividend date.

When will the HUL dividend be credited to my account?

The company typically announces a payment date along with the dividend declaration and record date. Dividends are usually credited directly to the bank account linked to your demat account within 30 days of the declaration.
