Article 265: Can Government Levy Taxes Without a Law?

TL;DR

  • Article 265 of the Indian Constitution mandates that no tax can be imposed or collected by the government without a valid law.
  • This constitutional provision acts as a fundamental safeguard against arbitrary taxation by any government authority.
  • Every tax, whether by the Central or State government, must be backed by legislation passed by Parliament or a State Legislature.
  • It ensures that citizens are protected from taxes that are not duly approved through the democratic legislative process.
  • Understanding Article 265 empowers taxpayers to question any tax demand lacking proper legal backing.

What this means in plain terms

In India, the government cannot simply decide to collect a new tax or increase an existing one on a whim. Every single rupee collected as tax, whether it's income tax, GST, or any other levy, must have a clear legal basis. This foundational principle is enshrined in Article 265 of the Indian Constitution.

Essentially, Article 265 states that for any tax to be valid, it must be authorised by a law passed by either the Parliament (for central taxes) or the respective State Legislature (for state taxes). This means that without a proper legislative act, no government body, be it central, state, or even a local municipality, has the power to demand or collect taxes from citizens. It's a crucial check on governmental power, ensuring transparency and accountability in taxation.

The Constitutional Mandate: Article 265 Explained

Article 265 of the Constitution of India is a cornerstone of India's tax framework, explicitly stating: "No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law." This short yet powerful sentence establishes a critical limitation on the executive power of both the Union and State governments. It ensures that the power to tax, which is inherent in any sovereign state, is exercised only within the bounds of democratic legislative approval.

Why Article 265 is Crucial

The primary purpose of Article 265 is to prevent arbitrary taxation. Historically, rulers often imposed taxes at their discretion, leading to exploitation and lack of predictability. The framers of the Indian Constitution, drawing lessons from such historical contexts, ensured that citizens would be protected from such arbitrary levies. It guarantees that any financial burden placed on individuals or businesses through taxation must first pass through the rigorous process of legislative debate, scrutiny, and approval. This constitutional safeguard is fundamental to maintaining a fair and just tax system.

The Legislative Process for Taxation

For any tax to be legally valid in India, it must originate from a legislative act. This involves a specific process, depending on whether the tax falls under the Union List, State List, or Concurrent List as defined in the Seventh Schedule of the Constitution.

Central Taxes and Parliament

For taxes that fall under the Union List (e.g., Income Tax, Corporation Tax, Customs Duty, Central GST), the law must be passed by the Parliament of India. This typically happens through a Finance Bill, which is introduced by the Union Finance Minister as part of the annual budget presentation. The Finance Bill, after being passed by both the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha and receiving presidential assent, becomes the Finance Act, which then amends or introduces tax laws. For instance, the Income Tax Act, 1961, derives its authority from parliamentary legislation.

State Taxes and State Legislatures

Similarly, for taxes that fall under the State List (e.g., State GST, Stamp Duty, Land Revenue, Professional Tax), the law must be passed by the respective State Legislature. State governments introduce their own finance bills or specific tax bills, which, upon passing by the state assembly and receiving the Governor's assent, become state laws. For example, the various State GST Acts are products of state legislative processes.

Beyond Formal Legislation

It's important to note that while notifications, circulars, and rules are issued by the executive (e.g., CBDT, GST Council) to provide clarity and implement tax laws, these cannot impose a tax on their own. They must always derive their authority from an existing law passed by the legislature. If a notification attempts to levy a tax not authorised by the parent act, it can be challenged in a court of law.

Implications for Taxpayers: Safeguarding Against Arbitrary Levies

Article 265 provides a powerful defence for taxpayers against any form of taxation that lacks proper legal backing. This constitutional provision ensures that the government cannot introduce new taxes or alter existing tax rates without following the due legislative process.

Protection from Unauthorised Demands

If a government department or official demands a tax that is not prescribed by a valid law, or if the demand exceeds what is stipulated in the law, taxpayers have the right to challenge it. This includes questioning any cess or surcharge that is not explicitly authorised by an act of Parliament or a State Legislature. The principle is clear: no law, no tax.

Judicial Review and Recourse

Citizens can seek judicial recourse if they believe a tax is being levied without the authority of law. The Supreme Court and various High Courts have, on numerous occasions, upheld the sanctity of Article 265, striking down tax demands or levies that were found to be ultra vires (beyond the powers) of the existing law or without any legislative backing. This judicial oversight reinforces the constitutional protection afforded to taxpayers. Understanding this right empowers individuals and businesses to ensure that all tax demands they face are legitimate and legally sound.

A real example

Meet Suresh, 45, who runs a small manufacturing unit in Nashik, Maharashtra, with an annual turnover of ₹85 lakhs. One day, he receives a notice from a local municipal corporation demanding a new "Infrastructure Development Cess" of 2% on his annual turnover, to be paid within 30 days. The notice cites a "circular from the municipal commissioner" as the authority for this new cess.

Suresh is initially worried about this additional ₹1.70 lakhs (2% of ₹85 lakhs) burden. However, recalling something he read about tax laws, he decides to consult his tax advisor. His advisor immediately points out that a municipal commissioner's circular cannot be the "authority of law" for imposing a new tax. Under Article 265, any new cess or tax must be enacted through a proper law passed by the Maharashtra State Legislature, or in specific cases, by the Parliament for central taxes.

The advisor explains that while municipalities can levy certain taxes (like property tax) as authorised by state laws, they cannot invent new taxes based on internal circulars. Suresh's advisor helps him draft a reply to the municipal corporation, citing Article 265 of the Constitution and demanding to know the specific legislative act under which this "Infrastructure Development Cess" has been imposed. Lacking such a law, the municipal corporation would have no legal basis to enforce the demand, thus protecting Suresh from an arbitrary levy.

FAQ

What is Article 265 of the Indian Constitution?

Article 265 states that "No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law." It means that every tax imposed by the government must be backed by a valid law passed by Parliament or a State Legislature.

Can the government impose a new tax without passing a law?

No, the government cannot impose a new tax without passing a specific law for it. Any new tax must go through the legislative process and be enacted into law by the Parliament or a State Legislature.

What is the difference between a tax and a fee in the context of Article 265?

Article 265 specifically applies to taxes. While a tax is a compulsory exaction for public purposes without a direct quid pro quo, a fee is generally charged for specific services rendered by the government. Fees are not strictly covered by Article 265 but must also have statutory backing.

Who is responsible for making tax laws in India?

Tax laws in India are made by the Parliament for central taxes (like Income Tax, Central GST) and by the respective State Legislatures for state taxes (like State GST, Stamp Duty).

What recourse do citizens have if a tax is imposed without proper legal authority?

If a tax is imposed without the authority of law, citizens can challenge it in a court of law, typically through a writ petition in a High Court or the Supreme Court, citing the violation of Article 265.
