Understanding Corporate Bonds vs. Fixed Deposits: Beyond the Interest Rate

TL;DR

  • Corporate bonds carry inherent credit risk, meaning the issuing company might default, unlike bank Fixed Deposits (FDs) which offer higher safety.
  • Fixed Deposits are considered safer investments, often backed by deposit insurance up to ₹5 lakhs per bank by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC).
  • Interest income from both FDs and corporate bonds is taxable as per your income tax slab, but bonds can also generate capital gains if sold before maturity.
  • The suitability of corporate bonds or FDs depends significantly on an individual's risk appetite, financial goals, and investment horizon.
  • Diversifying your fixed-income portfolio across various instruments can help manage risk and achieve different financial objectives.

What this means in plain terms

Many investors, especially those accustomed to the perceived safety of bank Fixed Deposits (FDs), often look at corporate bonds as simply FDs that offer a slightly higher interest rate. This perception, however, overlooks a fundamental difference: risk. While an FD is essentially a loan to a bank, carrying the implicit guarantee of the banking system and explicit insurance up to ₹5 lakhs by the DICGC, a corporate bond is a loan to a company.

This distinction is crucial. When you invest in a corporate bond, you are lending money directly to a business entity. The return you receive (the interest or coupon) is compensation for the risk you undertake that the company might not be able to repay its debt or the interest on time. This is known as credit risk, a factor largely absent from bank FDs. Understanding this core difference is paramount for making informed investment decisions and ensuring your portfolio aligns with your comfort level for risk.

Understanding Fixed Deposits (FDs)

Fixed Deposits (FDs) are a traditional and popular investment choice in India, valued for their simplicity and perceived safety. When you open an FD with a bank, you deposit a lump sum for a fixed period, and in return, the bank pays you a predetermined interest rate. This interest can be paid out periodically or compounded and paid at maturity.

The primary appeal of FDs lies in their low risk. Banks are regulated by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), and deposits up to ₹5 lakhs per bank are insured by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC), a wholly-owned subsidiary of the RBI. This insurance provides a significant safety net, protecting your principal amount even if the bank faces financial difficulties. However, the interest rates offered by FDs are generally lower compared to other fixed-income instruments that carry higher risk. The interest earned on FDs is fully taxable as per your income tax slab rate, and banks typically deduct Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) if the interest exceeds a certain threshold in a financial year.

Exploring Corporate Bonds

Corporate bonds are debt instruments issued by companies to raise capital. When you buy a corporate bond, you are essentially lending money to the issuing company. In exchange, the company promises to pay you interest (known as the coupon rate) at regular intervals and repay the principal amount (face value) on a specified maturity date. These bonds can be listed on stock exchanges, offering some liquidity, or unlisted.

Types and Features of Corporate Bonds

Corporate bonds come in various forms, including secured bonds (backed by specific assets of the company) and unsecured bonds (not backed by specific assets, relying on the company's general creditworthiness). They can also be convertible (can be converted into equity shares later) or non-convertible. The coupon rate, maturity period, and payment frequency vary widely depending on the issuer's credit profile and market conditions. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) regulates the issuance and trading of corporate bonds in India, ensuring transparency and investor protection.

Importance of Credit Ratings

A crucial aspect of corporate bonds is their credit rating. Credit rating agencies like CRISIL, ICRA, and CARE assign ratings to bonds based on the issuer's financial health, ability to meet debt obligations, and industry outlook. These ratings range from "AAA" (highest safety) to "D" (default) and provide investors with an assessment of the bond's credit risk. A higher rating generally indicates lower risk and usually corresponds to a lower interest rate, while lower-rated bonds offer higher interest rates to compensate for increased risk.

The Crucial Difference: Credit Risk

The fundamental distinction between a corporate bond and an FD revolves around credit risk. Credit risk is the possibility that the bond issuer (the company) may fail to make interest payments or repay the principal amount at maturity. This risk is inherent in corporate bonds because a company's financial health can deteriorate due to various factors like economic downturns, industry-specific challenges, or poor management decisions.

In contrast, Fixed Deposits with scheduled commercial banks in India carry significantly lower credit risk. This is primarily due to the robust regulatory oversight by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and the explicit deposit insurance provided by the DICGC. As mentioned, deposits up to ₹5 lakhs per bank are insured, meaning that even if a bank were to fail, your principal up to this limit is protected. While a bank failure is a rare event in India, the insurance provides an additional layer of security that corporate bonds do not offer. With corporate bonds, if the issuing company defaults, investors could lose a part or even all of their invested capital. This is why SEBI's Maninder Cheema emphasises that a corporate bond is not an FD with a better rate; the underlying risk profile is fundamentally different.

Taxation and Liquidity Considerations

Beyond risk, the tax implications and liquidity aspects also differentiate corporate bonds from Fixed Deposits, influencing their suitability for various investors.

Taxation

For Fixed Deposits, the interest earned is fully taxable as "Income from Other Sources" and added to your total income, taxed at your applicable income tax slab rate. Banks typically deduct Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) if the interest payout exceeds ₹40,000 (₹50,000 for senior citizens) in a financial year.

For corporate bonds, the taxation is slightly more nuanced. The interest (coupon) received from corporate bonds is also fully taxable as "Income from Other Sources" at your slab rate. However, if you sell a corporate bond before its maturity on a secondary market, any profit or loss you make is treated as capital gains. If held for more than 12 months, it's considered a long-term capital gain (LTCG) and taxed at 10% without indexation for listed bonds, or 20% with indexation for unlisted bonds and certain other cases. If held for 12 months or less, it's a short-term capital gain (STCG) and added to your income, taxed at your slab rate. This capital gains potential or loss is not applicable to FDs.

Liquidity

Fixed Deposits offer moderate liquidity. While you can prematurely withdraw your FD, banks usually levy a penalty for early withdrawal, which could be a reduction in the interest rate by 0.5% to 1%.

Corporate bonds, especially listed ones, offer liquidity through the secondary market on stock exchanges. However, the actual liquidity depends on the specific bond, its credit rating, and overall market demand. Highly rated, frequently traded bonds tend to be more liquid, meaning you can sell them relatively easily. Less popular or lower-rated bonds might be difficult to sell quickly without impacting the price, leading to potential losses if you need to exit before maturity.

A real example

Let's consider Priya, 42, earning ₹35 lakhs CTC, working in Mumbai. She has ₹10 lakhs to invest for a period of three years and is weighing her options between a bank FD and a corporate bond.

Scenario 1: Bank Fixed Deposit Priya considers an FD offering 7.0% p.a. for three years.

  • Investment: ₹10,00,000
  • Interest Rate: 7.0% p.a.
  • Maturity Value (approx., compounded annually): ₹10,00,000 * (1 + 0.07)^3 = ₹12,25,043
  • Total Interest Earned: ₹2,25,043
  • Taxation: Assuming Priya is in the 30% tax bracket, her tax on interest would be ₹2,25,043 * 30% = ₹67,513.
  • Net Post-Tax Return: ₹1,57,530.
  • Safety: Principal is insured up to ₹5 lakhs by DICGC.

Scenario 2: Corporate Bond Priya finds a highly-rated (AAA) corporate bond offering 8.5% p.a. for three years.

  • Investment: ₹10,00,000
  • Coupon Rate: 8.5% p.a.
  • Total Interest Earned over 3 years: ₹10,00,000 0.085 3 = ₹2,55,000 (assuming annual simple interest payouts and no reinvestment)
  • Taxation: Assuming Priya is in the 30% tax bracket, her tax on interest would be ₹2,55,000 * 30% = ₹76,500.
  • Net Post-Tax Return: ₹1,78,500.
  • Safety: No DICGC insurance. While AAA-rated, there is still an inherent credit risk, however small, that the company could default. Liquidity depends on secondary market demand.

In this example, the corporate bond offers a higher potential post-tax return of ₹1,78,500 compared to ₹1,57,530 from the FD, a difference of ₹20,970 over three years. However, this higher return comes with the crucial trade-off of credit risk, which is almost negligible in an FD. Priya must decide if the additional ₹20,970 is worth taking on the corporate bond's inherent credit risk and potentially lower liquidity.

What to do this week

  1. Review Your Current Fixed-Income Portfolio: Take stock of all your fixed-income investments, including FDs, bonds, and other debt instruments, to understand your current risk exposure.
  2. Assess Your Risk Appetite: Honestly evaluate how comfortable you are with the possibility of losing a portion of your principal. This will guide whether corporate bonds fit into your investment strategy.
  3. Research Credit Ratings: If considering corporate bonds, familiarise yourself with the concept of credit ratings and research the ratings of potential issuers from agencies like CRISIL, ICRA, or CARE.
  4. Understand Tax Implications: Use the Income Tax India website to understand how interest income and potential capital gains from bonds are taxed based on your income slab.

FAQ

Are corporate bonds always riskier than FDs?

Yes, generally, corporate bonds carry higher credit risk than bank FDs. FDs are backed by the bank's stability and DICGC insurance up to ₹5 lakhs, whereas corporate bonds depend solely on the issuing company's financial health and ability to repay.

What is a credit rating?

A credit rating is an assessment by an independent agency (like CRISIL or ICRA) of a bond issuer's ability to meet its financial obligations. It indicates the likelihood of default, with higher ratings (e.g., AAA) signifying lower risk.

How is interest from corporate bonds taxed?

Interest received from corporate bonds is taxable as "Income from Other Sources" and added to your total income, taxed at your applicable income tax slab rate.

Can I lose money in a corporate bond?

Yes, you can lose money in a corporate bond if the issuing company defaults on its payments (credit risk) or if you need to sell the bond in the secondary market before maturity at a price lower than your purchase price (market risk).

Are all corporate bonds illiquid?

Not all. Listed corporate bonds can be traded on stock exchanges, offering liquidity. However, the actual liquidity depends on factors like the bond's credit rating, market demand, and trading volume. Less popular bonds might be difficult to sell quickly.

What is DICGC insurance?

DICGC stands for Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation, a subsidiary of the RBI. It provides deposit insurance coverage up to ₹5 lakhs for each depositor in each scheduled commercial bank in India, protecting their funds in case of bank failure.
