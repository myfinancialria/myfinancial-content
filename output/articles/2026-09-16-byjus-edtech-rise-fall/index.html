BYJU'S: How India's Most Valuable Startup Wiped Out $22 Billion in 18 Months

Hook

In March 2022, the air around BYJU'S offices in Bengaluru buzzed with an almost palpable triumph. The Indian edtech giant had just secured another $800 million in funding, catapulting its valuation to a staggering $22 billion, making it the world's most valuable edtech startup. Founder Byju Raveendran, an engineer who began his journey by coaching students for competitive exams, stood at the pinnacle of entrepreneurial success. His company's logo adorned the jerseys of the Indian cricket team, and global icons like Lionel Messi were brought on board as brand ambassadors. BYJU'S was not just a company; it was a symbol of India's burgeoning digital economy, a narrative of innovation transforming education, and a beacon for ambitious startups. Yet, beneath the dazzling facade of growth and celebrity endorsements, cracks were already forming, unseen by many, that would, in less than two years, unravel this $22 billion empire into near insolvency and a valuation approaching zero.

The World Before

Before BYJU'S emerged, the landscape of Indian education was largely defined by traditional classroom teaching and a burgeoning, but fragmented, coaching institute industry for competitive exams. Online learning, while present, was nascent and often viewed as a supplementary tool rather than a comprehensive alternative. Challenges were abundant: lack of robust digital infrastructure in many parts of India, intermittent power supply in rural areas, and a general hesitation among traditional teachers to adopt digital technology. The idea of a primarily online platform commanding a significant share of the K-12 and test-prep market seemed, to many, a distant dream.

BYJU'S, founded in 2011 by Byju Raveendran and Divya Gokulnath, initially focused on online video-based learning programs for K-12 students and competitive exams like IIT-JEE, NEET, and CAT. Its freemium model offered a 15-day free trial, drawing in millions of curious students, before requiring a subscription for full access. The launch of "BYJU'S – The Learning App" in 2015 was a game-changer, quickly accumulating over 5.5 million downloads in its first year. The company leveraged India's increasing smartphone penetration and the widespread "test-prep culture" to position itself as a modern, engaging solution.

The Crack

The true accelerant for BYJU'S was the COVID-19 pandemic. Lockdowns forced schools and coaching centers offline, creating an unprecedented surge in demand for online education. BYJU'S seized this opportunity, scaling rapidly and attracting massive venture capital inflows from marquee investors like Sequoia Capital, Tencent, Naspers, and the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative. This period saw its valuation soar from $1 billion in 2018 to $22 billion by March 2022.

However, this meteoric rise was fueled by a "growth-at-all-costs" strategy that sowed the seeds of its downfall.

Aggressive Sales Tactics: BYJU'S became infamous for its high-pressure and often unethical sales practices. Former employees reported intense pressure to meet weekly sales targets, sometimes resorting to manipulative tactics like misleading parents about their children's academic performance to push expensive, multi-year subscriptions. There were allegations of sales teams instigating fear in parents and profiling potential customers from lower-income backgrounds, pushing them to take education loans without fully understanding the terms. Raveendran himself, in May 2024, admitted that "mistakes were made" by some salespeople and announced a shift to a "pull-based" sales model, urging staff to act as "education counselors" rather than pushy salespeople.

Opaque Accounting: Financial transparency became a major red flag. BYJU'S faced prolonged delays in filing its audited financial results, particularly for FY21, which remained outstanding for over 17 months. When the FY21 results were finally published in September 2022, they revealed a staggering loss of ₹4,588 crore (approximately $550.5 million), 19 times higher than the previous year, raising serious questions about its revenue recognition practices. In June 2023, its auditor, Deloitte, resigned, citing a lack of financial transparency and an inability to complete the audit, a move followed by the simultaneous exit of three board members. Further investigations by Indian authorities were launched into potential misreporting and fund diversion.

Over-Acquisition: To fuel its rapid expansion, BYJU'S embarked on an aggressive acquisition spree, spending billions. Key acquisitions included WhiteHat Jr (an online coding school for kids, acquired for $300 million in 2020) and Aakash Educational Services Ltd (a traditional test-prep giant, acquired for $1 billion in 2021). While these expanded BYJU'S reach, they came with significant integration challenges and financial strain.

WhiteHat Jr, in particular, became a symbol of acquisition woes. Despite its initial promise, it faced controversies over misleading advertising and high customer acquisition costs. By December 2023, it was sued by Code.org for allegedly failing to pay $3 million in licensing fees, part of a $4 million, four-year deal. BYJU'S founder Raveendran even referred to WhiteHat Jr as an "under-performer". The Aakash acquisition, intended to merge online and offline learning, also became mired in disputes, with minority shareholders, including Blackstone, alleging attempts to dilute their stake. The share-swap component of the Aakash deal also faced obstacles, with the Chaudhry family (Aakash's promoters) refusing to swap their remaining stake due to governance concerns.

What Happened Next

The combination of aggressive sales, opaque financials, and integration issues from over-acquisitions led to a dramatic and rapid collapse.

BYJU'S peak valuation of $22 billion in March 2022 quickly eroded. By January 2024, BlackRock, a major investor, had marked down its valuation to $1 billion, a staggering 95% drop. Other investors like Prosus also slashed their valuations, first to $5.1 billion in March 2023, then to $3 billion by November 2023. By October 2024, multiple media reports, and even founder Byju Raveendran himself, indicated that the company's valuation had effectively dropped to zero.

The company faced a severe liquidity crunch and mounting losses. In FY22, BYJU'S reported a net loss of ₹8,245 crore (approximately $860 million). To cut costs, BYJU'S initiated multiple rounds of layoffs. In October 2022, reports suggested plans to lay off nearly 12,000 employees, about 25% of its workforce. Further job cuts followed, with 2,500 employees laid off by June 2023. By February 2023, another 900+ employees were let go, and in April 2024, approximately 500 more staffers were laid off, mostly from sales and marketing and tuition centers, amidst a funding crisis and delayed salaries.

BYJU'S defaulted on a $1.2 billion term loan, leading to legal battles with its lenders. In 2024, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) initiated insolvency proceedings against BYJU'S parent company, Think & Learn Pvt. Ltd., following a petition by the BCCI over defaulted sponsorship dues. A US bankruptcy court also penalized BYJU'S director Riju Ravindran for contempt of court for refusing to disclose the location of $533 million in loan proceeds, implicating Byju Raveendran and Divya Gokulnath as complicit. The once high-flying edtech giant became mired in lawsuits, unpaid dues, and a fight for survival.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Growth at all costs is unsustainable without strong fundamentals: BYJU'S prioritized rapid expansion and valuation over profitability and sustainable unit economics (the revenue and costs associated with a single customer). While aggressive marketing built brand awareness, it couldn't sustain a business model where the cost to acquire and retain customers wasn't viable.
  2. Transparency and governance are non-negotiable: The prolonged delays in financial reporting and the subsequent auditor and board resignations severely eroded investor and public trust. Opaque accounting practices and allegations of financial mismanagement created an environment ripe for suspicion and ultimately, regulatory scrutiny.
  3. Acquisitions require strategic integration, not just scale: BYJU'S ambitious acquisition spree, particularly with WhiteHat Jr and Aakash, brought significant financial strain and integration challenges. Acquiring companies without clear synergy or effective post-merger integration plans can dilute focus and drain resources, especially when the acquired entities themselves face issues.
  4. Ethical sales practices build long-term trust: The aggressive, fear-based sales tactics, which reportedly misled parents and pushed expensive loans, generated short-term revenue but led to widespread customer complaints and a tarnished reputation. Sustainable businesses are built on customer satisfaction and trust, not coercion.
