Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The first day these shares are traded on the stock exchange is known as the listing day. Many people focus on the listing gain (the percentage difference between the IPO price and the closing price on listing day). This initial "pop" or "drop" often gets a lot of attention.

However, the listing day can be a very emotional time, driven by high demand or sudden profit-booking. It doesn't always reflect the company's true long-term value. This is why we look at IPOs 30 to 60 days after they list. By this time, the initial excitement or panic usually settles down. Also, a common "lock-in" period for certain large investors (like anchor investors) often ends around 30 days for 50% of their allotted shares, while the remaining 50% are locked in for 90 days. This means these investors are then free to sell their shares, which can affect the price. Looking at the price after this period gives us a calmer, more realistic picture of how the market is valuing the company.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's initial price movement is a dance between demand (how many people want to buy the shares) and supply (how many shares are available).

  • Demand vs. Supply: If many people want to buy shares, and there aren't enough to go around, the price tends to go up. If there are more shares than buyers, the price might drop. Think of it like a school fete stall selling special handmade crafts. If everyone wants the same few items, the stall owner might get a higher price. If nobody is interested, they might have to lower prices.
  • Subscription Levels: This refers to how many times the IPO shares were applied for. High subscription levels usually mean high demand, which can lead to a listing "pop" (price increase).
  • Market Mood: The overall sentiment of the stock market and economy also plays a role. If the broader market is doing well, new listings often benefit.
  • Pricing: The company sets the IPO price. If it's priced conservatively (a bit low), it leaves room for the price to rise on listing. If it's priced aggressively (a bit high), it might struggle to gain.
  • Lock-in and Anchor Investors: Some large investors, called anchor investors, commit to buying shares before the IPO opens to the public. Their shares are often "locked in" for a certain period (e.g., 30 days), meaning they cannot sell them immediately. Once this lock-in period ends, if these investors decide to sell, it can increase the supply of shares in the market, potentially affecting the price.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot. The stock market is full of volatility (quick ups and downs in price). A company's share price can change a lot over days, weeks, or months due to many factors.

For example, a stock might list flat (meaning its price doesn't change much from the IPO price) but then climb significantly over the next few weeks as more investors discover its value. Conversely, a stock might have a huge listing gain but then see its price fall as early investors book profits or new information about the company or industry emerges. The broader market performance also impacts individual stocks. If the overall market is falling, even good companies might see their share prices drop. The first day is just the beginning of a company's journey on the stock exchange.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a new sweet shop opens in your neighbourhood. On the grand opening day, there's a lot of buzz, maybe special offers, and a huge crowd. People might even pay a little extra just to be among the first. That's like the IPO listing day.

But after a month or two, the initial excitement fades. People now buy sweets based on how good they taste, the quality, the service, and the regular prices. The shop's daily sales and how customers feel about it over time determine its true popularity and value. This calmer, settled period is like looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after listing.

Things people get confused about

  • Myth: A big listing gain means it's a great company.

    • * Fact: A big listing gain mostly shows high immediate demand for the shares, not necessarily the company's long-term performance or financial health.

  • Myth: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment forever.

    • * Fact: An initial drop can be due to various reasons like market conditions, aggressive pricing, or early profit-booking. The company's performance can improve over time, and its share price might recover or even grow significantly later.

  • Myth: The IPO price is the "right" price for the company's shares.

    • * Fact: The IPO price is what the company offers shares at. Once listed, the open market, through buying and selling by millions of investors, decides the "right" price based on demand, supply, and future expectations.

FAQ

What is an IPO?

An IPO, or Initial Public Offering, is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary people to buy a part of the company.

What is the "issue price"?

The issue price is the fixed price per share at which the company offers its shares to investors during the IPO.

What is "current price"?

The current price is the latest trading price of a company's share on the stock exchange at a given moment.

What is "return vs IPO price"?

This number tells you the percentage change between the IPO price and the current market price. A positive percentage means the share is trading above its IPO price, and a negative percentage means it's below.

What is the difference between a Mainboard and SME IPO?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE or BSE), while SME (Small and Medium Enterprise) IPOs are for smaller companies with different listing requirements and often lower market capitalisation, listing on dedicated SME platforms.
