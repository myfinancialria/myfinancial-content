RBI Governor Shaktikanta Das Keeps Interest Rates Steady: What It Means For Your Loans And Savings

TL;DR

  • RBI Governor Shaktikanta Das announced the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) decided to keep the key interest rate, the repo rate, unchanged.
  • This means banks will continue to borrow money from the RBI at the same rate of 6.50%.
  • For you, this likely means your loan EMIs (like home or car loans) will stay stable for now.
  • Interest rates on your bank deposits (like FDs and savings accounts) are also expected to remain largely the same.
  • The decision aims to balance controlling inflation (rising prices) with supporting economic growth in India.

What's this about?

Shaktikanta Das, the head of India's central bank, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), recently shared the outcome of the latest meeting of the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC). This important committee, led by the Governor, decides on key interest rates to manage the country's money supply and overall economic health. In this meeting, they decided to keep the main policy interest rate, called the repo rate, at its current level of 6.50%. This is a significant announcement because the repo rate acts as a benchmark, influencing many other interest rates across the Indian economy, from your home loan to your fixed deposit returns.

Why it's in the news right now

The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) meets every two months to carefully review the economic situation and set key interest rates. The latest meeting concluded on October 6, 2026, and Governor Das announced the decisions to the public. The financial world, businesses, and ordinary citizens eagerly await these announcements because they directly affect borrowing costs for businesses and individuals, as well as the returns on people's savings. The decision to maintain the repo rate has been widely reported, with experts discussing its implications for managing inflation (rising prices) and supporting India's economic growth.

How it actually works

  1. The RBI and its Governor: The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is India's central bank, much like a country's financial guardian. Its Governor, currently Shaktikanta Das, is like the captain of the economic ship, guiding the nation's monetary policy.
  2. Monetary Policy Committee (MPC): This is a powerful six-member team, headed by the RBI Governor, that meets regularly to decide on interest rates. Their main goals are twofold: to keep inflation (the rate at which prices of goods and services rise) under control, and at the same time, support healthy economic growth.
  3. The Repo Rate: This is the most important interest rate the MPC decides, currently at 6.50%. "Repo" stands for "Repurchase Option." It's essentially the rate at which commercial banks (like State Bank of India, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, etc.) borrow money from the RBI for their short-term needs. Think of it as the wholesale price of money for banks.
  4. How it affects you: When the repo rate is kept unchanged, it means the cost for banks to borrow money from the RBI hasn't changed. This stability usually translates to stable interest rates for you on your loans (like home loans, car loans, personal loans) and your savings (like fixed deposits and savings accounts). If the repo rate were to go up, banks would likely increase their lending rates, making loans more expensive. Conversely, if it went down, loans might become cheaper, and deposit rates could also fall.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine the RBI is like the main wholesaler for all the kirana shops (banks) in your neighbourhood. The repo rate is the price at which these kirana shops buy their stock (money) from the wholesaler.

When Governor Shaktikanta Das announces that the repo rate is unchanged at 6.50%, it's like the wholesaler telling all the kirana shops that the price of their stock remains the same. Because their cost of getting money hasn't changed, the kirana shops (banks) are likely to keep the prices they charge their customers (you, for loans) and the prices they pay for your goods (your savings) stable too. If the wholesaler increased their price, the kirana shops would likely increase their prices to you. If the wholesaler lowered their price, the kirana shops might pass on some savings.

Why it matters to you

  1. Stable Loan EMIs: If you have a home loan, car loan, or personal loan that is linked to external benchmarks (like the repo rate), your Equated Monthly Instalments (EMIs) are likely to remain stable. This predictability helps you plan your monthly budget better without sudden increases in your loan payments.
  2. Predictable Savings Returns: Interest rates on your fixed deposits (FDs), recurring deposits (RDs), and even your savings accounts are expected to stay largely the same. This gives you a clearer idea of how much your hard-earned savings will grow over time, allowing for better financial planning.
  3. Inflation Watch: The RBI's decision also reflects its current view on inflation. By keeping rates steady, the RBI signals that it believes the current measures and economic conditions are sufficient to manage price rises without putting too much pressure on economic activity. This indirectly affects the purchasing power of your rupee – how much you can buy with your money.
  4. Economic Confidence: A stable interest rate environment can boost confidence among businesses and investors. When borrowing costs are predictable, companies are more likely to invest in new projects, expand their operations, and create jobs, which can lead to overall economic growth and prosperity for the country.

Common confusions cleared up

  • Myth: The RBI directly sets my home loan interest rate.

    • Fact: The RBI sets the repo rate, which is the rate at which banks borrow from it. Banks then add their own costs, operational expenses, and profit margins to this to decide the final interest rate they offer you for loans. So, while the repo rate influences your loan rate significantly, it doesn't directly set* it.

  • Myth: If the repo rate is unchanged, inflation will definitely stay the same.

    • * Fact: The repo rate is a powerful tool the RBI uses to manage inflation. While keeping it steady suggests the RBI is comfortable with the current inflation trajectory or its existing measures, many other factors (like global crude oil prices, supply chain disruptions, government fiscal policies, and even monsoon patterns) also impact inflation.

  • Myth: The Governor decides everything on his own.

    • * Fact: While Governor Shaktikanta Das leads the RBI, the crucial interest rate decisions are made collectively by the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), a six-member body. The Governor chairs this committee and has a casting vote in case of a tie, but it's a collaborative decision-making process.

FAQ

What is the current repo rate?

As of October 6, 2026, the repo rate has been maintained at 6.50% by the Monetary Policy Committee.

How often does the MPC meet?

The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) is mandated to meet at least four times in a financial year, but typically meets six times, every two months, to review the economic situation and decide on policy rates.

What happens if the repo rate increases?

If the repo rate increases, it becomes more expensive for banks to borrow money from the RBI. This usually leads to banks increasing their lending rates, making loans (like home and car loans) more expensive for you, and potentially increasing your EMIs.

What happens if the repo rate decreases?

If the repo rate decreases, it becomes cheaper for banks to borrow from the RBI. This often prompts banks to lower their lending rates, making loans cheaper for you and potentially reducing your EMIs. It might also lead to slightly lower interest rates on your savings.

Does the RBI only focus on interest rates?

No, while interest rates are a primary tool for monetary policy, the RBI also focuses on other critical aspects like maintaining financial stability, regulating banks and other financial institutions, managing foreign exchange reserves, and ensuring smooth payment systems in the country.
