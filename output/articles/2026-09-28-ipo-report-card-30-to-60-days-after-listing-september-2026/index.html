Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public through an IPO, there's often a lot of excitement. On the very first day its shares start trading, called the "listing day" (the first day shares are available to buy and sell on the stock exchange), the price can jump significantly. This initial jump is often called a "listing gain" (the percentage increase from the IPO price to the closing price on the first day of trading).

However, this first-day excitement doesn't always last. Many factors can influence the listing day price, and it might not reflect the company's true value in the long run. Also, some large investors, known as "anchor investors" (big institutions that commit to buying shares before the IPO opens to the public), have a "lock-in" period (a time when they cannot sell their shares). Once this lock-in period ends, these investors are free to sell, which can affect the share price. As per SEBI regulations, 50% of the shares allotted to anchor investors are locked in for 90 days, and the remaining 50% are locked in for 30 days from the date of allotment.

Looking at a company's share price 30 to 60 days after its listing gives us a calmer, more settled picture. By this time, the initial frenzy has usually faded, and the market has had more time to assess the company. This period often provides a more instructive read on how the shares are performing compared to their IPO price, beyond just the first-day pop or drop. For this report, the listing day closing prices were not available, so we are focusing on the return from the IPO price to the current market price.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's initial performance is a dance between demand and supply, much like a popular new shop opening.

  • Demand vs. Supply: If many people want to buy shares (high demand) and there aren't enough shares available (limited supply), the price tends to go up. If there are more shares than buyers, the price might drop.
  • Subscription Levels: This refers to how many times an IPO is "oversubscribed" (how many more applications were received than shares offered). High oversubscription often suggests strong demand and can lead to a listing pop.
  • Market Mood: The general sentiment of the stock market and economy plays a big role. If the overall market is doing well, new listings often get a warmer reception.
  • Pricing: The company sets the IPO price (the price at which shares are offered to the public). If the price is set too low, it leaves room for a big jump on listing. If it's set too high, investors might not see much value, leading to a flat or negative listing.
  • Lock-in and Anchor Investors: As mentioned, anchor investors have a lock-in period. Their initial commitment boosts confidence. Once their lock-in ends, their selling activity can influence the price.

A "pop" on listing means the share price went up, often because the demand was much higher than the supply, or the IPO was priced attractively. A "drop" means the price fell, perhaps due to lower-than-expected demand, concerns about the company, or an overall weak market.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The first day's trading can be very "volatile" (prices can change very quickly and unpredictably). It's often driven by short-term traders looking for quick profits. The "broader market" (the overall stock market performance) also influences individual stock prices. A company might list flat (meaning its price didn't change much from the IPO price) if the market is cautious, but then its shares could move a lot later as more information comes out or the company performs well. Conversely, a stock might have a big listing gain but then see its price fall in the weeks or months after, as the market reassesses its value. The initial price is just one point in time; a company's long-term performance depends on its business fundamentals.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a school fete where a new stall opens selling unique handmade crafts. On the first day, everyone rushes to buy, and the prices might even go up because of the excitement and limited stock. But after a few days, the initial rush settles. People have had a chance to look closely at the crafts, compare them with other stalls, and decide if they truly value them. The prices then settle at a level that reflects what regular buyers are willing to pay, rather than just the first-day buzz. This settled price is a bit like the share price after 30 to 60 days.

Things people get confused about

  • Myth: A big listing gain means it's a great company that will always do well.

    • Fact: A big listing gain only reflects strong initial demand and pricing strategy. It doesn't guarantee future business success or stock performance.

  • Myth: Applying for IPOs is a guaranteed way to make quick money.

    • Fact: IPOs carry risks, just like any other stock market investment. Prices can fall below the IPO price, leading to losses.

  • Myth: You must apply for every IPO that comes out.

    • Fact: It's important to research each company individually and understand its business, financials, and the risks involved before deciding to apply. Not every IPO is suitable for every investor.

FAQ

What is an IPO?

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary investors to buy a part of the company.

What is an SME IPO?

An SME IPO is an IPO by a Small and Medium Enterprise (SME). These are usually smaller companies that list on a separate platform of the stock exchange, like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, with different rules compared to mainboard IPOs.

How do I apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility or through your stockbroker's platform. The money is blocked in your account and only debited if you are allotted shares.

What is share allotment?

Share allotment is the process where shares are distributed to applicants after an IPO closes. If an IPO is oversubscribed, you might not get all the shares you applied for, or even any shares at all.

What is a lot size in an IPO?

A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot apply for fewer shares than the lot size, and applications must be in multiples of the lot size.
