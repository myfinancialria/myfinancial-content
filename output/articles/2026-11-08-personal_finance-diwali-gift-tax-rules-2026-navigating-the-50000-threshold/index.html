Diwali Gift Tax Rules 2026: Navigating the ₹50,000 Threshold

TL;DR

  • Gifts, whether in cash, movable, or immovable property, are taxable under the Income Tax Act, 1961, if their value exceeds ₹50,000 in a financial year.
  • This ₹50,000 exemption limit applies to gifts received from non-relatives, including friends.
  • If the aggregate value of such gifts from non-relatives crosses ₹50,000 in a financial year, the entire gift amount becomes taxable as "Income from Other Sources".
  • Gifts received from specified relatives, or on occasions like marriage or through inheritance, are fully exempt from income tax, regardless of the amount.
  • It is crucial for recipients to understand these rules to avoid unexpected tax liabilities, especially during festive seasons like Diwali.

What this means in plain terms

During festive seasons like Diwali, exchanging gifts is a common tradition. However, it's important to be aware that some gifts you receive might be subject to income tax in India. The Income Tax Act, 1961, has specific provisions that determine whether a gift is taxable in the hands of the recipient.

Simply put, if you receive gifts from certain individuals or entities, and their total value exceeds ₹50,000 in a financial year, you might have to pay tax on that amount. This rule primarily applies to gifts received from people who are not considered "relatives" as per the tax laws. Understanding these definitions and thresholds can help you avoid unwelcome surprises when filing your income tax return.

Understanding the Income Tax Act's Gift Provisions

The taxability of gifts in India is primarily governed by Section 56(2)(x) of the Income Tax Act, 1961. This section specifies that any sum of money, or the value of any movable or immovable property, received without consideration or for inadequate consideration, will be charged to income tax under the head "Income from Other Sources" in the hands of the recipient.

The ₹50,000 Threshold

A key aspect of this provision is the ₹50,000 threshold. If the aggregate value of gifts received by an individual or a Hindu Undivided Family (HUF) from non-relatives during a financial year exceeds ₹50,000, the entire amount of such gifts becomes taxable. It's important to note that it's not just the amount exceeding ₹50,000 that is taxed, but the whole sum if the threshold is breached. For instance, if you receive gifts worth ₹60,000 from friends in a financial year, the entire ₹60,000 will be added to your taxable income.

Types of Gifts Covered

Section 56(2)(x) covers various forms of gifts:

  • Any sum of money: This includes cash, cheques, bank transfers, etc.
  • Movable property: This includes shares, securities, jewellery, archaeological collections, drawings, paintings, sculptures, any work of art, or bullion. The fair market value of such property is considered for taxation.
  • Immovable property: This includes land or building or both. The stamp duty value of the property is considered. If the property is received for inadequate consideration (i.e., less than its fair market value), the difference between the stamp duty value and the consideration paid is treated as a gift, provided this difference exceeds ₹50,000.

Who is a 'Relative' for Tax Purposes?

The Income Tax Act provides specific exemptions for gifts received from "relatives". Gifts from these specified relatives are fully exempt from tax, regardless of the amount. This is a crucial distinction, as gifts from friends or distant acquaintances do not fall under this exemption.

Definition of 'Relative' for an Individual

For an individual, "relative" is defined as:

  • Spouse of the individual.
  • Brother or sister of the individual.
  • Brother or sister of the spouse of the individual.
  • Brother or sister of either of the parents of the individual.
  • Any lineal ascendant or descendant of the individual.
  • Any lineal ascendant or descendant of the spouse of the individual.
  • Spouse of any of the persons mentioned above.

This definition is quite broad and covers immediate family members and their spouses, ensuring that traditional family gifts are not taxed.

Definition of 'Relative' for a Hindu Undivided Family (HUF)

For a Hindu Undivided Family (HUF), any member of the HUF is considered a relative.

Exemptions Beyond Relatives

Beyond gifts from specified relatives, there are other situations where gifts are exempt from income tax, irrespective of the amount. These exemptions are also enshrined in Section 56(2)(x) of the Income Tax Act.

Key Exemptions:

  • Gifts received on the occasion of marriage: Any gifts, whether cash or property, received by an individual at the time of their marriage are fully exempt from tax. This exemption applies only to the person getting married, not to other family members.
  • Gifts received under a will or by way of inheritance: Property or money received through a will or as an inheritance is not subject to gift tax. This is because such transfers are typically covered under estate or inheritance laws, not income tax.
  • Gifts received in contemplation of death of the payer or donor: If a gift is made by a person who is on their deathbed, and the gift is made in anticipation of their death, it can be exempt.
  • Gifts from any local authority: Gifts received from a Panchayat, Municipality, Municipal Committee, District Board, Cantonment Board, or any other local authority are exempt.
  • Gifts from any fund, foundation, university, other educational institution, hospital, other medical institution, any trust or institution: Provided these entities are referred to in sub-clause (iv), (v), (vi), or (via) of clause (23C) of Section 10 of the Income Tax Act. These typically refer to registered charitable or educational institutions.
  • Gifts from a trust or institution registered under Section 12A or 12AA: These are generally charitable or religious trusts.

These exemptions ensure that certain customary or legally mandated transfers of assets are not burdened with income tax.

Valuation of Gifts and Tax Implications

The method of valuing gifts depends on whether they are cash, movable property, or immovable property, and this valuation directly impacts the potential tax liability.

Valuation Methods:

  • Cash Gifts: If the gift is a sum of money, the actual amount received is considered for taxation.
  • Movable Property: For movable property (like shares, jewellery, etc.), the fair market value (FMV) on the date of receipt is taken into account. If the property is received for inadequate consideration, the difference between the FMV and the consideration paid is taxable, provided this difference exceeds ₹50,000.
  • Immovable Property: For immovable property (land or building), the stamp duty value is the benchmark. If the property is received without consideration, the entire stamp duty value is taxable. If received for inadequate consideration, the difference between the stamp duty value and the consideration paid is taxable, provided this difference exceeds ₹50,000 and 10% of the consideration or ₹50,000, whichever is higher.

Tax Implications

When a gift is taxable, it is added to the recipient's "Income from Other Sources" and taxed at their applicable income tax slab rates. This means that a gift could push an individual into a higher tax bracket, increasing their overall tax burden for that financial year. It is crucial for recipients to maintain proper records of gifts received, including the donor's details, the date of receipt, and the value of the gift, to ensure compliance during tax filing.

A real example

Let's consider Priya, 32, ₹18L CTC, Gurugram.

Priya celebrates Diwali with her friends and family. In the financial year 2026-27, she receives the following gifts:

  1. From her parents (relatives): A cash gift of ₹2,00,000 for a down payment on a new car.
  2. From her brother (relative): A gold chain valued at ₹75,000.
  3. From her friend, Anjali (non-relative): A cash gift of ₹30,000.
  4. From her friend, Sameer (non-relative): A gift voucher for a luxury watch, valued at ₹25,000.

Let's break down the tax implications for Priya:

  • Gifts from parents (₹2,00,000) and brother (₹75,000): These gifts are received from specified relatives as per the Income Tax Act. Therefore, they are fully exempt from tax, regardless of the amount. Total exempt gifts: ₹2,75,000.
  • Gifts from friends Anjali and Sameer: These are from non-relatives.

    • * Cash gift from Anjali: ₹30,000 * Gift voucher from Sameer: ₹25,000 * Total gifts from non-relatives: ₹30,000 + ₹25,000 = ₹55,000

Now, we apply the ₹50,000 threshold for gifts from non-relatives. Since the aggregate value of gifts from non-relatives (₹55,000) exceeds ₹50,000, the entire amount of ₹55,000 becomes taxable in Priya's hands.

Conclusion: Priya will need to declare ₹55,000 as "Income from Other Sources" in her Income Tax Return for the financial year 2026-27. This amount will be added to her salary income and taxed according to her applicable income tax slab rate.

What to do this week

  1. Review Gift Sources: Make a list of all significant gifts (cash, movable, immovable property) you have received or expect to receive in the current financial year and identify the donor for each.
  2. Understand 'Relative' Definition: Familiarise yourself with the Income Tax Act's definition of a "relative" to correctly categorise your gifts. You can refer to the Income Tax India website.
  3. Track Non-Relative Gifts: Keep a running total of the aggregate value of gifts received from non-relatives. If this sum approaches or exceeds ₹50,000, be prepared for potential tax implications.
  4. Maintain Records: For any gifts received, especially substantial ones, keep clear records of the donor's name, relationship, date of receipt, type of gift, and its value. This documentation will be vital during tax filing.

FAQ

Is a gift from my employer taxable?

Non-cash gifts received from an employer with a value exceeding ₹5,000 in a financial year are taxable as "perquisite" under the head "Salaries"; cash gifts from an employer are fully taxable regardless of the amount.

Do I have to pay tax if I receive a gift from my NRI relative?

No, gifts received from specified relatives, whether they are resident Indians or Non-Resident Indians (NRIs), are fully exempt from income tax in India.

How is the value of a gifted property determined for tax purposes?

For immovable property, the stamp duty value is considered. For movable property like shares or jewellery, the fair market value on the date of receipt is used.

What if I receive a gift from a trust or a charitable organisation?

Gifts received from certain specified trusts or institutions registered under Section 12A or 12AA, or referred to in Section 10(23C), are exempt from tax.

If I give a gift, does the donor pay tax?

Generally, the donor does not pay gift tax in India. The tax liability, if any, falls on the recipient of the gift, under Section 56(2)(x) of the Income Tax Act.

Is there any tax on gifts received on my birthday?

Gifts received on occasions other than marriage (like birthdays or anniversaries) are subject to the same gift tax rules. If the aggregate value from non-relatives exceeds ₹50,000 in a financial year, the entire amount is taxable.
