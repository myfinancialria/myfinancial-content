Vijay Shekhar Sharma and Paytm: The 8 November 2016 Bet That Made — Then Broke — Him

The air crackled with uncertainty on the night of November 8, 2016. At 8 PM, Prime Minister Narendra Modi addressed the nation, declaring all ₹500 and ₹1,000 currency notes illegal tender from midnight. Chaos ensued. ATMs would run dry, banks would be swamped, and the everyday cash economy of India—where over 78% of consumer payments were still cash-based—ground to a halt.

Yet, for Vijay Shekhar Sharma, founder and CEO of digital payments platform Paytm, this wasn't a crisis. It was an opportunity. As the nation grappled with the sudden cash crunch, Sharma reportedly called his team that very night, instructing them to place a full-page newspaper advertisement for the following day. The ad, which appeared on November 9, famously thanked PM Modi for "taking the boldest decision in the financial history of independent India!" and urged citizens to embrace cashless payments with the tagline, "Ab ATM nahin, #Paytm karo." This audacious move, positioning Paytm as the immediate solution to a national crisis, would catapult the company to unprecedented heights, but also set in motion a chain of events that would later bring it crashing down.

The World Before

Before November 2016, India's financial landscape was largely dominated by cash. While digital payment solutions like mobile banking and e-wallets existed, their adoption was moderate, particularly outside urban centers. Companies like Paytm, Mobikwik, and Freecharge had emerged, targeting mobile phone recharges and online payments, but faced challenges of trust, awareness, and limited infrastructure in rural areas. Paytm, founded in 2010, had launched its mobile wallet in 2014 and had around 125-150 million wallet users by late 2016.

The government had initiated programs like Jan Dhan Yojana to increase financial inclusion and the Digital India campaign to boost digital literacy and infrastructure. The Unified Payments Interface (UPI), an instant, real-time payment system developed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), had also launched in August 2016. However, UPI's initial uptake was slow, processing only around 300,000 transactions nationwide by October 2016 – a tiny fraction compared to Paytm's daily transactions. The idea of a truly cashless India seemed distant, almost impossible for a country of over a billion people with deeply ingrained cash habits.

The Crack

The demonetization announcement on November 8, 2016, changed everything overnight. With 86% of the currency in circulation invalidated, millions found themselves without usable cash. Paytm seized the moment. Their controversial full-page ad, featuring PM Modi, immediately drew attention and criticism, including from Delhi Chief Minister Arvind Kejriwal. However, it also cemented Paytm's image as the go-to solution for the cash-strapped public.

The impact was immediate and dramatic. Paytm’s user base, which stood at 125-140 million before demonetization, exploded. Within hours of the announcement, the platform saw a 25% increase in transactions, a 200% hike in app downloads, and a 435% increase in overall traffic. Between November 10 and December 20, 2016, Paytm added over 20 million new users, bringing its total to 170 million. By November 14, Paytm was processing 5 million transactions daily, surpassing the combined average daily usage of all credit and debit cards in India at the time. By late November, it was handling over 7 million transactions worth ₹120 crore in a single day.

Sharma and his team worked at a frantic pace, reportedly achieving "600 days' worth of work in 60." They rapidly expanded their merchant network, focusing on the unorganized sector and equipping small vendors with QR codes to accept digital payments. By March 2017, Paytm's wallet users had surged to 218 million, adding 700,000 new users daily during the month of demonetization itself. Within a year of demonetization, the user base had catapulted to 270-280 million. Paytm had successfully leveraged a national crisis to become a household name, synonymous with digital payments.

What Happened Next

Paytm’s post-demonetization boom set the stage for its highly anticipated Initial Public Offering (IPO). In November 2021, One97 Communications, Paytm's parent company, launched India's largest-ever IPO, aiming to raise ₹18,300 crore (approximately $2.5 billion at the time) at a valuation of $20 billion. The IPO was oversubscribed 1.89 times.

However, the euphoria was short-lived. Shares began trading on November 18, 2021, opening at ₹1,950 on the NSE, a 9.3% discount to the IPO price of ₹2,150. By the end of its listing day, Paytm's stock had plummeted by over 27% to ₹1,560, making it the biggest drop on a listing day in Indian IPO history. The market capitalization dropped by nearly ₹39,000 crore on listing day itself, eroding a significant portion of investor wealth. Analysts cited concerns over inflated valuation, a large float, and the company's core wallet business becoming less relevant with the rise of government-backed UPI.

The troubles didn't end there. Paytm Payments Bank, launched in 2017, soon faced increasing regulatory scrutiny from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). In March 2022, the RBI barred Paytm Payments Bank from onboarding new customers due to "supervisory concerns" related to compliance processes, particularly Know Your Customer (KYC) norms. Persistent non-compliance and "material supervisory concerns" led to stricter measures. On January 31, 2024, the RBI imposed severe restrictions, prohibiting the bank from accepting new deposits or credit transactions after February 29, 2024 (later extended to March 15, 2024). This effectively froze most of its banking services, including wallets, FASTags, and NCMC cards. Finally, on April 24, 2026, the RBI canceled Paytm Payments Bank's banking license entirely, citing continued breaches of licensing conditions and operations detrimental to depositors' interests and public interest. This action marked the complete shutdown of Paytm's banking arm. Vijay Shekhar Sharma, who held a 51% stake in Paytm Payments Bank, resigned as part-time non-executive chairman and board member, citing regulatory challenges.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. The Double-Edged Sword of Crisis-Driven Growth: While demonetization provided an unparalleled tailwind for Paytm, accelerating digital adoption, it also fostered a growth model heavily reliant on external circumstances. When the initial crisis faded and competitors (especially UPI) caught up, sustaining that momentum became challenging. The rapid, forced adoption may not have translated into deep-seated, sticky user behavior for all services, especially when a free, interoperable alternative (UPI) became dominant.
  2. Regulatory Compliance is Non-Negotiable: Paytm's repeated run-ins with the RBI, culminating in the revocation of its Payments Bank license, highlight that rapid growth and market leadership do not exempt a financial entity from stringent regulatory adherence. Neglecting compliance, particularly in critical areas like KYC and data security, can lead to severe operational restrictions and, ultimately, business termination, regardless of market size.
  3. Valuation vs. Sustainable Business Model: The spectacular collapse of Paytm's IPO share price demonstrates the market's eventual demand for sustainable unit economics and clear profitability pathways over sheer user base or transaction volume. An "inflated valuation" based on hyper-growth during a unique market event, without a robust, long-term business model that justifies that valuation, can lead to significant investor disappointment.
  4. The Peril of Political Alignment: Paytm's decision to run an ad praising PM Modi's demonetization, while strategically brilliant for immediate visibility, also drew political scrutiny and potentially tied the company too closely to a government policy. While demonstrating agility and opportunism, such alignment can also expose a business to criticism and perceived favoritism, as seen with the controversy surrounding the ad itself.
