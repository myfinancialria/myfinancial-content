Business Mavericks · 2026-10-17

The clock had just ticked past 3 AM on Sunday, March 8, 2020, when the Enforcement Directorate (ED) knocked on Rana Kapoor’s Mumbai residence. After over 20 hours of intense questioning and a raid the previous day, the founder and former CEO of Yes Bank was arrested under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA). The charges? Allegations of fraud exceeding US$100 million, or roughly ₹750 crore at the time. Just three days prior, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) had imposed a moratorium on Yes Bank, capping withdrawals at ₹50,000 per depositor, plunging the bank and its millions of customers into unprecedented uncertainty. It was a stunning downfall for a man who had, in less than two decades, built a financial powerhouse from the ground up, only to see it crumble under the weight of his aggressive lending and alleged governance failures.

The World Before

In the early 2000s, India’s banking landscape, while opening up, was still largely dominated by public sector behemoths and a few established private players. Getting a new banking license was a rare feat, and the industry was perceived as relatively conservative, especially in corporate lending. This was the environment Rana Kapoor, along with his brother-in-law Ashok Kapur and Harkirat Singh, sought to disrupt. Their vision, initially, was to create a bank that could cater to a broader spectrum, including the rural population. However, the dream quickly evolved. After initial partnerships and early exits (Harkirat Singh left in 2003, and Ashok Kapur tragically died in the 2008 Mumbai terror attacks), Rana Kapoor became the sole promoter, steering Yes Bank towards an ambitious, high-growth trajectory.

Kapoor positioned Yes Bank as a nimble, aggressive lender, willing to say "yes" where others said "no". This audacious strategy was considered unconventional, even risky, by the more cautious players in the market. While traditional banks shied away from certain high-risk corporate clients, Yes Bank embraced them, rapidly expanding its loan book and aiming for a slice of the big corporate loans.

The Crack

Yes Bank’s ascent was meteoric. Founded in 2003, it grew its total assets to an impressive ₹3.8 trillion (₹3.8 lakh crore) by March 31, 2019, making it India's fourth-largest private sector bank. Its market capitalization peaked at over ₹1 lakh crore in August 2018, with its share price touching an all-time high of ₹404.00. Rana Kapoor, once hailed as a maverick banker, became a billionaire by 2017.

However, beneath this veneer of rapid growth lay a dangerous core. After Ashok Kapur's death in 2008, Rana Kapoor effectively ran Yes Bank as his "personal fiefdom," consolidating power and allegedly sidelining other stakeholders, including Ashok Kapur's widow and daughter. The aggressive lending strategy intensified, with Yes Bank becoming known as the "lender of the last resort" for companies struggling to secure funds elsewhere.

Kapoor’s alleged modus operandi involved extending credit to companies considered too risky by conservative banks, often in exchange for alleged kickbacks that benefited his family’s businesses. This included significant exposures to troubled groups like Dewan Housing Finance Corporation (DHFL), Anil Ambani’s Reliance Group, Jet Airways, Cox & Kings, and Essel Group.

For instance, the Enforcement Directorate alleged that Yes Bank invested ₹3,700 crore in DHFL's short-term debentures. In return, DHFL's promoter, Kapil Wadhawan, allegedly provided kickbacks of ₹600 crore in the form of a loan to DOIT Urban Ventures, a company where Rana Kapoor's daughters were directors and 100% shareholders. Similarly, investigations found that Yes Bank officials allegedly cleared around ₹3,000 crore in loans to Anil Ambani's companies between 2017 and 2019 without due diligence, allegedly influenced by "illegal quid pro quo arrangements" where Yes Bank promoters received payments in privately held concerns.

The RBI began raising concerns about Yes Bank's governance and asset quality as early as 2016, intensifying its supervision. The central bank uncovered a significant divergence between Yes Bank's reported non-performing assets (NPAs) and the actual figures. For example, while Yes Bank reported NPAs of ₹6,000 crore, the RBI's assessment indicated the true figure was over ₹30,000 crore. This practice of under-reporting bad loans and "over-financing" new projects of distressed borrowers to mask existing bad loans became a critical red flag.

In September 2018, the RBI curtailed Kapoor's tenure as MD and CEO, allowing him to continue only until January 31, 2019, despite the board's recommendation for a longer extension. This decision triggered a sharp decline in the bank's share price, which had already seen a 78% drop since August 2018. Rana Kapoor eventually exited the bank, selling his remaining stake by December 2019.

What Happened Next

The departure of its charismatic founder, coupled with mounting bad loans and repeated unsuccessful attempts to raise fresh capital, eroded investor and depositor confidence. By December 2019, Yes Bank reported a staggering loss of ₹18,564 crore for the third quarter of FY20, with its common equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio plummeting to 0.62%, well below the RBI's mandated level. Depositors, fearing for their savings, began withdrawing funds en masse, leading to a severe liquidity crisis. Deposits declined from ₹2.1 trillion in September 2019 to ₹1.1 trillion by March 2020.

On March 5, 2020, the RBI intervened decisively, placing Yes Bank under a 30-day moratorium and superseding its board. Withdrawals were capped at ₹50,000 per depositor, sending shockwaves through the Indian financial system. The bank's share price hit an all-time low of ₹5.65 on March 6, 2020. Rana Kapoor was arrested on March 8, 2020, on charges of money laundering and fraud. He spent over four years in custody before being granted bail in April 2024.

To prevent a systemic crisis, the RBI orchestrated a rescue plan. On March 13, 2020, the Union Cabinet approved the "Yes Bank Ltd Reconstruction Scheme 2020," led by the State Bank of India (SBI). SBI committed to investing ₹7,250 crore for a 49% stake in the bank. Other major private banks like ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, Axis Bank, and Kotak Mahindra Bank also injected capital, contributing approximately ₹10,000 crore in total. As part of the controversial reconstruction, ₹8,415 crore worth of Additional Tier-1 (AT1) bonds were written down to zero, impacting numerous institutional and retail investors. The Supreme Court later rejected appeals by the bank and government regarding this write-off in October 2026, causing a 14.53% drop in Yes Bank's shares.

The moratorium was lifted on March 18, 2020, and a new management team, led by Prashant Kumar, a former SBI CFO, took charge. Yes Bank’s balance sheet contracted significantly, with total assets shrinking by ₹1.23 trillion between March 2019 and March 2020.

Post-rescue, Yes Bank embarked on a path of recovery. It sold ₹48,000 crore of stressed assets to JC Flowers Asset Reconstruction Company and recovered over ₹13,000 crore in bad loans between 2020 and 2023. The bank shifted its lending focus from corporate to retail and MSME segments, which now account for 60% of its loan book, up from 36% in 2020. Its gross NPA significantly improved, falling to 1.3% in Q1 FY27 from 16.8% in fiscal 2020. In 2024, Japan's Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation (SMBC) announced plans to acquire a 20% stake for ₹13,483 crore, signaling renewed global confidence. As of March 2026, Yes Bank reported total assets of ₹469,105 crore and a net profit of ₹3,476 crore.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Aggressive Growth Can Mask Deeper Flaws: Yes Bank's pursuit of rapid expansion, particularly through high-risk corporate lending, created an illusion of success that ultimately proved unsustainable. Growth at all costs, especially when fueled by questionable lending practices, often leads to an accumulation of hidden risks that eventually surface.
  2. Corporate Governance is Paramount: The story highlights the dangers of concentrated power and a lack of robust corporate governance. When a single individual, even a founder, wields unchecked influence, it can lead to decisions that prioritize personal gain or short-term targets over the bank's long-term health and regulatory compliance.
  3. The Regulator's Role as a Backstop: While the RBI faced scrutiny for the timing of its intervention, its eventual actions — curtailing Kapoor's tenure, imposing a moratorium, and orchestrating a consortium-led rescue — proved critical in preventing a full-blown systemic crisis. This underscores the vital role of a vigilant and assertive central bank in maintaining financial stability.
  4. Systemic Risk Requires Collective Action: The Yes Bank crisis demonstrated how the failure of a single large private bank can pose significant systemic risk to the broader financial ecosystem. The coordinated rescue effort, involving public sector banks and competitors, illustrated the necessity of collective action to safeguard depositor trust and financial stability.
