RBI Revives FCNR(B) Swap Window: Can It Attract NRI Inflows?

TL;DR

  • The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has reintroduced a special FCNR(B) swap window for banks to attract Non-Resident Indian (NRI) deposits.
  • This mechanism allows banks to hedge the currency risk of FCNR(B) deposits with the RBI at a subsidised rate.
  • By reducing banks' hedging costs, the scheme enables them to offer more attractive interest rates on FCNR(B) deposits to NRIs.
  • The move aims to bolster India's foreign exchange reserves and stabilise the rupee amidst global economic uncertainties.
  • NRIs, particularly those in the Gulf, could find FCNR(B) deposits more appealing due to potentially higher returns and reduced currency risk.

What this means in plain terms

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has brought back a special scheme designed to encourage Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) to deposit foreign currency, primarily US dollars, into Indian banks. This scheme, known as the FCNR(B) swap window, was last used in 2013 when the rupee was facing significant pressure. Essentially, when an NRI deposits dollars into an FCNR(B) account in India, the bank converts these dollars into rupees to lend within India. However, when the deposit matures, the bank needs to convert rupees back to dollars to repay the NRI, exposing it to currency fluctuation risk.

To mitigate this risk for banks, the RBI is now offering to swap the foreign currency with rupees at a special, favourable rate, effectively absorbing a significant portion of the currency hedging cost for banks. This reduction in cost allows banks to offer higher interest rates on these FCNR(B) deposits to NRIs, making them more attractive compared to other investment options. The primary goal for the RBI is to increase the inflow of foreign currency into India, thereby strengthening the rupee and boosting the country's foreign exchange reserves during a period of global economic volatility.

Understanding FCNR(B) Deposits for NRIs

Foreign Currency Non-Resident (Bank) or FCNR(B) accounts are term deposit accounts denominated in foreign currencies, such as US Dollars (USD), British Pounds (GBP), Euros (EUR), Japanese Yen (JPY), and Canadian Dollars (CAD), held by Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) in India. These accounts allow NRIs to park their foreign earnings in India without converting them into rupees, thereby protecting them from rupee depreciation during the deposit tenure.

Key Features of FCNR(B) Accounts

  • Currency Denomination: Unlike NRE (Non-Resident External) or NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary) accounts, FCNR(B) deposits are held in a specified foreign currency, eliminating exchange rate risk for the NRI depositor.
  • Eligibility: Only NRIs and PIOs can open FCNR(B) accounts.
  • Tenure: These deposits typically have a fixed tenure ranging from one year to five years.
  • Interest Rates: Interest rates are determined by individual banks, usually linked to international benchmark rates (like LIBOR or SOFR) for the respective currency, plus a spread.
  • Repatriability: Both the principal amount and the interest earned are fully repatriable, meaning they can be freely transferred back abroad in the original foreign currency.
  • Taxation: Interest earned on FCNR(B) deposits is exempt from income tax in India for NRIs. However, it might be taxable in the country of residence depending on local tax laws.

The RBI's FCNR(B) Swap Window Mechanism

The FCNR(B) swap window is a special liquidity and hedging facility provided by the Reserve Bank of India to banks. When an NRI places an FCNR(B) deposit, the bank receives foreign currency. To deploy these funds domestically, the bank typically converts the foreign currency into Indian Rupees. This creates a currency mismatch, as the bank will need to convert rupees back into the foreign currency at maturity to repay the NRI. To manage this currency risk, banks usually enter into forward contracts, which involve a cost.

How the Swap Window Works

Under the revived swap window, the RBI steps in to absorb this currency risk for banks at a subsidised rate. Here's a simplified breakdown:

  1. NRI Deposit: An NRI opens an FCNR(B) deposit with an Indian bank, depositing foreign currency (e.g., USD).
  2. Bank's Hedging Need: The bank converts the USD to INR for domestic lending. It now has an obligation to repay the NRI in USD at maturity.
  3. RBI Intervention: Instead of hedging in the open market at potentially higher costs, the bank enters into a currency swap agreement with the RBI. The RBI provides rupees to the bank in exchange for the foreign currency and commits to reversing this swap at a pre-determined, favourable rate at maturity.
  4. Subsidised Cost: The rate offered by the RBI for this swap is lower than what banks would typically pay in the interbank forward market. This reduction in hedging cost allows the bank to offer a higher interest rate on the FCNR(B) deposit to the NRI.
  5. Increased Attractiveness: For the NRI, this translates into a more attractive return on their foreign currency deposit compared to what they might get otherwise, or even compared to similar deposits in other global markets.

Comparison to 2013 Intervention

The FCNR(B) swap window was last used in 2013 during a period of significant rupee depreciation and capital outflows. At that time, the RBI offered a fixed swap rate for three-year FCNR(B) deposits, which was substantially lower than market rates. This intervention successfully attracted over USD 34 billion in inflows, helping to stabilise the rupee and shore up India's foreign exchange reserves. While the exact terms of the current revival might differ, the underlying principle remains the same: to incentivise foreign currency inflows by making FCNR(B) deposits more lucrative for NRIs and less risky for banks.

Why the RBI Revived This Tool Now

The decision by the Reserve Bank of India to revive the FCNR(B) swap window is a strategic move driven by the prevailing global and domestic economic landscape. Several factors contribute to this policy decision:

Global Economic Volatility and Rupee Stability

The global economy has been grappling with high inflation, aggressive monetary policy tightening by major central banks (like the US Federal Reserve), and geopolitical tensions. These factors often lead to capital outflows from emerging markets like India, putting downward pressure on the domestic currency. A weaker rupee makes imports more expensive, potentially fueling inflation and increasing the cost of foreign debt. By attracting foreign currency inflows through the FCNR(B) swap window, the RBI aims to bolster the rupee's stability and mitigate the impact of external shocks.

Boosting Foreign Exchange Reserves

India's foreign exchange reserves, while substantial, have seen some depletion due to the RBI's interventions to defend the rupee and meet import demands. Increasing these reserves is crucial for enhancing the country's external sector resilience, providing a buffer against unforeseen economic challenges, and maintaining investor confidence. The FCNR(B) swap window is an effective tool to quickly augment these reserves.

Managing Liquidity and Interest Rate Differentials

The subsidised swap rate offered by the RBI also helps manage liquidity in the banking system and allows banks to navigate interest rate differentials between India and global markets. With global interest rates rising, Indian banks might find it challenging to offer competitive FCNR(B) rates without incurring significant hedging costs. The RBI's intervention effectively bridges this gap, enabling banks to attract foreign currency deposits more easily.

Potential Impact and Investor Considerations

The revival of the FCNR(B) swap window is expected to have a multi-faceted impact on both the Indian economy and Non-Resident Indian investors.

For the Indian Economy

  • Rupee Stabilisation: Increased foreign currency inflows can help strengthen the rupee against major global currencies, reducing imported inflation and making foreign debt servicing more manageable.
  • Enhanced Forex Reserves: A boost in foreign exchange reserves provides a stronger cushion against external shocks and improves India's creditworthiness.
  • Liquidity Management: It injects foreign currency liquidity into the banking system, which can be deployed for domestic economic activities.

For NRI Investors

  • Potentially Higher Returns: The primary benefit for NRIs is the likelihood of higher interest rates on FCNR(B) deposits compared to what banks might offer without the RBI's swap facility. This makes parking foreign currency in India more attractive.
  • Currency Risk Protection: FCNR(B) deposits are denominated in foreign currency, meaning the NRI depositor is not exposed to the risk of rupee depreciation. They get back their principal and interest in the original foreign currency.
  • Diversification: It offers NRIs an additional avenue to diversify their savings and investments, combining the stability of a fixed deposit with the potential for better returns in a foreign currency.
  • Tax Efficiency: Interest earned on FCNR(B) deposits is exempt from income tax in India.

Considerations for NRIs

While attractive, NRIs should consider a few points:

  • Interest Rate Comparison: Always compare the FCNR(B) rates offered under the swap window with other investment options available in their country of residence and other NRI deposit schemes.
  • Tenure: FCNR(B) deposits are term deposits, so funds are locked in for the chosen tenure (1-5 years). Early withdrawal penalties may apply.
  • Bank Specifics: Rates and terms can vary between banks. It's prudent to check offerings from multiple Indian banks.
  • Taxation Abroad: While tax-free in India, NRIs must understand the tax implications of FCNR(B) interest income in their country of residence.

A real example

Priya, 45, works as a software engineer in Dubai, earning a good salary in USD. She has accumulated USD 75,000 in her savings account and is looking for a safe investment option that offers better returns than her current bank and protects her from currency fluctuations. She frequently sends money to her family in India and is aware of the rupee's volatility.

Currently, her bank in Dubai offers a 1-year fixed deposit at 3.5% per annum. An Indian bank, without the RBI swap window, might offer an FCNR(B) rate of, say, 4.0% for a 1-year USD deposit, but would factor in a significant hedging cost, perhaps 1.0% of the interest, making their net return lower.

With the RBI's FCNR(B) swap window, the same Indian bank can hedge its currency risk at a subsidised rate. Let's assume this subsidy allows the bank to reduce its hedging cost by 0.75%. This reduction enables the bank to offer a more competitive rate to Priya. Instead of 4.0%, the bank might now offer 4.75% per annum for a 1-year FCNR(B) deposit.

Here's how Priya's investment would look:

  • Investment Amount: USD 75,000
  • Tenure: 1 year
  • Interest Rate (with swap window): 4.75% p.a.

Calculation: Interest earned = USD 75,000 * 4.75% = USD 3,562.50

At maturity, Priya would receive USD 75,000 (principal) + USD 3,562.50 (interest) = USD 78,562.50.

Compared to her Dubai bank's 3.5% rate (yielding USD 2,625 interest), the FCNR(B) deposit under the swap window offers her an additional USD 937.50 in interest, while still protecting her principal from currency risk. This makes the Indian FCNR(B) deposit a significantly more attractive option for her foreign currency savings.

What to do this week

  1. Contact Your Bank: Reach out to your preferred Indian bank (or banks) and inquire about their FCNR(B) deposit rates and terms, specifically asking if they are leveraging the RBI's new swap window.
  2. Compare Rates: Gather interest rate offerings for FCNR(B) deposits across multiple banks and compare them with similar foreign currency deposit options in your country of residence.
  3. Review Eligibility: Confirm your eligibility as an NRI or PIO for opening an FCNR(B) account and understand the necessary documentation.
  4. Understand Tenure Options: Decide on a suitable deposit tenure (1 to 5 years) that aligns with your financial goals and liquidity needs.
  5. Assess Tax Implications: Consult a tax advisor in your country of residence to understand any potential tax liabilities on the interest earned from FCNR(B) deposits.

FAQ

Who is eligible to open an FCNR(B) account?

Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) are eligible to open FCNR(B) accounts.

What are the typical tenures for FCNR(B) deposits?

FCNR(B) deposits typically have fixed tenures ranging from one year to five years.

Is the interest earned on FCNR(B) deposits taxable in India?

No, the interest earned on FCNR(B) deposits is exempt from income tax in India for NRIs. However, it may be taxable in your country of residence.

How does the FCNR(B) swap window benefit NRIs?

The swap window reduces the hedging cost for banks, allowing them to offer higher interest rates on FCNR(B) deposits, making them more attractive for NRIs.

What currencies can FCNR(B) deposits be held in?

FCNR(B) deposits can be held in major foreign currencies such as USD, GBP, EUR, JPY, and CAD.

Can I withdraw my FCNR(B) deposit before maturity?

Yes, premature withdrawals are generally allowed, but they may be subject to penalties as per the bank's terms and conditions.
