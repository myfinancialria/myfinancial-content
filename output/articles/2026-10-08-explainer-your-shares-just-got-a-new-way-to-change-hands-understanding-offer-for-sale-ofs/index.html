Your Shares Just Got a New Way to Change Hands: Understanding Offer for Sale (OFS)

TL;DR

  • An Offer for Sale (OFS) is a way for existing large shareholders, like company promoters or the government, to sell their shares in a company that is already listed on the stock market.
  • Unlike an Initial Public Offering (IPO), the company itself does not issue new shares or raise fresh money through an OFS.
  • The money from an OFS goes directly to the selling shareholders, not into the company's bank account.
  • OFS is often used to meet rules set by SEBI, like maintaining a minimum public shareholding, or for the government to sell its stake in public sector companies.
  • Retail investors get a reserved portion (at least 10%) and sometimes a discount, making it a chance to buy shares in established companies.

What's this about?

You might have heard about companies offering shares to the public, or the government selling its stake in a big company. Often, this happens through something called an "Offer for Sale," or OFS. Simply put, an OFS is a special way for big shareholders of a company that is already trading on the stock market to sell a chunk of their shares to other investors. These big shareholders could be the company's founders (called promoters), large investment funds, or even the Government of India.

The key difference from other share sales is that in an OFS, no new shares are created by the company. Instead, existing shares just change hands from the selling shareholder to new buyers. The company itself doesn't get any money from this sale; the funds go straight to the sellers.

Why it's in the news right now

Offer for Sale is a mechanism that frequently appears in the financial news. Right now, you might be hearing about it because HD Fire Protect Limited, a company, is set to open its Initial Public Offering (IPO) on October 13, 2026, which is entirely an Offer for Sale. This means the company's promoters are selling their existing shares, and the company itself won't receive any proceeds from this IPO. This highlights how OFS can be a significant part of even a company's debut on the stock market. You also often see OFS announcements when the government plans to sell its stake in public sector undertakings (PSUs) or when company promoters need to reduce their holdings to meet regulatory requirements.

How it actually works

An OFS is a structured process regulated by SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India). Here's a simplified breakdown:

  1. Who can sell? Only promoters or large shareholders (holding more than 10% of the company's share capital) of already listed companies can use the OFS route. This includes the Government of India for disinvestment in PSUs.
  2. Announcement: The selling shareholder announces their intention to sell shares through OFS. This announcement usually comes a couple of days before the actual sale. It includes important details like the date of the OFS and the "floor price." The floor price is the minimum price at which investors can bid for the shares.
  3. Bidding Days: The OFS usually takes place over one or two trading days.

    4. * On Day 1 (often called 'T day'), only non-retail investors (like big institutions, mutual funds, foreign investors) can place their bids. * On Day 2 (often 'T+1 day'), retail investors get their chance to bid. * Retail investors are individuals who bid for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs.

  4. Allotment: After all bids are received, the selling shareholder decides the "cut-off price" – the final price at which shares will be allotted. Investors who bid at or above this price get shares.
  5. Settlement: Successful bidders receive the shares in their demat accounts, usually within T+1 days (one trading day after the allotment). The funds are deducted from their bank accounts.

SEBI rules also state that at least 10% of the shares offered in an OFS must be reserved for retail investors. For OFS by Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs), this retail reservation can sometimes go up to 20%. Retail investors might also get a discount on the cut-off price, especially in PSU OFS issues.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a large family owns a successful kirana store in your neighbourhood. The store is already well-known and has many regular customers. Now, one of the elder family members decides they want to sell a part of their personal share in the store to other people in the community.

This is like an OFS. The kirana store (the company) is already established and running. The family member (the promoter/selling shareholder) is selling their existing ownership stake, not adding a new section to the store or bringing in more goods. The money from this sale goes directly to that family member, not into the store's cash register for new stock or expansion. Other people in the community (investors) can buy a piece of this already-running, familiar business.

Why it matters to you

  1. Opportunity to buy existing shares: An OFS allows you to buy shares of companies that are already listed and trading, often at a potentially attractive price.
  2. Access to established companies: Unlike IPOs, which are for new listings, OFS gives you a chance to invest in companies with a track record and publicly available financial information.
  3. Potential for discounts: Retail investors sometimes receive a discount on the OFS price, especially in government disinvestments, offering a better entry point.
  4. Government disinvestment: When the government sells its stake in PSUs through OFS, it provides an avenue for you to participate in these large, often stable, companies.
  5. Meeting regulatory norms: Companies use OFS to meet SEBI's minimum public shareholding norms (usually 25% for private companies), which means more shares become available for the general public to trade.

Common confusions cleared up

Myth: OFS is the same as an IPO. Fact: No, they are different. An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company sells shares to the public for the very first time to get listed on the stock exchange. The company issues new shares and raises fresh capital for its own growth. An OFS is for companies already listed, where existing shareholders sell their shares, and no new capital is raised by the company.

Myth: The company gets the money from an OFS. Fact: This is incorrect. The money raised from an OFS goes entirely to the selling shareholders (promoters, government, etc.), not to the company itself. This is different from an IPO or a Follow-on Public Offering (FPO), where the company receives the funds.

Myth: OFS is a very long process, like an IPO. Fact: OFS is typically a much faster process than an IPO. It usually concludes within one or two trading days, making it a quick way for large shareholders to divest their holdings.

FAQ

What is the minimum public shareholding norm?

SEBI requires all listed companies (with some exceptions for PSUs) to have at least 25% of their shares held by the public (non-promoters). OFS is a common way for promoters to reduce their stake to meet this rule.

Can anyone apply for an OFS?

Yes, any individual investor with a demat and trading account can apply for an OFS through their broker. There are specific reservations for retail investors.

Is there a discount for retail investors in OFS?

Sometimes, yes. In certain OFS issues, especially those by Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs), retail investors may be offered a discount on the floor price or cut-off price.

How do I know about upcoming OFS opportunities?

Companies are required to announce OFS details to the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE) at least two banking days before the offer opens. You can track these announcements through financial news, brokerage platforms, or the websites of the stock exchanges.
