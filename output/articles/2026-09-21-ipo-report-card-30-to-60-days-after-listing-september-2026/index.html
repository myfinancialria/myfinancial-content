Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public through an Initial Public Offering (IPO), there's often a lot of excitement. The "listing day" (the first day shares are traded on the stock exchange) can see prices jump significantly, known as a "listing gain" (the percentage increase from the IPO price to the listing day's closing price). However, this initial buzz can sometimes be short-lived.

Looking at an IPO's performance 30 to 60 days after it lists gives us a calmer, more settled picture. This period is important because the initial frenzy often fades. Also, "anchor investors" (large institutional investors who commit to buying shares before the IPO opens) have a "lock-in period" where 50% of their allotted shares are locked in for 90 days, and the remaining 50% are locked in for 30 days from the date of allotment. Once this lock-in ends, their buying or selling activity can also influence the stock price. So, checking a month or two later helps us see where the market has settled the price after the initial excitement and these technical factors have played out.

Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's initial performance is largely driven by the basic economic principle of "demand versus supply."

  • Demand: If many investors want to buy shares, and the company has offered a limited number, the demand is high. This often leads to a "pop" (a significant price increase) on listing day.
  • Supply: If there are more shares available than investors want to buy, or if many existing shareholders decide to sell quickly, the supply is high, which can lead to a "drop" (a price decrease).

"Subscription levels" (how many times the IPO was oversubscribed, meaning how many more bids came in than shares offered) are a key indicator of demand. High subscription levels often suggest a strong listing. "Market mood" (the general sentiment of investors towards the stock market) also plays a big role. A positive market mood can boost IPO performance, while a cautious mood might lead to a weaker debut.

The "pricing" of the IPO by the company is also crucial. If the company offers shares at a price that the market feels is reasonable or even a bit low, it creates more interest. "Anchor investors" are large investors who commit to buying a significant portion of the IPO shares. Their participation often signals confidence, but their "lock-in period" (usually 30 days) means they cannot sell these shares immediately, which can temporarily reduce the supply of shares in the market. Once this lock-in ends, if these investors decide to sell, it can increase supply and affect the price.

Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot. The stock market is dynamic, and prices are subject to "volatility" (quick and often unpredictable changes). A stock might open with a big gain, but then fall during the day, or vice-versa. The "broader market" (the performance of the overall stock market, like the Nifty or Sensex) can also influence individual stock prices.

Sometimes, a stock might list flat (with little to no change from its IPO price) but then perform very well over the next few weeks or months as more investors discover its value. Conversely, a stock with a huge listing gain might see its price drop later as the initial excitement fades or new information comes to light. The initial price action only tells you about the immediate market reaction, not the long-term journey of the company's shares.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a popular new restaurant opening in your neighbourhood. On the first day, everyone rushes to try it out. There's a long queue, and people might even pay a bit extra for a quick table. That's like the listing day pop for an IPO – lots of excitement and high demand. However, after a month or two, the initial rush settles down. The restaurant's true popularity and consistent quality will determine if people keep coming back regularly at the normal prices. This calmer period, 30 to 60 days after listing, is when the market starts to judge the company's shares based on its actual business and future prospects, not just the opening day hype.

Things people get confused about

Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.

Fact: A big listing gain primarily reflects high initial demand for the shares, often driven by market sentiment and IPO pricing, not necessarily the long-term quality or performance of the company.

Myth → Fact: Listing below the IPO price means it's a bad company.

Fact: A stock listing below its IPO price can be due to various reasons like broader market conditions, aggressive pricing by the company, or lower-than-expected investor demand, and does not automatically mean the company itself is bad.

Myth → Fact: IPOs are a guaranteed way to make quick money.

Fact: While some IPOs offer quick listing gains, many can also list flat or below their issue price. There is no guarantee of profit, and prices can fall after listing.

FAQ

What is an IPO?

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary investors to buy a part of the company.

What is a Mainboard IPO?

A Mainboard IPO is for larger, more established companies that list their shares on the main exchanges like the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).

What is an SME IPO?

An SME (Small and Medium Enterprise) IPO is for smaller companies that list on dedicated platforms within the NSE or BSE, often with different rules and lower minimum investment amounts.

What is the issue price?

The issue price is the fixed price per share at which the company offers its shares to investors during the IPO.

What is listing gain?

Listing gain is the percentage difference between the IPO issue price and the price at which the shares close on their first day of trading on the stock exchange.
