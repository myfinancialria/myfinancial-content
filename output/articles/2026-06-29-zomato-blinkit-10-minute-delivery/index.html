Why Deepinder Goyal Bet Zomato on 10-Minute Grocery Delivery When Everyone Said It Was Impossible

The year was 2022. India's quick commerce sector was a battlefield of burning cash and uncertain futures. Startups promising lightning-fast grocery deliveries were hemorrhaging money, and investor skepticism was at an all-time high. It was against this backdrop that Deepinder Goyal, the co-founder and CEO of Zomato, made a move that many considered sheer madness: Zomato announced its acquisition of Blinkit (then Grofers) for $568 million in an all-stock deal. Critics balked, Zomato's stock price tumbled 10% in a single day, and analysts questioned the sanity of buying a company that was reportedly burning ₹100 crore monthly with no clear path to profitability. Yet, Goyal was unperturbed. He had a vision, a conviction that 10-minute grocery delivery wasn't just a fleeting trend but the future of hyperlocal commerce, and that Zomato needed to be at its forefront.

The World Before: A Category Burning Cash

Before Blinkit's resurgence, the quick commerce landscape in India was fraught with challenges. The model, which promised groceries and essentials delivered in 10-30 minutes, gained traction during the pandemic as online grocery adoption surged. Players like Swiggy Instamart, Zepto, and even Amazon Fresh and Flipkart Quick entered the fray, all vying for market share by offering rapid deliveries. However, beneath the veneer of rapid growth lay a harsh reality: razor-thin margins, intense competition, high operational costs, and massive cash burn.

Grofers, founded in 2013, had been an early player in online grocery delivery. For years, it operated on a scheduled delivery model, struggling with high operational costs and low customer loyalty. The company realised that convenience alone wasn't enough; speed was the missing ingredient. In 2021, Grofers pivoted dramatically, rebranding itself as Blinkit to signal its singular focus on "instant commerce indistinguishable from magic" – 10-minute deliveries. This pivot, while visionary, was also capital-intensive, requiring a dense network of "dark stores" (small, local warehouses) to fulfill orders rapidly. By 2022, despite delivering 125,000 orders per day in 12 cities, Blinkit was in a precarious financial position, making it a "money-losing business" in the eyes of many.

The broader market shared this skepticism. The quick commerce sector was valued at $1.5 billion in 2022, but profitability remained elusive for most players as growth and market share were prioritized over margins. The prevailing sentiment was that 10-minute delivery was an unsustainable gimmick, pushing riders to dangerous speeds and solving a problem that traditional kirana stores already addressed.

The Crack: A Bet on Density and Discipline

Deepinder Goyal saw not a money pit, but a strategic imperative. Zomato's core food delivery business, while dominant, was facing commoditization and brutal competition with Swiggy. Growth was slowing, and unit economics were challenging. Goyal recognized that a strong quick commerce player could easily threaten food delivery in the long term, and Zomato needed a new growth engine.

The acquisition, announced in June 2022 for ₹4,447 crore (approximately $568 million), was an all-stock deal that valued Blinkit shareholders at one Zomato share for every ten Blinkit shares. Zomato had already invested $100 million in Blinkit in March 2022 for a 9.3% stake and extended a $150 million loan. This meant Zomato was doubling down on a business that was deeply in the red.

Goyal's belief stemmed from a fundamental understanding of quick commerce's underlying mechanics. He argued that 10-minute delivery wasn't about riders speeding, but about a "sheer complexity of the system design" enabled by the density of dark stores around customer homes. Orders are picked and packed within 2.5 minutes, and riders cover an average of less than 2 km in about 8 minutes, traveling at around 15 km/h. This hyperlocal model, with dark stores strategically placed in densely populated areas, allowed for both speed and efficiency.

Blinkit, under Zomato's ownership and led by CEO Albinder Dhindsa, focused relentlessly on operational efficiency rather than deep discounting. They streamlined supply chains, optimized delivery networks, and expanded their dark store footprint, aiming for 1,000 stores by March 2025. The strategy was to improve delivery times and order fulfillment rates while maintaining a sustainable cost structure. Blinkit also began expanding its product assortment beyond groceries to include high-margin categories like electronics, beauty, and home appliances, increasing average order value (AOV).

What Happened Next: A Profitable Powerhouse

Fast forward to 2024, and Deepinder Goyal's "madness" looks like a stroke of genius. Blinkit has transformed from a cash-burning liability into Zomato's fastest-growing and now profitable segment.

In the fourth quarter of fiscal year 2024 (Q4 FY24), Blinkit achieved adjusted EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization) positive status in March 2024, a significant milestone that proved the quick commerce model could be financially viable. Its revenue from operations nearly tripled year-on-year, surging to ₹769 crore in Q4 FY24 from ₹363 crore in Q4 FY23. For the full financial year 2024, Blinkit's revenue stood at ₹2,301 crore, a massive increase from ₹806 crore in FY23.

Blinkit's Gross Order Value (GOV)—the total value of all orders placed—soared by 97% year-on-year in Q4 FY24, reaching ₹4,027 crore. This growth propelled Zomato to a consolidated net profit of ₹175 crore in Q4 FY24, a dramatic turnaround from a loss of ₹189 crore in the same period last year. Zomato's overall revenue from operations jumped 73% year-on-year to ₹3,562 crore in Q4 FY24.

More impressively, Blinkit has outpaced its rivals, including Swiggy Instamart, in market share. As of mid-2025 estimates, Blinkit holds approximately 44-46% of the Indian quick-commerce market, with Zepto at 29-30% and Instamart at 23-25%. Some reports from early 2025 even suggested Blinkit had a 39% market share, ahead of Instamart's 37% and Zepto's 20%. By FY26, Blinkit further strengthened its lead with 916.6 million orders, compared to Instamart's 412.2 million orders. Goldman Sachs, in April 2024, reportedly valued Blinkit at an astounding $13 billion, thirteen times its acquisition price, even surpassing Zomato's core food delivery business valuation.

Deepinder Goyal himself has stated that Blinkit's monthly transacting users (MTUs) are expected to eventually surpass Zomato's food delivery MTUs, and that Blinkit's GOV could potentially grow fourfold in the long term. The company is focused on increasing store density, particularly in the top 8 urban locations, and aims for a steady-state adjusted EBITDA margin of 4-5% for Blinkit.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Counter-Intuitive Bets Can Pay Off: When the market sees only cash burn and impossibility, a visionary leader can spot a nascent market with immense potential. Goyal’s acquisition of a struggling quick-commerce player, against widespread skepticism, highlights the value of conviction in an unproven category.
  2. Operational Excellence Trumps Deep Discounts: While quick commerce is inherently capital-intensive, Blinkit’s success wasn't built on unsustainable promotions. Instead, its focus on optimizing dark store networks, efficient order fulfillment, and expanding high-margin product categories allowed it to achieve profitability through disciplined execution and improved unit economics.
  3. Strategic Diversification for Long-Term Growth: Zomato recognized that relying solely on food delivery was a growth trap. By integrating quick commerce, it not only diversified its revenue streams but also created a stronger hyperlocal ecosystem. This move allowed Zomato to tap into new customer needs and defend its existing business against emerging threats.
  4. Density is the Moat in Hyperlocal: The success of 10-minute delivery isn't about reckless speed, but about a dense network of dark stores that minimizes travel distance and time for delivery partners. This geographic density and operational scale are hard to replicate, creating a significant competitive advantage in the quick commerce space.
