First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local shop that has been doing well for years. Now, it wants to grow bigger – maybe open more branches or buy new equipment. To do this, it needs a lot of money. Instead of borrowing from a bank, the shop decides to sell a small piece of its ownership to the public.

This is exactly what an IPO (Initial Public Offering) is. It's when a private company offers its shares for the very first time to the public. This process happens in the primary market (where new shares are first sold). Once these shares are sold, they then get listed (become available for buying and selling) on a stock exchange like the NSE or BSE, where anyone can trade them. By doing an IPO, the company raises money for its growth, and in return, you get to own a small part of that company.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, there are a few key pieces of information that help you understand what's being offered:

  1. Price Band: This is the range of prices, like ₹203 to ₹214 for Laser Power & Infra, within which you can bid for shares. You usually bid at the "cut-off price" or the highest price in the band.
  2. Lot Size: This is the minimum number of shares you must apply for. You cannot apply for just one share; you have to apply in multiples of the lot size. For example, if the lot size is 50 shares, you must apply for 50, 100, 150 shares, and so on. The lot size for the current IPOs is not specified in our data, but it's a crucial detail for investors.
  3. Minimum Investment: This is calculated by multiplying the lot size by the lower end of the price band. For instance, if Laser Power & Infra had a lot size of 70 shares, your minimum investment would be 70 shares * ₹203 = ₹14,210. This tells you the smallest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO.
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public, or the total value of these shares. For example, SBI Funds Management is offering 170,956,631 shares. This figure helps you understand the scale of the IPO.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer for Sale (OFS): An IPO can be a combination of these two. A fresh issue means the company is selling new shares, and the money raised goes directly to the company for its business needs. An Offer for Sale (OFS) means existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) are selling some of their shares, and the money goes to them, not the company. This distinction tells you whether the company itself is getting funds for growth.
  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the official, detailed document filed by the company with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India). It contains everything you need to know about the company, its business, financials, risks, management, and the IPO details. Reading this document helps you understand the company thoroughly.

The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO might seem complex, but it follows a clear sequence:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files a DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) with SEBI. This document contains all the preliminary information about the company and its plans for the IPO.
  2. Price Band & Dates Announcement: Once SEBI approves the DRHP, the company announces the price band (the price range for shares) and the open date and close date for the IPO.
  3. Bidding Period: During the open dates, investors can place their bids for shares. Applications are divided into different categories:

    4. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Ordinary investors who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs): Investors who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign portfolio investors.

  4. Allotment: After the IPO closes, the company and its registrars decide how to distribute the shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than available shares), shares are typically allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
  5. Listing: Finally, the shares get listed on the stock exchange (NSE or BSE). This is when they start trading, and you can buy or sell them like any other stock.

A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a popular school fete stall that makes delicious samosas. The stall owner wants to buy a bigger fryer and more ingredients to make even more samosas. To raise money, they decide to sell "ownership coupons" for their stall.

When you buy these coupons during the initial sale (the IPO), you become a small part-owner of the samosa stall. The money you pay goes to the owner to help them expand. Once the initial sale is over, these coupons can then be traded among other students at the fete. The price of these coupons might go up if the samosas become even more popular, or down if the quality drops. You're not just buying samosas; you're buying a piece of the business that makes them!

Things people get confused about

Here are some common misconceptions about IPOs:

  • Myth: Listing gain is guaranteed.

    • * Fact: While some IPOs do list at a higher price than their issue price, this is not guaranteed. Share prices can go up or down on listing day and afterwards, depending on market conditions, company performance, and investor sentiment.

  • Myth: Applying for more lots increases my chances of getting an allotment in the retail category.

    • * Fact: For retail investors, if an IPO is oversubscribed, allotment is typically done through a lottery system. Applying for more than one lot in the retail category (up to ₹2 lakhs) does not increase your chances; each unique applicant has an equal chance in the lottery.

  • Myth: IPOs are only for rich people or big investors.

    • * Fact: IPOs are open to everyone. Retail individual investors can apply for shares with an investment as low as the minimum lot size, which often works out to ₹14,000-₹15,000.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank or stockbroker using the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility. Your application money is blocked in your bank account but only debited if you are allotted shares.

What is a lot size?

The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot apply for individual shares; you must bid in multiples of this specified lot.

What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME IPOs are for smaller companies, listed on dedicated SME platforms, often with different regulations and trading rules.

What happens if an IPO is oversubscribed?

If an IPO receives more applications than available shares, it is oversubscribed. For retail investors, shares are usually allotted through a lottery system. If you don't get an allotment, your blocked application money is released back to your account.

Can I sell my shares on the first day of listing?

Yes, once the shares are officially listed and trading begins on the stock exchange, you are free to sell them at the prevailing market price, just like any other stock.
