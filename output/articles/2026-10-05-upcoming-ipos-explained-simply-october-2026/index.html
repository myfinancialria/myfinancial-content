First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local sweet shop that has been doing well for years. Now, the owners want to open more branches or buy new machines to make even more sweets. To get this money, they decide to sell a small part of their shop's ownership to the public for the very first time.

This act of a private company selling its shares to the public for the first time is called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). When a company does an IPO, it is selling shares in the primary market (the market where new securities are sold). Once these shares are sold, they then get listed (made available for trading) on a stock exchange like the NSE or BSE, where people can buy and sell them among themselves.

How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO announcement, you'll see a few key terms. Understanding them helps you make sense of the offering:

  1. Price Band: This is the price range (e.g., ₹70 to ₹75 for Nityas Gems and Jewellery) within which investors can bid for shares. You usually bid at the 'cut-off price', which means you agree to pay the final price discovered within this band. If it's a single price (like ₹1000 for SMC Global Securities), it's a fixed price issue.
  2. Lot Size: Shares in an IPO are not sold individually. You must apply for a minimum number of shares, which is called a 'lot'. For example, if a lot size is 200 shares, you must apply for 200 shares or multiples of 200 (like 400, 600, etc.).
  3. Minimum Investment: This is the smallest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO. It's calculated by multiplying the lot size by the upper end of the price band. For instance, if Nityas Gems and Jewellery had a lot size of 200 shares and an upper price band of ₹75, your minimum investment would be 200 shares * ₹75 = ₹15,000.
  4. Issue Size: This refers to the total value or number of shares the company plans to sell through the IPO. For example, Vishal Nirmiti Limited is offering 8,090,909 shares. This number helps you understand the scale of the IPO.
  5. Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS): This tells you where the money from the IPO is going.

    6. * A Fresh Issue means the company is selling new shares, and the money raised goes directly to the company for its business needs (like expansion or debt repayment). * An Offer-for-Sale (OFS) means existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) are selling some of their shares. The money from an OFS goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself. You can find this breakdown in the company's official documents.

  6. RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus): This is the most important document for any IPO. It's a detailed legal document filed with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) that contains all the information about the company, its business, financials, risks, management, and the IPO details. Always read this document to understand the company better.

The IPO process, step by step

Here's how an IPO typically unfolds:

  1. DRHP Filing: The company first files a Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI. This document contains all the preliminary information about the company and its plans for the IPO.
  2. SEBI Approval & RHP: SEBI reviews the DRHP. Once satisfied, SEBI gives its approval, and the company then files the final Red Herring Prospectus (RHP), which includes any changes requested by SEBI.
  3. Price Band Announcement: The company, along with its investment bankers, announces the price band (the range within which shares will be sold) and the lot size.
  4. Bidding Period: The IPO opens for a few days (usually 3-5 days). During this time, investors can place their bids for shares. There are different categories of investors:

    5. * Retail Individual Investors (RIIs): Ordinary individuals applying for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. * High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs) / Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs): Individuals or entities applying for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. * Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs): Large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign portfolio investors.

  5. Allotment: After the bidding period closes, the shares are allocated to investors. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more applications than shares available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
  6. Listing: Finally, the shares get listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE). This is when the shares start trading publicly, and their price can go up or down based on market demand and supply.

A simple way to think about it

Think of a popular school fete stall that sells delicious homemade snacks. Everyone loves them, and the stall owner wants to expand, maybe buy a bigger fryer or hire more helpers. To raise money, the owner decides to sell "ownership coupons" for a small part of the stall. These coupons are like shares.

When the owner sells these coupons for the first time, that's the IPO. People buy these coupons hoping the stall will become even more popular and their coupons will be worth more later. Once sold, these coupons can then be traded among students in the school playground – that's like the shares getting listed on the stock market.

Things people get confused about

It's easy to get caught up in the excitement around IPOs, but here are some common misunderstandings:

  • Myth: "Listing gain is guaranteed in every IPO."

    • * Fact: While some IPOs might list at a higher price than their issue price (a "listing gain"), this is not guaranteed. The listing price depends on market conditions, investor demand, and the company's fundamentals. Shares can also list at a discount.

  • Myth: "You must apply for every IPO that comes out."

    • * Fact: Applying for an IPO is a personal choice. It's important to research the company, understand its business, and assess the risks before deciding to apply. Not every IPO is suitable for every investor.

  • Myth: "IPOs are always a quick way to get rich."

    • * Fact: Like any investment in the stock market, IPOs carry risks. The value of shares can go down after listing. Investing in IPOs should be part of a well-thought-out financial plan, not a get-rich-quick scheme.

FAQ

How do I even apply for an IPO?

You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility or through your stockbroker's online platform. Your money remains in your bank account until shares are allotted.

What is a lot size?

A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for in an IPO. You cannot buy individual shares; you must apply in multiples of the specified lot.

What is a Mainboard IPO vs. an SME IPO?

Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME IPOs are for smaller companies that list on dedicated SME platforms of these exchanges, often with different regulations and minimum investment amounts.

Can I apply for multiple lots?

Yes, you can apply for multiple lots. However, as a retail investor, your total application value cannot exceed ₹2 lakhs. For applications above ₹2 lakhs, you fall into the HNI/NII category.

What happens if I don't get an allotment?

If you don't receive an allotment of shares, the money that was blocked in your bank account for the IPO application will be unblocked or refunded to you.
