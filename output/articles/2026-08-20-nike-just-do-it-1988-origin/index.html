The Death-Row Confession That Birthed 'Just Do It' — Nike's $43B Three-Word Bet

In a cramped conference room in 1988, Dan Wieden, co-founder of the nascent advertising agency Wieden+Kennedy, faced a dilemma. His team had crafted five disparate television commercials for Nike, but they lacked a unifying punch, a cohesive message to tie them together. Staring at the wall, Wieden remembered a grim story from his home state of Oregon. Gary Gilmore, a convicted murderer, had faced a Utah firing squad in 1977. When asked for his last words, Gilmore reportedly offered a chillingly resolute, "Let's do it."

Wieden didn't like the "Let's" but the raw, unyielding intention of "Do it" resonated with him. He tweaked it slightly, simplifying it to three potent words: "Just Do It." It was a gamble, a morbid inspiration for what would become one of the most iconic and successful advertising slogans in history. Nike's co-founder Phil Knight initially dismissed it, reportedly saying, "We don't need that shit." But Wieden insisted, urging, "Just trust me on this one." That trust, built on a three-word bet, would propel Nike from a struggling contender to a global powerhouse, transforming its worldwide sales from $877 million in 1988 to $9.2 billion by 1998.

The World Before

Before "Just Do It" became a cultural phenomenon, the athletic footwear landscape looked very different, and Nike, despite its early successes, was facing a significant challenge. In the early 1980s, Nike had established itself as a respected manufacturer of high-quality running shoes, primarily targeting serious male athletes and advertising in niche publications like Runner's World. They were innovative, but their focus was narrow.

Meanwhile, a new wave was sweeping the fitness world: aerobics. Women, previously underserved by the athletic shoe industry, flocked to classes, creating a massive, untapped market. Reebok, a competitor, spotted this opportunity and launched the Freestyle, the first athletic shoe designed specifically for women, in 1982. This strategic move, combined with the burgeoning popularity of fitness icons like Jane Fonda, allowed Reebok to rapidly gain traction. By the mid-1980s, Reebok had not only caught up but surpassed Nike in U.S. sales, becoming the market leader. In 1988, Reebok's revenues hit $1.8 billion, while Nike trailed at $1.2 billion in consolidated revenues, or $877 million in worldwide sales, depending on the reporting metric.

Nike was in a slump. In 1987, the company was forced to lay off 20% of its workforce, a stark indicator of its precarious position. The brand, once a frontrunner, was losing ground and needed a radical shift. They recognized the need to move beyond just selling products to elite athletes; they had to sell an idea, a lifestyle, an emotion that could resonate with a broader, more diverse audience, including women and general fitness enthusiasts. This was the context when Nike turned to Wieden+Kennedy for a national television campaign – a significant investment for a company that had traditionally been skeptical about the power of mass advertising.

The Crack

The challenge was to create a message that could unify disparate ads and speak to everyone, regardless of age, gender, or athletic ability. The initial television spots Wieden+Kennedy developed were varied, some humorous, some serious, and Wieden felt they needed a common thread. It was during this critical period, the night before a major presentation to Nike, that Wieden found his unlikely inspiration in the story of Gary Gilmore.

Wieden presented the three words, "Just Do It," to a roomful of Nike executives. Despite Phil Knight's initial reluctance, the team at Wieden+Kennedy believed in the slogan's power. The phrase was simple, direct, and emotionally charged, tapping into the universal feeling of hesitation before an undertaking and offering a powerful push to overcome it. It didn't focus on product features or technology; it focused on human ambition and determination.

The "Just Do It" campaign officially launched in 1988 with a $20 million budget. Its very first television commercial, which aired on August 7, 1988, featured an 80-year-old marathon runner named Walt Stack. The ad showed Stack jogging across the Golden Gate Bridge, speaking candidly about his daily 17-mile run and jokingly remarking that he kept his teeth from chattering in winter by leaving them in his locker. This choice was deliberate: instead of focusing on celebrity athletes like Michael Jordan (who Nike had recently signed), the campaign highlighted an ordinary person making an extraordinary effort, signaling that sport was for everyone.

The public response was immediate and deeply personal. People called Nike, sharing how the slogan motivated them in various aspects of their lives, not just sports. It became a rallying cry, a mindset that transcended mere advertising, fostering a sense of community and belonging around the brand.

What Happened Next

The impact of "Just Do It" was nothing short of transformative for Nike. In the decade following its launch, from 1988 to 1998, Nike's worldwide sales skyrocketed from $877 million to $9.2 billion. The company's share of the North American domestic sport-shoe business more than doubled, climbing from 18% to a commanding 43%. Nike had not only regained its footing but had sprinted far ahead of its competitors, leaving Reebok, which had once been the market leader, struggling to keep pace.

The slogan became synonymous with the Nike brand, achieving such widespread recognition that Ad Age ranked it as the second-best advertising slogan of the 20th century, surpassed only by "A diamond is forever." "Just Do It" wasn't just a tagline; it became a cultural touchstone, a motivational mantra that seeped into everyday language. It helped Nike position itself not merely as a seller of athletic gear, but as a brand embodying a personal philosophy of determination, perseverance, and empowerment.

Today, Nike continues to dominate the global athletic apparel and footwear market. The brand's value has soared into the tens of billions of dollars, with Brand Finance valuing it at $31.3 billion in 2023 and Kantar reporting it as the most valuable apparel brand at $71.6 billion in 2024. The "Just Do It" philosophy has been consistently applied across decades of campaigns, featuring legendary athletes like Michael Jordan, Serena Williams, and LeBron James, as well as addressing broader social issues, further cementing Nike's place not just in sports, but in global culture.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. A Powerful Message Trumps Product Features: In a crowded market, Nike's success with "Just Do It" demonstrates that a compelling, emotional message can be far more effective than simply listing product specifications. While competitors focused on cushioning technology, Nike owned the abstract concepts of ambition, determination, and overcoming personal hurdles. This allowed the brand to connect with consumers on a deeper, more personal level.
  2. Broad Appeal Expands Your Market: Nike learned the hard way that catering exclusively to a niche (elite male athletes) limits growth. "Just Do It" was intentionally universal, speaking to everyone from seasoned marathoners to casual walkers, and crucially, to women who had been overlooked. By broadening its appeal beyond a narrow demographic, Nike unlocked exponential growth and became relevant to a much larger segment of the population.
  3. Consistency Builds Cultural Iconography: Despite experimenting with various sub-campaigns and taglines over the years, Nike never abandoned "Just Do It." This unwavering commitment allowed the three words to embed themselves deeply into the global psyche, transforming from a marketing slogan into a widely recognized cultural mantra. Long-term consistency in messaging creates an enduring brand identity that temporary campaigns cannot achieve.
  4. Embrace Risk for Breakthroughs: The origin of "Just Do It" from Gary Gilmore's last words was unconventional, even controversial. Phil Knight's initial skepticism highlights the resistance to truly novel, risky ideas. However, Dan Wieden's conviction, and Nike's eventual trust in his vision, paid off immensely. Sometimes, the most impactful innovations in marketing come from unexpected places and require a willingness to take a chance on a bold idea.
