FII Tax Exemption on G-Secs: What Changed and Why it Matters

TL;DR

  • The Indian government has exempted Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) from taxes on interest income and capital gains derived from Indian Government Securities (G-Secs).
  • This strategic move aims to attract more global capital into India's sovereign debt market, enhancing its depth and liquidity.
  • The exemption is expected to help stabilise the Indian Rupee by increasing foreign currency inflows.
  • Foreign investors can now enjoy higher net returns from G-Secs, making these instruments more attractive compared to other emerging markets.
  • This policy change could have a positive ripple effect on bond yields and overall market sentiment in India.

What this means in plain terms

The Indian government has made a significant policy change to encourage more foreign money to flow into its bonds. Previously, when foreign investors bought Indian government bonds (G-Secs), they had to pay taxes on the interest they earned and also on any profits they made when selling these bonds. Now, these taxes have been removed for eligible foreign investors.

Essentially, this means that for Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs), investing in Indian G-Secs has become more profitable. They get to keep a larger share of their earnings, making Indian government bonds a more attractive option globally. The government's goal is to bring in more foreign currency, strengthen the Rupee, and make India's bond market a deeper and more vibrant place for international investment.

What Changed and Why

The core of this policy shift lies in the exemption of specific tax liabilities for Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) investing in Indian Government Securities (G-Secs). Specifically, the government has exempted FIIs from paying income tax on the interest earned from G-Secs and also from capital gains tax on the sale of these securities. This exemption applies to both short-term and long-term capital gains, making the entire investment cycle more tax-efficient for foreign entities.

This move is driven by several strategic objectives. Firstly, India aims to attract a larger share of global capital flows, particularly into its sovereign debt market. By removing the tax burden, the net yield for foreign investors increases, making Indian G-Secs more competitive against similar instruments in other emerging economies. Secondly, a significant inflow of foreign capital is expected to help stabilise the Indian Rupee. Increased demand for the Rupee from foreign investors purchasing G-Secs can strengthen its value against major global currencies. Thirdly, this measure seeks to deepen and broaden the Indian bond market, enhancing its liquidity and efficiency. A more active and diverse investor base can lead to better price discovery and market stability. The Ministry of Finance has spearheaded this initiative, reflecting a concerted effort to integrate India further into the global financial architecture.

How Foreign Investors Access G-Secs

Foreign investors primarily access Indian Government Securities through specific routes established by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI). The two prominent routes are:

Fully Accessible Route (FAR)

Introduced in 2020, the FAR allows non-resident investors to invest in specified G-Secs without any restrictions on the quantum of investment. This route is designed to provide full access to certain benchmark G-Secs, making them attractive for large institutional investors. Securities under FAR are identified by the RBI and are typically longer-dated bonds. This route is crucial for India's eventual inclusion in global bond indices.

Voluntary Retention Route (VRR)

The VRR was introduced in 2019 to encourage long-term foreign investment in Indian debt markets. Under VRR, FIIs commit to investing a certain amount in Indian debt for a minimum retention period. In return, they are granted certain operational flexibilities and exemptions from macro-prudential and other regulatory norms applicable to foreign portfolio investments. This route is popular among investors seeking stable, long-term exposure to Indian debt.

Both routes require FIIs to register with SEBI as Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs). Once registered, they can invest in G-Secs through approved custodians and depositories. The process typically involves opening a demat account and a special non-resident rupee account (SNRR account) for transactions. The recent tax exemption significantly enhances the appeal of both FAR and VRR for eligible foreign investors.

Key Provisions of the Exemption

The tax exemption for FIIs on G-Secs is a targeted measure with specific provisions to ensure its efficacy and adherence to policy goals. The exemption covers two primary components:

Exemption on Interest Income

FIIs are now exempt from paying income tax on the interest earned from their investments in Indian G-Secs. This directly boosts the coupon returns for foreign investors. Previously, interest income was subject to a withholding tax, which reduced the effective yield. The removal of this tax makes the coupon payments more attractive on a post-tax basis.

Exemption on Capital Gains

Any capital gains realised by FIIs from the sale or transfer of G-Secs are also exempt from income tax. This includes both short-term capital gains (assets held for 36 months or less) and long-term capital gains (assets held for more than 36 months). The absence of capital gains tax significantly improves the overall return profile, especially for active bond traders or funds that manage their portfolios based on yield curve movements.

The eligibility for this exemption is generally tied to FIIs registered as Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPIs) with SEBI. The types of G-Secs covered typically include central government dated securities and treasury bills. The effective date of this exemption is crucial, as it applies to income and gains arising from a specified date, as notified by the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT). This ensures clarity and retrospective application where intended, providing immediate benefits to foreign investors.

Potential Impact on India's Markets

The FII tax exemption on G-Secs is poised to have a multi-faceted impact on India's financial markets and broader economy.

Strengthening the Rupee

A primary objective is to attract substantial foreign capital inflows. As FIIs increase their investments in G-Secs, they will need to convert foreign currency into Indian Rupees, thereby increasing demand for the Rupee. This increased demand is expected to help stabilise and potentially strengthen the Rupee against major global currencies, which can be beneficial for controlling imported inflation and improving India's trade balance.

Deepening the Bond Market

The exemption makes Indian G-Secs more attractive, potentially leading to increased participation from a wider range of global investors. This influx of capital can enhance the liquidity and depth of the bond market. A deeper market allows for better price discovery, reduces volatility, and provides more efficient avenues for government borrowing. It also paves the way for India's inclusion in global bond indices, which could unlock passive foreign investment worth billions of dollars.

Impact on Bond Yields

Increased foreign demand for G-Secs could lead to higher bond prices and, consequently, lower bond yields. Lower government borrowing costs would free up fiscal space, allowing the government to allocate more funds towards infrastructure development or social welfare programmes. However, the actual impact on yields will also depend on domestic inflation, RBI's monetary policy, and global interest rate movements.

Spillover to Equity Markets

While the exemption directly targets debt, a stronger Rupee and a more stable economic outlook can positively influence equity markets. Foreign investors might view India as a more attractive overall investment destination, leading to increased FPI inflows into equities as well. Furthermore, lower bond yields can make equities relatively more appealing, potentially driving up valuations.

Overall, this policy change signals India's commitment to liberalising its financial markets and integrating further into the global financial system, aiming for enhanced economic stability and growth.

A real example

Let's consider Sarah, 42, a Fund Manager at 'Global Alpha Investments' in London, managing a portfolio focused on emerging market debt. Her fund typically invests in government bonds of various developing nations.

Before the tax exemption, Sarah's fund considered investing ₹100 crores in a 10-year Indian G-Sec with a coupon rate of 7.25% per annum.

Scenario 1: Before Tax Exemption

  • Investment: ₹100 crores
  • Annual Interest Income: 7.25% of ₹100 crores = ₹7.25 crores
  • Withholding Tax on Interest (e.g., 5% as per DTAA): 5% of ₹7.25 crores = ₹0.3625 crores
  • Net Annual Interest Income (after tax): ₹7.25 crores - ₹0.3625 crores = ₹6.8875 crores

Now, suppose after 18 months, Sarah's fund sells these G-Secs for ₹102 crores, making a capital gain.

  • Capital Gain: ₹102 crores - ₹100 crores = ₹2 crores
  • Short-Term Capital Gains Tax (e.g., 30%): 30% of ₹2 crores = ₹0.60 crores
  • Net Capital Gain (after tax): ₹2 crores - ₹0.60 crores = ₹1.40 crores

Scenario 2: After Tax Exemption

With the new tax exemption for FIIs on G-Secs:

  • Investment: ₹100 crores
  • Annual Interest Income: 7.25% of ₹100 crores = ₹7.25 crores
  • Withholding Tax on Interest: ₹0 (Exempt)
  • Net Annual Interest Income (after tax): ₹7.25 crores (Full amount retained)

And upon selling the G-Secs for ₹102 crores:

  • Capital Gain: ₹2 crores
  • Short-Term Capital Gains Tax: ₹0 (Exempt)
  • Net Capital Gain (after tax): ₹2 crores (Full amount retained)

Comparison: In Scenario 1, Sarah's fund would have received ₹6.8875 crores in net interest and ₹1.40 crores in net capital gains. In Scenario 2, with the exemption, her fund receives the full ₹7.25 crores in net interest and the full ₹2 crores in net capital gains.

This exemption significantly increases the post-tax returns for Global Alpha Investments, making Indian G-Secs a more appealing and competitive asset class for Sarah to allocate her fund's capital.
