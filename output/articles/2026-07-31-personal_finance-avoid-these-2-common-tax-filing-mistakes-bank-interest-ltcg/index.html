Avoid These 2 Common Tax Filing Mistakes: Bank Interest & LTCG

TL;DR

  • Many taxpayers incorrectly assume that income with TDS deducted or tax-exempt capital gains do not need to be reported in their Income Tax Return (ITR).
  • Omitting such details can lead to discrepancies between your ITR and the information available with the Income Tax Department, such as Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Form 26AS.
  • Even if no additional tax is payable, non-disclosure can trigger tax notices from the department, requiring you to explain the mismatch.
  • It is mandatory to report all sources of income, including interest from savings accounts, fixed deposits, and long-term capital gains from equity, regardless of taxability or TDS deduction.
  • Proactively reconciling your financial statements with AIS and Form 26AS before filing your ITR can help prevent future complications.

What this means in plain terms

When you file your Income Tax Return (ITR), the Income Tax Department expects a full disclosure of all your income sources, even if some of that income has already had tax deducted at source (TDS) or is considered tax-exempt. Many individuals mistakenly believe that if TDS has been applied to their bank interest, or if their Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG) from equity investments fall below the exemption limit, they don't need to mention these details in their ITR. This is a common and significant error.

The Income Tax Department now has sophisticated systems like the Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Form 26AS, which capture almost all your financial transactions, including interest earned and capital gains. If the income declared in your ITR does not match the information in these statements, it creates a "mismatch." Such discrepancies can automatically trigger a tax notice, asking for clarification or even leading to adjustments in your tax assessment, even if you owe no extra tax. The core principle is full disclosure, ensuring transparency and accuracy in your tax filings.

Reporting All Bank Interest Income

One of the most frequent omissions in ITRs is the complete reporting of bank interest income. This includes interest earned from savings accounts, fixed deposits (FDs), recurring deposits (RDs), and even post office savings schemes. Taxpayers often make two key assumptions that lead to non-reporting:

Interest Below TDS Threshold

Many believe that if the interest earned is below the threshold for Tax Deducted at Source (TDS), currently ₹40,000 for most individuals (₹50,000 for senior citizens) for bank/post office interest under Section 194A, it doesn't need to be reported. This is incorrect. TDS is merely an advance tax payment mechanism. Regardless of whether TDS was deducted or not, all interest income must be declared in your ITR. The taxability of this income is then determined based on your total income and applicable slab rates.

Claiming Deductions like 80TTA/80TTB

While Section 80TTA allows a deduction of up to ₹10,000 for interest from savings accounts for individuals and HUFs, and Section 80TTB allows up to ₹50,000 for senior citizens on interest from savings, FDs, and RDs, this deduction comes after you have fully reported the gross interest income. You cannot simply exclude the interest income from your ITR because you intend to claim a deduction. The full interest amount must first be declared under "Income from Other Sources," and then the eligible deduction can be claimed. Failing to report the gross interest creates a mismatch with your AIS, which will reflect all interest credited to your accounts.

Disclosing Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG)

Another critical area of oversight is the reporting of Long Term Capital Gains, particularly those that are exempt from tax. This typically applies to gains from the sale of equity shares or equity-oriented mutual funds.

Exempt LTCG from Equity and Equity MFs

Under Section 112A of the Income Tax Act, LTCG exceeding ₹1 lakh from the sale of equity shares (listed) or equity-oriented mutual funds held for more than 12 months is taxable at 10% (without indexation). However, gains up to ₹1 lakh in a financial year are exempt from tax. Many taxpayers interpret this exemption to mean they don't need to report these gains at all. This is a significant misconception.

Even if your LTCG is fully exempt (i.e., ₹1 lakh or less), you are still required to report it in your ITR. These gains need to be declared in Schedule 112A of the ITR form. The Income Tax Department receives information about all your equity and mutual fund transactions from depositories and asset management companies. If you omit these details, your ITR will not align with the data available to the tax authorities, potentially leading to a notice. It's about disclosure, not just tax payment.

The Crucial Role of AIS and Form 26AS

The Income Tax Department has significantly enhanced its data analytics capabilities, primarily through the Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Form 26AS. These documents are powerful tools that provide the department with a comprehensive view of your financial transactions.

Understanding AIS

The Annual Information Statement (AIS) provides a consolidated view of all financial information related to a taxpayer, including interest, dividends, securities transactions, mutual fund transactions, foreign remittances, and more. It is much more comprehensive than Form 26AS. The information in AIS is sourced from various reporting entities like banks, mutual fund houses, brokers, etc.

Understanding Form 26AS

Form 26AS is your consolidated annual tax statement, which primarily reflects details of tax deducted at source (TDS), tax collected at source (TCS), advance tax paid, self-assessment tax paid, and details of high-value transactions. While AIS is broader, Form 26AS remains crucial for verifying TDS credits.

Why Mismatches Matter

When you file your ITR, the data you provide is cross-referenced with the information contained in your AIS and Form 26AS. If there is a mismatch – for example, if your bank has reported ₹50,000 in interest to the Income Tax Department (which shows up in your AIS), but you only reported ₹10,000 in your ITR – the system flags this discrepancy. This can lead to:

  • Intimation under Section 143(1): An automated notice pointing out the mismatch and potentially adjusting your income, leading to a demand for additional tax or a reduction in refund.
  • Scrutiny Notice under Section 142(1) or 148: A more detailed inquiry requiring you to provide explanations and documents.
  • Penalties: In cases of deliberate misreporting or under-reporting of income, penalties can be levied.

Therefore, it is imperative to download and review your AIS and Form 26AS before filing your ITR to ensure all reported income and transactions are accurately reflected in your return. You can access these documents through the e-filing portal of the Income Tax Department.

A real example

Priya, 42, ₹22L CTC, Gurugram

Priya is a salaried professional earning ₹22 lakhs per annum. In the financial year 2024-25, she had the following additional income:

  1. Bank Interest:

    2. * Savings account interest: ₹8,500 * Fixed Deposit interest: ₹45,000 (Bank deducted ₹4,500 as TDS, as it exceeded ₹40,000) Total Bank Interest: ₹53,500*

  1. Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG):

    2. * Sold equity mutual funds held for 2 years, resulting in a gain of ₹75,000. This gain is exempt under Section 112A as it is below the ₹1 lakh threshold.

Priya, like many, thought:

  • "My savings interest is below ₹10,000, so it's exempt under 80TTA, no need to report."
  • "For the FD interest, TDS has already been deducted, so the bank has handled it. I'll just adjust for the net amount."
  • "My LTCG is exempt, so I don't need to show it anywhere."

Based on these assumptions, Priya only reported her salary income in her ITR.

The Outcome:

  1. Bank Interest Mismatch: Priya's bank reported the total interest of ₹53,500 to the Income Tax Department. This amount appeared in her AIS. In her ITR, she reported ₹0 for interest income.
  2. LTCG Mismatch: The mutual fund house reported the sale of units and the gain of ₹75,000 to the Income Tax Department. This also appeared in her AIS. In her ITR, she reported ₹0 for capital gains.

Approximately six months after filing her ITR, Priya received an intimation notice under Section 143(1) from the Income Tax Department. The notice highlighted a mismatch of ₹53,500 in interest income and ₹75,000 in capital gains, proposing to add these amounts to her income.

  • For the interest income, the department added the full ₹53,500 to her income. While she could have claimed ₹10,000 under Section 80TTA, by not reporting the gross income, she complicated the process.
  • For the LTCG, even though it was exempt, the non-disclosure led to a query. She had to respond to the notice, explain the situation, and provide proof of the gain and its exempt nature.

Even though Priya might not have owed significant additional tax (after claiming 80TTA and proving LTCG exemption), the process of responding to the notice, gathering documents, and clarifying the situation was time-consuming and stressful, all due to common reporting mistakes.

What to do this week

  1. Download Your AIS and Form 26AS: Log in to the Income Tax e-filing portal (incometax.gov.in) and download your Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Form 26AS for the relevant financial year.
  2. Reconcile Bank Statements with AIS: Compare all interest entries (savings, FDs, RDs) from your bank statements and Form 16A (for TDS on FDs) with the interest income reported in your AIS. Ensure every rupee of interest is accounted for.
  3. Review Capital Gains Data: Check your AIS for details of equity and mutual fund transactions. Compare these with your broker statements or mutual fund consolidated account statement to ensure all capital gains/losses, even exempt ones, are captured.
  4. Identify Discrepancies: Note down any mismatches between your personal records and the AIS/26AS. If you find errors in AIS, you can submit feedback on the portal.
  5. Gather Proof for Non-Taxable Income: If you have any income that is genuinely non-taxable (e.g., agricultural income, gifts from relatives), ensure you have adequate documentation to support its nature, in case of future queries.

FAQ

Do I need to report bank interest if TDS has already been deducted?

Yes, absolutely. TDS is only a part payment of your tax liability. You must report the gross interest income in your ITR, and then claim credit for the TDS deducted.

My Long Term Capital Gains (LTCG) from equity are below ₹1 lakh, so they are exempt. Do I still need to report them?

Yes, even if your LTCG from equity or equity-oriented mutual funds is exempt (up to ₹1 lakh under Section 112A), you must still report the full amount in Schedule 112A of your ITR. Non-reporting can lead to mismatches with your AIS.

What is the Annual Information Statement (AIS) and why is it important?

AIS is a comprehensive statement provided by the Income Tax Department that consolidates all financial information related to a taxpayer, including interest, dividends, securities transactions, and more. It is crucial because the department uses this data for cross-verification with your ITR.

What happens if there's a mismatch between my ITR and AIS/Form 26AS?

A mismatch can trigger an intimation notice under Section 143(1) or even a scrutiny notice. You would then need to explain the discrepancy and provide supporting documents, which can be time-consuming and stressful.

Can I revise my ITR if I realise I've missed reporting some income?

Yes, if you discover an error or omission after filing your original ITR, you can file a revised return under Section 139(5) of the Income Tax Act. This can usually be done up to 31st December of the assessment year, or before the completion of assessment, whichever is earlier.

Is interest from a savings account also considered taxable income?

Yes, interest earned from a savings account is taxable under the head "Income from Other Sources." While Section 80TTA allows a deduction of up to ₹10,000 for such interest, the gross amount must first be reported in your ITR.
