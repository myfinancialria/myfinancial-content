The Death-Row Confession That Birthed 'Just Do It' — Nike's $43B Three-Word Bet

January 17, 1977. Dawn broke cold and bleak over the Utah State Prison in Draper. Gary Gilmore, a convicted double-murderer, was led to an abandoned cannery behind the prison, strapped into a chair, his face covered by a black hood. Five rifles were aimed at his chest. Asked if he had any last words, Gilmore didn't beg or philosophize. He simply said, "Let's do it."

More than a decade later, in 1988, that chilling phrase echoed in the mind of advertising executive Dan Wieden. He was struggling to find a unifying tagline for a series of Nike commercials, a phrase potent enough to tie together diverse stories of athleticism. "I was recalling a man in Portland," Wieden later recounted, "who murdered a man and a woman, and was sent to jail and put before a firing squad. And they asked him if he had any final thoughts and he said: 'Let's do it.'" Wieden tweaked it slightly, removing the "Let's." The result was three words that would transcend advertising to become a global cultural mantra: "Just Do It." Nike's co-founder, Phil Knight, initially disliked the idea, dismissing it with, "We don't need that shit." But Wieden convinced him, saying, "Just trust me on this one." That gamble, inspired by a death-row confession, would redefine Nike, catapulting its revenue from $877 million in 1988 to a staggering $9.2 billion by 1998.

The World Before

Before "Just Do It," Nike, though respected, was battling for its life. The early 1980s had seen the company establish itself as a serious contender, especially among elite runners and male athletes. However, by the mid-decade, a formidable challenger had emerged: Reebok. Reebok had identified an underserved market – women participating in the burgeoning aerobics craze. Their 1982 Freestyle shoe, specifically designed for women, tapped directly into this cultural phenomenon, propelling Reebok to market leadership. By 1988, Reebok controlled more than a quarter of the U.S. athletic footwear market, boasting revenues of $1.8 billion, while Nike trailed at $1.2 billion in consolidated revenues. The competitive pressure was so intense that Nike was forced to lay off 20% of its workforce in 1987.

Nike's brand identity, while strong in performance, was seen as niche, appealing primarily to serious male athletes. They needed to pivot, to broaden their appeal beyond just product features and reach a mass audience, embracing an idea, a lifestyle, an emotion. The perception was that Nike made shoes for the pros, leaving many everyday fitness enthusiasts feeling excluded. The market needed a message that resonated with everyone, from the casual jogger to the seasoned marathoner, from women embracing fitness to men hitting the gym. This was the landscape when Dan Wieden presented his audacious, three-word solution.

The Crack

Dan Wieden, co-founder of the then-fledgling advertising agency Wieden+Kennedy, was tasked with creating Nike's first major national television campaign. He recognized that the five different commercials they had produced lacked a cohesive theme. He needed a tagline that could unify them, a universal yet intensely personal message. That's when the memory of Gary Gilmore's final defiant words, "Let's do it," resurfaced. Wieden admired the raw, decisive conviction behind the phrase, the notion of pushing through uncertainty and facing an ultimate challenge. He decided to adapt it, crafting "Just Do It."

Despite Phil Knight's initial skepticism, Wieden's conviction prevailed, and the slogan debuted on July 1, 1988. The first commercial featuring "Just Do It" wasn't about a superstar athlete, but an 80-year-old marathon runner from San Francisco named Walt Stack. The ad showed Stack, a rugged construction worker, jogging his daily 17 miles across the Golden Gate Bridge, talking about his routine. This deliberate choice was revolutionary. Instead of focusing solely on elite performance, Nike pivoted to celebrate the everyday athlete, the spirit of perseverance in everyone. The campaign aimed to target all Americans, regardless of age, sex, or physical fitness level, positioning sneakers as more than just gear – but as a statement of personal will.

This was a critical moment. While Reebok was still riding the wave of the aerobics trend, Nike's "Just Do It" cut through with a tougher, more universal message of empowerment. It wasn't about what you did, but that you did it. It was about overcoming internal resistance, pushing past limits, and embracing action. This shift from product-centric marketing to an emotion-driven, aspirational brand identity proved to be a masterstroke.

What Happened Next

The impact of "Just Do It" was immediate and profound. The slogan, coupled with Nike's iconic "Swoosh" logo, helped transform the company from a niche running-shoe manufacturer into a global powerhouse. Nike's share of the North American domestic sport-shoe business dramatically increased from 18% in 1988 to a commanding 43% by 1998.

Financially, the rise was equally meteoric. As noted, Nike's worldwide sales surged from $877 million in 1988 to $9.2 billion by 1998. While fiscal 1998 did see a slight dip in net income due to the Asian economic crisis and restructuring charges, the overall decade was one of unprecedented growth, cementing Nike's dominance over competitors like Reebok. The slogan resonated so deeply that Ad Age later ranked "Just Do It" as the second-best advertising slogan of the 20th century, behind only "A diamond is forever." It became more than a tagline; it was an ethos, an invitation, and a challenge.

Lessons to Take Away

  1. Embrace Emotional Resonance Over Product Features: Nike's success with "Just Do It" demonstrates that connecting with consumers on an emotional level can be far more powerful than merely highlighting product specifications. The slogan tapped into universal themes of struggle, determination, and achievement, making the brand relatable to a much broader audience than just elite athletes.
  2. Boldness Can Be a Breakthrough: Dan Wieden's decision to draw inspiration from an unconventional, even dark, source and then persist despite internal skepticism shows the power of bold, counter-intuitive thinking. Sometimes, the most impactful ideas come from unexpected places and require a willingness to take calculated risks.
  3. A Slogan as a Statement of Purpose: "Just Do It" transcended a mere marketing catchphrase; it became a declaration of Nike's brand purpose. It wasn't just about selling shoes, but about inspiring action and empowering individuals. A truly great slogan acts as a mission statement, guiding both internal culture and external messaging.
  4. Inclusivity Expands Your Market: By shifting focus from only elite athletes to everyday individuals like Walt Stack, Nike expanded its perceived market and solidified its position as a brand for everyone who sought to push their personal limits. This broad appeal was crucial in overtaking competitors who were too narrowly focused.
