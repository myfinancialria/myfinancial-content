Your Chance to Buy Shares from Big Owners: Understanding Offer For Sale (OFS)

TL;DR

  • An Offer For Sale (OFS) is when large shareholders, like promoters or the government, sell their existing shares.
  • It's a quick way for them to reduce their stake and raise money, directly from investors.
  • Retail investors, large institutions, and sometimes employees can bid for these shares.
  • Often, retail investors get a small discount on the share price.
  • OFS helps companies meet rules about how many shares must be publicly available.

What's this about?

When you hear about a company's shares being sold to the public, you might first think of an Initial Public Offering (IPO). But there's another common way for shares to change hands in large numbers: an Offer For Sale, or OFS. Simply put, an OFS is a special window where existing large shareholders – typically the company's promoters (the original owners) or the government in the case of public sector companies – sell a big chunk of their shares to new investors. It's not the company itself issuing brand new shares; instead, it's the current big owners selling what they already hold.

Why it's in the news right now

Offer For Sales are frequently in the news, especially when the government decides to sell part of its stake in a Public Sector Undertaking (PSU) to raise funds or when a private promoter aims to reduce their ownership. For instance, the government recently utilised an OFS mechanism to offload a portion of its shares in a major public sector bank, aiming to streamline its holdings and generate revenue. Such events are closely watched by investors as they present an opportunity to buy shares in established companies.

How it actually works

An OFS is a structured process regulated by SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) to ensure fairness and transparency. Here’s how it generally works:

  1. Who can sell? Only promoters, promoter group entities, or non-promoter shareholders holding at least 10% of the company's share capital can offer shares through an OFS. This means only significant owners can use this route.
  2. Announcement: The company, on behalf of the selling shareholder, informs the stock exchanges (like NSE and BSE) at least two trading days before the OFS opens. This announcement includes details like the number of shares being offered and the date of the sale.
  3. Bidding Day: The OFS usually takes place over one or two trading days. Investors place their bids (how many shares they want and at what price) through their brokers on the stock exchange platform.
  4. Floor Price: The selling shareholder sets a "floor price" – this is the minimum price at which bids can be placed. You cannot bid below this price.
  5. Retail Investor Portion & Discount: A certain percentage of the OFS (usually 10%) is reserved for retail investors (those bidding for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs). Often, retail investors are offered a small discount (e.g., 5%) on the final allotment price, making it an attractive proposition.
  6. Allocation: After the bidding closes, shares are allotted based on the demand and the prices bid. Institutional investors usually bid at or above the floor price, and the final "cut-off" price is determined by the demand. Retail investors often get their shares at the cut-off price minus their discount.
  7. Settlement: Once allotted, the shares are credited to the investor's Demat account, and the money is debited. This process is similar to buying shares in the normal market.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine a popular, well-established kirana (grocery) shop owner who owns ten branches across the city. Over time, they decide they want to reduce their direct involvement and raise some cash. Instead of opening a brand new eleventh branch (which would be like an IPO), they decide to sell two of their existing, fully-running branches to new owners.

These new owners could be smaller, individual shopkeepers (retail investors) or bigger business houses that own many shops (institutional investors). The original owner sets a minimum price for these two branches, and potential buyers bid. The original owner gets the money, and the new owners now run those two branches, which continue to operate as before. The overall business (the company) doesn't get new money; the money just moves from the new buyers to the old owner.

Why it matters to you

  1. Opportunity for Discounted Shares: As a retail investor, you often get the chance to buy shares at a slight discount to the prevailing market price, which can be an immediate benefit.
  2. Access to Established Companies: OFS often involves large, well-known companies, including government-owned entities (PSUs). This gives you an opportunity to invest in such businesses that might not have new IPOs.
  3. Increased Public Float: When promoters sell shares, more shares become available for trading in the open market. This can sometimes improve the "liquidity" of the stock, meaning it's easier to buy and sell without impacting its price too much.
  4. Government Divestment: For PSUs, OFS is a key tool for the government to sell its holdings. The funds raised through these sales can be used for various public welfare schemes or to reduce government debt.
  5. Meeting Regulatory Requirements: Companies need to maintain a minimum public shareholding (currently 25% for most listed companies). OFS is a common way for promoters to reduce their stake and comply with these SEBI rules.

Common confusions cleared up

  • Myth: OFS is the same as an IPO.

    • Fact: An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a company lists its shares for the first time and raises new capital for its business. An OFS is when existing* shares are sold by large shareholders, and the money goes to them, not the company.

  • Myth: The company gets money from an OFS to fund its growth.

    • * Fact: The funds from an OFS go directly to the selling shareholder (the promoter or the government). The company itself does not receive any money from an OFS.

  • Myth: OFS shares are always a guaranteed bargain.

    • * Fact: While retail investors often get a discount, the final price depends on market demand and the floor price set. There's no guarantee the shares will always deliver immediate profits, as market conditions can change.

FAQ

What is a "floor price" in OFS?

The floor price is the minimum price per share at which bids can be placed by investors in an Offer For Sale.

Can I sell my OFS shares immediately after allotment?

Yes, once the shares are allotted to your Demat account, you can sell them in the secondary market just like any other shares you own.

Is there a maximum amount a retail investor can bid in an OFS?

Yes, retail investors are typically defined as those bidding for shares with a total value of up to ₹2 lakhs.

How is the OFS price for retail investors determined?

Retail investors usually receive shares at the "cut-off price" (the price at which most non-retail bids are accepted) with an additional discount applied to it.

Who regulates the Offer For Sale process in India?

The entire Offer For Sale process, from announcement to allotment, is regulated by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) to ensure transparency and protect investor interests.
