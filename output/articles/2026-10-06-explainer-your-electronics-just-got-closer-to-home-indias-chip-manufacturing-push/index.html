Your Electronics Just Got Closer to Home: India's Chip Manufacturing Push

TL;DR

  • India is actively building factories to produce semiconductor chips, the tiny "brains" of all electronics.
  • This effort, backed by significant government incentives, aims to reduce India's reliance on imported chips.
  • Recently, facilities like Tata's Assam plant began pilot production, and Micron's Gujarat unit started commercial output.
  • The goal is to create thousands of new jobs and establish India as a global hub for chip manufacturing.
  • Ultimately, this could lead to more affordable and readily available electronic devices for you.

What's this about?

India is making a big push to start manufacturing semiconductor chips right here within the country. Think of semiconductors as the tiny "brains" or "hearts" of almost every electronic device you use – your smartphone, laptop, car, washing machine, and even your smart TV. Until recently, India has mostly imported these crucial components from other countries. The government, along with private companies, is now working to set up large, advanced factories, called fabs (short for fabrication plants) for making the core chips, and OSAT (Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test) facilities for packaging and testing them, to produce these chips locally.

Why it's in the news right now

This initiative is buzzing with activity. Just this month, on October 3, 2026, Tata Semiconductor Assembly and Test (TSAT) announced that its plant in Jagiroad, Assam, has completed its first clean room and started pilot chip production. This is a significant step for an OSAT facility, which focuses on the crucial back-end stages of chip manufacturing. Earlier in the year, in February 2026, Micron Technology celebrated the grand opening of its semiconductor assembly and test facility in Sanand, Gujarat, which has already begun commercial production of "Made in India" memory chips for global customers. These milestones, along with Prime Minister Narendra Modi's recent statement in September 2026 that India-made chips have started reaching customers globally, highlight the concrete progress in India's ambitious semiconductor journey. The government has also expanded its support with India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) 2.0, broadening incentives for the entire chip ecosystem.

How it actually works

Making a semiconductor chip is incredibly complex, like building a city on a pinhead. Here’s a simplified breakdown:

  1. Design: First, engineers design the intricate circuits that make up the chip. This is like drawing a very detailed blueprint for a tiny electronic brain.
  2. Raw Material: The main raw material is silicon, purified to an extreme level and sliced into thin, round wafers, like very thin rotis.
  3. Fabrication (The "Fab"): These silicon wafers go into a highly specialised factory called a fab. Inside, in ultra-clean rooms, layers of different materials are deposited onto the wafer. Tiny patterns are then "etched" onto these layers using special light (a process called photolithography), creating billions of transistors and connections. This process is repeated many, many times.
  4. Assembly and Test (OSAT): Once the wafer has all the circuits, it's sent to an OSAT facility. Here, it's cut into individual chips, each chip is then tested thoroughly, and encased in a protective package with tiny pins, ready to be put into an electronic device.
  5. Government Support: To encourage companies to build these expensive fabs and OSAT facilities in India, the government offers substantial financial incentives (like subsidies or grants) under the India Semiconductor Mission. This helps companies cover a large part of the huge cost of setting up and running these advanced facilities. The government provides up to 50% fiscal support for eligible projects.

A simple way to think about it

Imagine your kitchen is running low on spices. Usually, you buy them pre-packaged from the market. Now, imagine the government decides it's better for everyone if you start growing your own chillies, turmeric, and cumin in your backyard, and even grinding them into powder.

  • The spices are like the semiconductor chips – essential ingredients for your electronic "dishes."
  • Buying spices from the market is like importing chips from other countries.
  • Setting up your backyard garden and grinder is like building a chip manufacturing fab and OSAT facility in India. It requires a lot of initial investment (good soil, seeds, a grinder), but once it's running, you have your own supply.
  • Government incentives are like your neighbour helping you buy the seeds and a good grinder, or giving you a discount on water for your garden, to encourage you to start.

This way, you become self-sufficient in spices, and eventually, they might even become cheaper for you and your family.

Why it matters to you

  1. Potentially Cheaper Electronics: If chips are made in India, the cost of importing them (and associated duties) could reduce. This might eventually lead to slightly lower prices for your phones, TVs, and other gadgets.
  2. More Jobs: Setting up and running chip fabs and OSAT units requires a massive workforce – from engineers and scientists to technicians and factory workers. This means thousands of new, high-skill jobs across the country. Micron's facility alone is expected to create thousands of direct and indirect jobs.
  3. Reliable Supply: During global chip shortages (like the one during the pandemic), many industries suffered. Making chips locally means India will have a more stable and secure supply, reducing disruptions to manufacturing of cars, electronics, and other goods.
  4. Boost to "Make in India": This initiative strengthens India's manufacturing base and helps the country become less dependent on imports, aligning with the "Make in India" vision and positioning India as a global manufacturing hub.
  5. Technological Advancement: Having chip manufacturing capabilities brings cutting-edge technology and research to India, fostering innovation in related fields and enhancing India's overall technological prowess.

Common confusions cleared up

  • Myth: Making chips is easy; any factory can do it.

    • * Fact: Chip manufacturing is one of the most complex and capital-intensive industries in the world, requiring highly specialised equipment, ultra-clean environments, and advanced scientific expertise. A single fab can cost billions of dollars to build.

  • Myth: India will soon make all the chips for the world.

    • * Fact: While India aims to become a significant player and a trusted alternative in the global supply chain, established chip-making nations have decades of head start. The goal is to build a strong domestic ecosystem and capture a meaningful share, not necessarily to dominate the entire global supply immediately.

  • Myth: This will only benefit big companies.

    • * Fact: While large companies lead the fab and OSAT projects, the ecosystem creates opportunities for many smaller ancillary industries, suppliers, research institutions, and a vast number of skilled professionals across the value chain.

FAQ

What is the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM)?

The India Semiconductor Mission (ISM) is a government initiative launched in December 2021 to build a robust semiconductor and display manufacturing ecosystem in India by providing financial incentives and support to companies.

How much money is the government investing?

Under the Semicon India Programme, the government has approved a significant financial outlay, with incentives covering up to 50% of the project cost for eligible semiconductor and display manufacturing projects. The total approved investment across 12 projects under ISM 1.0 is around ₹1.64 lakh crore (approximately US$18 billion in 2026).

Which companies are involved in India's chip manufacturing?

Several global and Indian companies are involved. Key players include Micron Technology, which has an operational OSAT facility in Gujarat, and Tata Electronics, which has an OSAT facility under pilot production in Assam and is developing a large fab in Gujarat in partnership with PSMC.

How long will it take for India to make its own chips?

Setting up a semiconductor fab is a multi-year process. While OSAT facilities like Micron's and Tata's have begun commercial and pilot production, the larger silicon fabrication plants (fabs) are expected to start production between 2027 and 2029.

Will this make my current phone cheaper right away?

Not immediately. The impact on consumer prices will likely be gradual as domestic production scales up and becomes a significant part of the supply chain. It's a long-term strategy for cost reduction and supply security.
