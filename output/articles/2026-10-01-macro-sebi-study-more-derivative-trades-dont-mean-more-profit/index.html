SEBI Study: More Derivative Trades Don't Mean More Profit

TL;DR

  • SEBI recently published studies for FY25 and FY26, analysing individual traders in the equity derivatives segment.
  • The findings indicated that a significant majority of individual participants in Futures & Options (F&O) incurred financial losses.
  • A key revelation was that increasing trading frequency did not lead to higher profits; instead, it often correlated with larger losses.
  • Transaction costs, encompassing brokerage, taxes, and various fees, played a substantial role in eroding potential gains for active traders.
  • The studies reinforce the high-risk nature of derivative trading for retail investors, stressing the need for caution and a clear strategy.

What this means in plain terms

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) recently released findings from two comprehensive studies, examining the trading patterns and profitability of individual investors in the equity derivatives segment, commonly known as Futures and Options (F&O). These studies, covering the financial years 2024-25 and 2025-26, analysed client-level data from the top 15 stockbrokers across the country.

The core takeaway is a stark reminder for retail participants: frequent trading in the F&O segment does not necessarily lead to profits. In fact, SEBI's data strongly suggests the opposite – that trading more often tends to amplify losses, primarily due to the compounding effect of transaction costs and the inherent volatility of these instruments. This insight is crucial for anyone considering or currently involved in the fast-paced world of derivatives.

The Stark Reality of F&O Trading Losses

SEBI's studies for FY25 and FY26 paint a clear picture of the challenges faced by individual traders in the equity derivatives market. While specific percentages for the latest studies are awaited, previous SEBI analyses have consistently shown that a vast majority, often over 90%, of individual traders in the F&O segment experience net losses. This trend appears to have continued, with the recent data reinforcing that derivative trading is a high-stakes arena where the odds are heavily stacked against the retail participant.

Why do so many lose money?

The reasons are multifaceted. Derivatives, by their nature, are complex instruments designed for hedging and speculation, requiring deep market understanding and risk management. Many retail traders enter the segment with insufficient capital, inadequate knowledge, and without a robust trading plan. The allure of quick profits often overshadows the inherent risks, leading to impulsive decisions and significant capital erosion.

Trading Frequency: A Path to Deeper Losses

One of the most critical findings from SEBI's latest studies is the inverse relationship between trading frequency and profitability. Conventional wisdom might suggest that more activity leads to more opportunities for profit, but the data indicates otherwise. Individual traders who engaged in more frequent transactions within the F&O segment were found to incur higher losses on average.

The compounding effect of activity

Each trade, irrespective of its outcome, incurs costs. For highly active traders, these costs accumulate rapidly, eating into small profits or exacerbating losses. The psychological aspect also plays a role; frequent trading can lead to overconfidence, emotional decision-making, and deviation from a disciplined strategy, all of which contribute to negative outcomes. The studies highlight that simply being 'active' in the market does not translate to being 'profitable'.

The Hidden Drag of Transaction Costs

While market movements and trading strategies are often the focus, transaction costs represent a significant, yet often underestimated, drain on a trader's capital. For frequent F&O traders, these costs can quickly turn a break-even scenario into a loss, or significantly reduce otherwise profitable trades. SEBI's analysis underscored the substantial impact of these charges on overall profitability.

Breaking down the costs

  • Brokerage: Even with discount brokers, per-trade charges or percentage-based fees add up quickly. For high-volume traders, these can amount to lakhs of rupees annually.
  • Securities Transaction Tax (STT): A direct tax levied on the value of securities transacted on a stock exchange. For options, STT is charged on the premium at the time of sale, and on the strike price for options exercised. For futures, it's charged on the sell side.
  • Stamp Duty: A state-level tax levied on the value of transactions.
  • Exchange Transaction Charges: Fees charged by stock exchanges (NSE, BSE) for using their platforms.
  • SEBI Turnover Fees: A small fee levied by SEBI on the turnover.
  • GST: Goods and Services Tax is applicable on brokerage, exchange charges, and SEBI fees.

Cumulatively, these charges can be substantial. A trader making hundreds of trades a month might find that even if their gross profits are decent, their net profits after all these deductions are negligible or negative.

A real example

Consider Priya, 32, a software engineer in Pune, earning ₹22L CTC. Inspired by online forums, she decides to try F&O trading in FY26, believing that frequent trading will give her more chances to profit.

  • Initial Capital: ₹1,00,000
  • Trading Frequency: She executes an average of 15 trades (buy/sell legs) per day, 20 trading days a month.
  • Monthly Trades: 15 trades/day * 20 days = 300 trades.
  • Average Contract Value (per trade): ₹2,00,000 (e.g., buying/selling 2 lots of Nifty options at ₹100 premium, Nifty at 20,000, lot size 50).

Let's estimate her monthly costs:

  1. Brokerage: Assuming ₹20 per executed order (buy/sell).

    2. Monthly Brokerage: 300 trades ₹20 = ₹6,000

  2. STT (Securities Transaction Tax): For Options Sell, it's 0.05% on the premium value. For Futures Sell, it's 0.0125% on the notional value. Let's assume a mix, averaging to 0.02% of contract value per trade.

    3. Monthly STT: 300 trades ₹2,00,000 (avg contract value) * 0.02% = ₹12,000

  3. Exchange Transaction Charges: Typically around 0.002% on notional value for options, 0.0019% for futures. Averaging 0.002%.

    4. Monthly Exchange Charges: 300 trades ₹2,00,000 * 0.002% = ₹1,200

  4. SEBI Turnover Fees: Very small, let's estimate ₹100 per month.
  5. Stamp Duty: Varies by state, but for derivatives, it's often a small percentage of turnover. Let's estimate ₹500 per month.
  6. GST (18% on Brokerage + Exchange Charges + SEBI Fees):

    7. (₹6,000 + ₹1,200 + ₹100) 18% = ₹7,300 * 0.18 = ₹1,314

Total Estimated Monthly Costs: ₹6,000 (Brokerage) + ₹12,000 (STT) + ₹1,200 (Exchange) + ₹100 (SEBI) + ₹500 (Stamp) + ₹1,314 (GST) = ₹21,114

Even if Priya manages to make a gross profit of, say, ₹15,000 from her trading activity in a good month, her net result is a loss of ₹6,114 (₹15,000 - ₹21,114). In a typical month where she might break even or make a small gross loss, these costs quickly push her deep into negative territory. This example clearly illustrates how frequent trading, even with some successful trades, can lead to significant net losses due to accumulated transaction costs, as highlighted by SEBI's findings.

What to do this week

  1. Review your F&O trading statement: If you actively trade derivatives, download your broker's statement for the last quarter. Calculate your total brokerage, STT, and other charges paid. See how much these costs are eating into your gross profits or adding to your losses.
  2. Assess your trading frequency: Count the number of F&O trades you initiated (buy/sell legs) in a typical week or month. Compare this with your net profitability. Does more trading genuinely lead to better outcomes for you?
  3. Re-evaluate your strategy: If you're frequently trading and consistently losing money, or if your profits are negligible after costs, consider if your current F&O strategy is sustainable. Research alternative, less frequent strategies or consider reducing your exposure.
  4. Educate yourself on derivatives: Before making any further F&O trades, spend 30 minutes reading up on the basics of derivatives, their risks, and appropriate risk management techniques from reliable sources like the SEBI website.
  5. Consider alternative investments: If F&O trading isn't yielding results, explore other investment avenues like equity mutual funds, direct equity investing for the long term, or debt instruments that align better with your financial goals and risk appetite.

FAQ

Is SEBI against F&O trading for retail investors?

No, SEBI's role is to regulate the market and protect investor interests. These studies aim to provide data-driven insights into the actual outcomes of F&O trading for retail investors, highlighting the risks involved.

What are the key takeaways from the SEBI studies?

The primary takeaways are that a large majority of individual F&O traders incur losses, and increased trading frequency often leads to higher losses due, in part, to accumulating transaction costs.

How can I reduce my losses in F&O trading?

Consider reducing your trading frequency, focusing on a well-researched strategy, implementing strict risk management (like stop-losses), and ensuring you have adequate capital and knowledge before trading.

Are transaction costs the only reason for F&O losses?

No, transaction costs are a significant factor, especially for frequent traders, but other reasons include lack of market knowledge, poor risk management, emotional trading, and insufficient capital.

Where can I find the full SEBI studies?

SEBI publishes its reports and studies on its official website (sebi.gov.in). Investors can look for the "Reports & Publications" section for detailed documents.

Should I stop trading F&O entirely?

The studies do not suggest stopping F&O entirely. Instead, they serve as a caution for retail investors to approach derivatives trading with thorough knowledge, a disciplined strategy, and a clear understanding of the high risks and costs involved.
