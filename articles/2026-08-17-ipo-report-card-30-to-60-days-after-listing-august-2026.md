## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public through an IPO (Initial Public Offering), there's often a lot of excitement. The "listing day" (the first day shares are traded on the stock exchange) can see prices jump significantly, creating a "listing gain" (the percentage increase from the IPO price to the closing price on the first day). This initial surge is often driven by high demand and market buzz.

However, this first-day excitement can sometimes be misleading. Many factors, like the initial allocation to large investors (called "anchor investors") who have a "lock-in period" (a time when they cannot sell their shares), can influence the listing price. After about a month or two, this initial frenzy tends to settle down, and the lock-in periods for some investors might end. Looking at the share price 30 to 60 days after listing gives us a calmer, more realistic picture of how the market is valuing the company's shares once the initial hype has faded. It helps us see if the listing-day gains have held, grown, or reversed.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

Think of a popular new snack stall opening at a school fete. If many students want to buy the snack (high "demand") but there are only a limited number of servings (low "supply"), the stall might sell out quickly, and some eager students might even offer a little extra to get their hands on it.

Similarly, with an IPO, if a company is seen as very promising and many investors want to buy its shares, the "subscription levels" (how many times the IPO shares were oversubscribed by investors) will be high. This creates strong demand. If the company's "pricing" (the IPO price per share) was set at a level that the market feels is reasonable or even a bit low, and the overall "market mood" (how optimistic investors are about the stock market in general) is positive, the share price can "pop" or increase significantly on listing day.

However, if there isn't much interest in the company, or if the IPO price is considered too high, or if the market mood is negative, there might be less demand than supply. In such cases, the share price might "drop" below its "issue price" (the price at which the company offered its shares in the IPO) on listing day. "Anchor investors" are large institutional investors who subscribe to a portion of the IPO before it opens to the public, usually with a "lock-in period" (a restriction on selling their shares for a certain number of days, often 30 days). Their participation can signal confidence, but once their lock-in ends, their selling activity can also influence the price.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot of the market's initial reaction. It's often influenced by short-term trading and speculative interest. The stock market is dynamic, and prices are subject to "volatility" (quick and often unpredictable changes).

A stock might list with a huge gain, but if the company's actual performance doesn't meet expectations, or if the "broader market" (the overall stock market) turns negative, that gain can quickly disappear or even turn into a loss. Conversely, a stock might list flat or even below its issue price, but if the company performs well over time, or if market conditions improve, its price can rise significantly later. The first-day price is like the opening score in a cricket match – it sets the stage, but the final result can be very different.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a new restaurant opening in your neighbourhood. On its first day, there's a lot of buzz, maybe a special offer, and everyone rushes to try it. The queues are long, and it seems incredibly popular. But after a month or two, the initial excitement settles. The restaurant's true popularity will then depend on the quality of its food, service, and how it compares to other options. The price of its dishes will reflect this long-term value, not just the opening day hype. Similarly, an IPO's listing day is like the restaurant's grand opening, while the price 30-60 days later is closer to its regular business.

## Things people get confused about

### Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.
**Fact:** A big listing gain simply means there was high demand for the shares on the first day of trading. It doesn't guarantee the company's long-term success or that its share price will continue to rise. Company performance, industry trends, and market conditions all play a role in the long run.

### Myth → Fact: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment forever.
**Fact:** Listing below the issue price can be disappointing, but it's not a permanent verdict. Market sentiment or specific factors on listing day might have caused the drop. A company's fundamentals (its business strength, profits, growth prospects) can improve over time, leading to a recovery in its share price.

### Myth → Fact: All IPOs are the same.
**Fact:** There are primarily two types: Mainboard IPOs (for larger, more established companies listed on NSE/BSE) and SME IPOs (for Small and Medium Enterprises listed on dedicated SME platforms). SME IPOs often have different trading rules, lower liquidity (fewer buyers and sellers), and higher investment lot sizes compared to Mainboard IPOs.

## FAQ

### What is an IPO?
An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, becoming a publicly traded company.

### What is a Mainboard IPO?
A Mainboard IPO is for larger companies that list their shares on the main exchanges like the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).

### What is an SME IPO?
An SME IPO is for Small and Medium Enterprises that list their shares on separate platforms like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, designed for smaller companies.

### What does "issue price" mean?
The "issue price" is the fixed price per share at which a company offers its shares to investors during the IPO.

### What is "listing day"?
"Listing day" is the first day that a company's shares start trading on the stock exchange after its IPO.
