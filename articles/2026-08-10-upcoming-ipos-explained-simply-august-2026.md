## First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local shop that has been doing very well. Now, the owners want to grow bigger – maybe open more branches, buy new equipment, or expand their product range. To do this, they need a lot of money. Instead of borrowing from a bank, they decide to sell a small part of their ownership to the public.

This is what an **IPO (Initial Public Offering)** is. It's when a private company offers its shares for the first time to the public. When you buy these shares, you become a part-owner of the company. This initial sale happens in the **primary market** (think of it as the company's first-ever sale counter). After the IPO, these shares start trading on a stock exchange like NSE or BSE; this is called **listing**.

## How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, you'll see a few key terms. Understanding them helps you make sense of the offer:

1.  **Price Band:** This is the fixed range within which you can bid for the shares. For example, if the price band is ₹151 to ₹159, you can choose any price within this range. The company usually sets a "cut-off price" at the higher end of the band after all bids are in.
2.  **Lot Size:** You cannot buy just one share in an IPO. Instead, you must bid for a minimum number of shares, called a **lot**. If the lot size is 800 shares, you must apply for 800 shares or multiples of 800 (e.g., 1600, 2400).
3.  **Minimum Investment:** This is the smallest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO. You calculate it by multiplying the lot size by the upper end of the price band. For Fascinate Textiles, with a lot size of 800 and an upper price band of ₹156, the minimum investment would be 800 shares * ₹156/share = ₹1,24,800.
4.  **Issue Size:** This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public, or the total value of these shares. A larger issue size means more shares are available, potentially leading to a higher chance of allotment if demand is high. We often quote this in crores for easier understanding of the total money being raised.
5.  **Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS):**
    *   **Fresh Issue:** When a company issues new shares to raise money for its business (like for expansion or debt repayment). The money goes directly to the company.
    *   **Offer-for-Sale (OFS):** When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares. The money from these shares goes to the selling shareholders, not the company itself.
    *   *Note: The data provided for this week's IPOs only specifies the total "issue_size" in terms of shares, not the split between fresh issue and OFS. This information is usually found in the company's official documents.*
6.  **RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus):** This is the detailed legal document filed with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India). It contains all crucial information about the company, its financials, risks, management, and how the money from the IPO will be used. It's like the company's detailed report card and business plan for potential investors.

## The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO involves a clear sequence of steps:

1.  **DRHP Filing:** The company first files a **DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus)** with SEBI. This document contains all the details about the company and the proposed IPO.
2.  **SEBI Approval:** SEBI reviews the DRHP to ensure all disclosures are complete and fair. Once approved, the company files the final **RHP (Red Herring Prospectus)**.
3.  **Price Band & Lot Size Announcement:** The company announces the price band (e.g., ₹151-₹159) and the lot size (minimum number of shares to bid for).
4.  **Bidding Period:** The IPO opens for a few days (usually 3-5 days) during which investors can place their bids. Bids are typically divided into categories:
    *   **Retail Individual Investors (RIIs):** Individuals applying for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs) / High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs):** Individuals or entities applying for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs):** Large institutional investors like mutual funds, foreign institutional investors, and insurance companies.
5.  **Allotment:** After the bidding closes, the company and its registrars process the applications. If the IPO is oversubscribed (more demand than shares available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for RIIs.
6.  **Refund/Debit:** If you are allotted shares, the money for those shares is debited from your bank account. If you are not allotted shares, the blocked amount is released back to your account.
7.  **Listing:** The shares are then listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and/or BSE) on a specific date, usually a few days after allotment. This is when the shares start trading publicly.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a school fete where different stalls are selling handmade crafts to raise money for new school equipment. One popular stall, "The Crafty Corner," decides to sell small 'ownership slips' in its stall. Each slip represents a tiny part of the stall. When you buy these slips, you're giving money to "The Crafty Corner" so they can buy more materials and make more crafts. You hope that if "The Crafty Corner" becomes even more popular, your slip will be worth more later. An IPO is just like "The Crafty Corner" selling ownership slips (shares) to the public for the first time to raise money for its growth.

## Things people get confused about

Here are some common misunderstandings about IPOs:

*   **Myth:** Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares.
    *   **Fact:** If an IPO receives more applications than available shares (it's "oversubscribed"), shares are allotted through a lottery system, especially for retail investors. You might not get any shares.
*   **Myth:** All IPOs lead to quick profits on listing day.
    *   **Fact:** While some IPOs do see a rise in share price on listing day, this is not guaranteed. Market conditions, company performance, and investor sentiment can cause prices to go up or down. Investing in IPOs, like any market investment, carries risks.
*   **Myth:** You must apply for every IPO that comes out.
    *   **Fact:** There's no compulsion to apply for every IPO. It's important to understand the company, its business, and the offer details before deciding if it aligns with your financial goals.

## FAQ

### How do I even apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your bank's net banking portal using the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility, or through your stockbroker's platform. Your money remains blocked in your account until allotment.

### What is a lot size?
The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot apply for fewer than this number, and you must apply in multiples of the lot size.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME (Small and Medium Enterprise) IPOs are for smaller companies that list on dedicated SME platforms (like NSE Emerge or BSE SME) with different, often less stringent, regulations.

### Can I apply for more than one lot?
Yes, you can apply for multiple lots. However, for retail investors, the total application value cannot exceed ₹2 lakhs. If you apply for more than ₹2 lakhs, you fall into the HNI/NII category, which has different allotment rules.

### What happens if I don't get an allotment?
If you are not allotted any shares, the money that was blocked in your bank account for the IPO application will be unblocked and available for use, usually within a few days after the allotment date.
