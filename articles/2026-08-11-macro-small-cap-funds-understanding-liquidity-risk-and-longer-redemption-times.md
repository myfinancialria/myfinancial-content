# Small-Cap Funds: Understanding Liquidity Risk and Longer Redemption Times

**TL;DR**
- SEBI data indicates that top small-cap funds can take significantly longer to liquidate half their portfolios compared to mid-cap funds.
- Small-cap funds required an average of 51 days to sell half their assets, while mid-cap funds needed only 23 days as per FY26 data.
- This extended liquidation period highlights potential redemption risks for investors, especially during market downturns or high outflow periods.
- The rapidly growing Assets Under Management (AUM) in small-cap schemes further exacerbates these liquidity concerns.
- Investors should carefully monitor fund house stress test reports and consider their investment horizon and risk tolerance for small-cap exposure.

## What this means in plain terms

Imagine you own a piece of property. If it's a popular flat in a metro city, you can likely sell it quickly. But if it's a niche property in a remote area, it might take much longer to find a buyer and get your money. This is similar to how "liquidity" works in mutual funds. Small-cap funds invest in shares of smaller companies. Often, fewer buyers and sellers exist for these shares compared to large, well-known companies. This means if a fund manager needs to sell a large chunk of these shares to meet investor redemption requests, it can take more time and potentially impact the price they get.

Recent data from SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) shows that the top five small-cap funds could take an average of 51 days to sell off half of their portfolio holdings. In stark contrast, the top mid-cap funds needed only 23 days for the same exercise. This significant difference highlights the inherent liquidity challenges in the small-cap segment, which can become a critical factor when many investors want to exit their funds simultaneously.

## Understanding Liquidity in Equity Funds

Liquidity, in the context of mutual funds, refers to how easily and quickly a fund manager can sell the underlying assets (shares) in the portfolio without significantly affecting their market price. For large-cap companies, shares are traded in high volumes daily, making them very liquid. A fund manager can sell a substantial number of shares without causing a major price drop.

Small-cap companies, by their very nature, have a smaller market capitalisation and often lower trading volumes. This means that if a fund needs to sell a large block of shares from a small-cap company, finding enough buyers at a fair price can be challenging. This can lead to a situation where the fund manager either has to sell at a lower-than-desired price to offload shares quickly or take a longer time to execute the sale, potentially staggering it over several days or weeks. This inherent characteristic makes small-cap funds less liquid than their large-cap or even mid-cap counterparts.

## SEBI's Stress Test Data: A Closer Look

SEBI, as the market regulator, mandates mutual fund houses to conduct stress tests on their small-cap and mid-cap schemes. These tests are designed to assess how long it would take for a fund to liquidate a certain percentage of its portfolio under various market conditions, specifically to meet redemption pressures. The recent findings, based on SEBI’s FY26 data as reported by Livemint, are particularly insightful.

The data indicates that the top five small-cap funds would require an average of 51 days to sell half of their portfolio holdings. This is more than double the time taken by the top five mid-cap funds, which could liquidate 50% of their assets in approximately 23 days. This stark difference underscores the varying degrees of liquidity across market capitalisation segments. The increasing Assets Under Management (AUM) in small-cap funds, which have seen significant inflows recently, further amplifies the challenge. As funds grow larger, the absolute value of assets they might need to liquidate also increases, potentially prolonging the time required to sell without causing significant market impact.

## Implications of Redemption Pressure

The extended liquidation period for small-cap funds has direct implications for investors, particularly during periods of market stress or widespread redemptions. If a large number of investors decide to withdraw their money from a small-cap fund simultaneously, the fund manager faces the challenge of selling illiquid assets quickly.

In such a scenario, the fund might be forced to sell shares at a discount to attract buyers, which can negatively impact the fund's Net Asset Value (NAV). This means that investors redeeming their units might receive a lower value than anticipated. Furthermore, if the liquidation process takes too long, fund managers might even have to gate redemptions (i.e., temporarily stop or limit withdrawals) or stagger payouts over several days, weeks, or even months, as permitted by SEBI regulations under extreme circumstances. While such measures are rare, the stress test results highlight the potential for delays in accessing funds, which can be critical for investors relying on their investments for specific financial goals.

## Mitigating Small-Cap Fund Risks

Fund houses employ several strategies to manage liquidity risks in small-cap funds. These include maintaining a certain percentage of the portfolio in cash or highly liquid instruments, staggering the sale of illiquid assets over time, and diversifying across a wider range of small-cap stocks to avoid over-concentration in any single illiquid scrip. SEBI's stress test guidelines also encourage fund houses to be transparent about these risks.

For investors, understanding these risks is crucial. Diversification across market caps (large, mid, and small) is a fundamental principle. Investors should not allocate an excessively large portion of their portfolio to small-cap funds, especially if they have a shorter investment horizon or anticipate needing the funds in the near future. A long-term investment horizon (typically 7-10 years or more) is generally recommended for small-cap funds, allowing fund managers ample time to navigate market cycles and manage liquidity without distress. Reviewing the stress test reports published by Asset Management Companies (AMCs) on the AMFI India website can provide valuable insights into a fund's liquidity profile.

## A real example

Consider **Rohan, 38, earning ₹30 lakhs CTC, based in Gurugram**. Rohan had invested ₹20 lakhs in a small-cap fund over the last five years, aiming for aggressive growth for his child's higher education, which is still 8 years away. However, due to an unexpected family medical emergency, he suddenly needs ₹10 lakhs urgently.

He places a redemption request for ₹10 lakhs from his small-cap fund. Let's assume the fund's stress test report indicates it would take 51 days to liquidate 50% of its portfolio. While Rohan is not redeeming 50% of the *fund's* portfolio, the overall liquidity environment affects how quickly his redemption can be processed, especially if many other investors are also redeeming.

1.  **Day 1:** Rohan places his redemption request.
2.  **Day 2-5:** The fund typically processes redemptions within T+3 business days for equity funds. However, due to the inherent illiquidity of small-cap stocks and potential market conditions, the actual settlement might take longer if the fund manager struggles to sell underlying assets without impacting NAV.
3.  **Potential Delay:** If the market is experiencing a downturn and many investors are pulling out, the fund manager might face pressure to sell stocks quickly, potentially at lower prices. In a severe scenario, where the fund experiences high redemptions, Rohan might not receive his entire ₹10 lakhs within the standard T+3 days. Instead, he might receive a portion initially, with the remainder being paid out over a longer period, potentially stretching towards the 51-day mark if the fund has to stagger sales.
4.  **Impact:** Rohan's urgent need for ₹10 lakhs could be delayed, causing significant stress and potentially forcing him to arrange funds from other, more liquid sources, or even take a short-term loan. This scenario highlights the importance of matching investment horizons with the liquidity profile of the chosen fund.

## FAQ

### What causes small-cap funds to be less liquid?
Small-cap companies generally have lower trading volumes and fewer shares available in the market compared to large-cap companies. This makes it harder for fund managers to buy or sell large blocks of shares quickly without significantly impacting their prices.

### What are SEBI's stress tests for mutual funds?
SEBI mandates mutual fund houses to conduct stress tests to assess how long it would take to liquidate a certain percentage (e.g., 25% or 50%) of their portfolio under various market conditions to meet redemption requests. These reports are published monthly.

### How can I check the liquidity of my small-cap fund?
You can check the monthly stress test reports published by Asset Management Companies (AMCs) on the AMFI India website. These reports provide data on the number of days required to liquidate portions of the fund's portfolio.

### Does lower liquidity always mean lower returns?
Not necessarily. Small-cap funds are known for their high growth potential and can deliver superior returns over the long term. However, lower liquidity introduces higher risk, particularly in terms of accessing your money quickly during market downturns.

### Should I avoid small-cap funds due to liquidity concerns?
Small-cap funds can be a valuable part of a diversified portfolio for long-term growth. The key is to understand the associated liquidity risks, allocate an appropriate portion of your portfolio to them, and have a sufficiently long investment horizon (7-10+ years).

### What is AUM, and why does its growth impact liquidity?
AUM stands for Assets Under Management, which is the total market value of all investments managed by a mutual fund. As AUM grows in small-cap funds, the absolute value of illiquid small-cap stocks held by the fund also increases, potentially making it harder to sell a large portion quickly without affecting market prices.
