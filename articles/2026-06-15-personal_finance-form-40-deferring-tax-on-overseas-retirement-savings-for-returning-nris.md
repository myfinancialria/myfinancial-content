# Form 40: Deferring Tax on Overseas Retirement Savings for Returning NRIs

**TL;DR**
-   Indian tax residents who previously lived abroad can use Form 40 to defer tax on their foreign retirement accounts.
-   This form allows tax to be paid only when funds are withdrawn from the overseas account, rather than annually on accruals.
-   It primarily benefits individuals with retirement savings in countries like the US, UK, Canada, and Australia.
-   Filing Form 40 prevents double taxation or taxation on unrealised gains in these foreign retirement schemes.
-   Consulting a financial advisor specialising in international taxation is recommended for accurate compliance.

## What this means in plain terms

When Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) return to India and become tax residents, their global income typically becomes taxable in India. This can pose a challenge for retirement savings held in foreign countries, such as a 401(k) in the US or a SIPP in the UK. Without a specific provision, the annual growth or accruals within these overseas retirement accounts could become taxable in India each year, even if the money hasn't been withdrawn. This is known as accrual taxation.

Form 40, introduced by the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT), offers a significant relief for returning NRIs. By filing this form, you can effectively defer the tax liability on your foreign retirement savings until you actually start withdrawing money from those accounts. This aligns the tax treatment of these foreign funds with how most domestic retirement products like the Employees' Provident Fund (EPF) or Public Provident Fund (PPF) are taxed in India – tax-exempt on accrual, taxable on withdrawal (subject to conditions). It simplifies compliance and prevents you from paying tax on money you haven't yet received.

## Understanding Tax Residency for Returning Indians

Becoming a tax resident in India is a crucial trigger for your global income being taxed here. For individuals, tax residency is determined by your physical presence in India during a financial year (April 1st to March 31st). Broadly, you become a tax resident if you are in India for:
*   182 days or more in the financial year, OR
*   60 days or more in the financial year AND 365 days or more in the four preceding financial years.

However, there are specific nuances for returning Indian citizens or Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs). If you are an Indian citizen or PIO leaving India for employment abroad, or returning to India after being employed abroad, the 60-day limit for the second condition is extended to 182 days. This means for such individuals, you generally become a tax resident only if you are in India for 182 days or more in the financial year.

Once you qualify as a tax resident, your global income, including income from foreign assets, generally becomes taxable in India. This is where Form 40 becomes particularly relevant for overseas retirement savings, as it provides a mechanism to manage the tax implications of these foreign assets. Understanding your tax residency status is the first step in determining if Form 40 is applicable to your situation.

## How Form 40 Works for Deferring Tax on Overseas Retirement Funds

Form 40 allows a returning Indian resident to notify the Income Tax Department that they hold a foreign retirement fund. Upon successful filing, the income accruing in such a fund, and not actually withdrawn, will not be taxed in India annually. Instead, the tax liability is deferred until the time of withdrawal. This applies to both contributions made by the employer and the individual, as well as the investment gains within the fund.

The key benefit is the shift from an 'accrual basis' to a 'receipt basis' of taxation for these specific foreign retirement funds. Without Form 40, under the general principles of Indian tax law, the annual increase in the value of your foreign retirement fund could theoretically be treated as taxable income in India, even if you haven't touched the money. This could lead to a significant tax burden on notional gains.

By filing Form 40, you essentially declare your intention to avail of this deferred taxation benefit. When you eventually make withdrawals from your foreign retirement fund, those withdrawals will then be subject to tax in India as per the applicable income tax slab rates in that financial year. It's crucial to understand that Form 40 defers tax, it does not exempt it. The income will eventually be taxed, just at a later, more convenient stage for the individual. This provision is particularly helpful in avoiding premature tax payments and managing cash flow upon return to India.

## Eligible Countries and Retirement Accounts

The benefit of deferred taxation under Form 40 is specifically available for retirement funds situated in certain countries with which India has a Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement (DTAA) that includes provisions for such treatment. Currently, the most commonly cited countries for which this relief is applicable are:
*   **United States:** Retirement accounts like 401(k), Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs), and Roth IRAs.
*   **United Kingdom:** Pensions such as Self-Invested Personal Pensions (SIPPs), Personal Pensions, and Occupational Pensions.
*   **Canada:** Registered Retirement Savings Plans (RRSPs) and Registered Retirement Income Funds (RRIFs).
*   **Australia:** Superannuation funds.

The CBDT notification specifies that the foreign retirement fund must be established in a country or specified territory outside India, where the contributions to and earnings from the fund are not taxable until withdrawal or distribution. It's essential that the specific retirement fund you hold in these countries aligns with the definition provided in the relevant DTAA and the CBDT notification. Before filing, it's advisable to verify if your particular fund qualifies, as the rules can be quite specific to the nature of the retirement scheme in each country. This ensures that the relief is correctly applied and avoids future compliance issues.

## Filing Requirements and Compliance

Form 40 must be filed electronically with the Income Tax Department. It is generally required to be filed by the due date for filing your Income Tax Return (ITR) for the financial year in which you become a tax resident in India. For instance, if you become a tax resident in FY 2025-26, you would need to file Form 40 by July 31, 2026 (for non-audit cases).

The form requires details of the foreign retirement fund, including its name, the country where it's located, your account number, and the nature of the fund. You also need to declare that you meet the conditions for availing the deferred taxation benefit, which includes confirming that contributions and earnings from the fund are not taxable in the foreign country until withdrawal.

**Key compliance points:**
*   **Annual Filing:** While the initial filing establishes the deferral, it's generally good practice to continue reporting the existence of such funds in your annual ITR through relevant schedules (e.g., Schedule FA for Foreign Assets), even if no tax is payable on accruals.
*   **Withdrawal Reporting:** When you eventually make withdrawals from the foreign retirement fund, you must report this income in your Indian ITR for the financial year of withdrawal. This income will be taxed at your applicable slab rates.
*   **Documentation:** Maintain all relevant documents related to your foreign retirement fund, including statements, contribution proofs, and withdrawal statements, as these may be required during assessment.
*   **DTAA Impact:** The specific DTAA between India and the country where your retirement fund is located plays a crucial role. Form 40 essentially operationalises the benefits often outlined in these DTAAs. It's important to understand the specific article in the DTAA that deals with pensions or retirement funds.

Failing to file Form 40, if applicable, could lead to the annual accruals in your foreign retirement fund being taxed in India, potentially resulting in higher tax liabilities and penalties. Therefore, timely and accurate filing is paramount.

## A real example

Meet Suresh, 45, who worked in the UK for 15 years and contributed regularly to a Self-Invested Personal Pension (SIPP). In March 2025, he moved back to Bengaluru permanently with his family and started a new job with a ₹28 lakh CTC. For the financial year 2025-26, he will be an Indian tax resident.

Suresh's SIPP in the UK has a current value of £300,000 (approximately ₹3 crore, assuming £1 = ₹100). In FY 2025-26, his SIPP saw an investment gain of £15,000 (₹15 lakhs). Suresh does not plan to withdraw any money from his SIPP for at least another 10-15 years.

**Scenario without Form 40:**
If Suresh did not file Form 40, the ₹15 lakhs gain in his SIPP in FY 2025-26 could be considered taxable income in India in that year. Assuming he falls into the 30% tax bracket, he would potentially owe ₹4.5 lakhs (30% of ₹15 lakhs) in tax, even though he hasn't actually received this money. This would be a significant cash flow burden.

**Scenario with Form 40:**
By filing Form 40 by July 31, 2026, for FY 2025-26, Suresh notifies the Income Tax Department about his SIPP. The ₹15 lakhs gain in his SIPP for FY 2025-26, and any subsequent annual gains, will *not* be taxed in India until he makes a withdrawal.

For example, if Suresh starts withdrawing from his SIPP in FY 2040-41, say ₹20 lakhs in that year, only then will that ₹20 lakhs be added to his taxable income in India for FY 2040-41 and taxed at his applicable slab rates at that time. This defers his tax liability, allowing his retirement savings to grow without annual tax erosion in India.

## FAQ

### What is Form 40?
Form 40 is a declaration form filed with the Indian Income Tax Department by returning NRIs to defer tax on income accruing in specified foreign retirement funds until withdrawal.

### Which countries' retirement funds are covered by Form 40?
Form 40 primarily covers retirement funds in countries like the US (e.g., 401k, IRA), UK (e.g., SIPP), Canada (e.g., RRSP), and Australia (e.g., Superannuation), subject to DTAA provisions.

### Does Form 40 exempt me from paying tax on my foreign retirement savings?
No, Form 40 does not provide an exemption. It only defers the tax liability, meaning you pay tax on the withdrawals when you receive them, rather than on the annual accruals.

### When do I need to file Form 40?
You generally need to file Form 40 by the due date for filing your Income Tax Return for the financial year in which you become a tax resident in India.

### What happens if I don't file Form 40?
If you don't file Form 40 and are a tax resident, the annual accruals in your foreign retirement fund could potentially be taxed in India, leading to a higher tax burden.

### Can I file Form 40 for any foreign investment account?
No, Form 40 is specifically for certain *retirement funds* that meet the criteria laid out in CBDT notifications and relevant DTAAs, not for general investment accounts.

*   **Electronic Filing:** Form 40 must be furnished electronically either under a digital signature or through an electronic verification code. There is no offline route to file Form 40.
*   **Mandatory PAN:** Form 40 cannot be submitted without a valid Permanent Account Number (PAN).
