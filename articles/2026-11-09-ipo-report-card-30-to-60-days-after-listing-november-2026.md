## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public through an Initial Public Offering (IPO), there's often a lot of excitement. The first day of trading, called the "listing day," can see prices jump significantly, leading to what's known as a "listing gain" (the percentage increase from the IPO price to the closing price on the first trading day).

However, this initial buzz can sometimes be misleading. Many factors, like high demand or speculative trading, can push prices up on day one. But what happens after the initial excitement fades?

Looking at an IPO's performance 30 to 60 days after listing gives us a calmer, more realistic picture. By this time, the initial frenzy has usually settled down. Also, for IPOs that opened on or after April 1, 2022, the lock-in period for anchor investors (large institutional investors who commit to buying shares before the IPO opens) is split: 50% of their allocation is locked for 30 days, and the remaining 50% is locked for 90 days from the date of allotment. After these periods, these investors are free to sell their shares, which can sometimes impact the price. So, checking a month or two later helps us see where the market truly values the company's shares once the dust has settled.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

When a company goes public, its shares start trading on the stock exchange. The first price movement, whether a "pop" (price increase) or a "drop" (price decrease), is a result of basic economics: demand versus supply.

*   **Demand vs. Supply:** If many investors want to buy the shares and there aren't enough sellers, the price tends to go up. If there are more sellers than buyers, the price tends to fall.
*   **Subscription Levels:** This refers to how many times an IPO is "oversubscribed." If an IPO receives bids for, say, 10 times more shares than it's offering, it's considered 10 times oversubscribed. High subscription levels often indicate strong demand, which can lead to a listing pop.
*   **Market Mood:** The overall sentiment in the stock market plays a big role. If the broader market is doing well and investors are confident, IPOs tend to perform better. A gloomy market can lead to a weaker listing.
*   **Pricing:** The company and its investment bankers decide the "issue price" (the price at which shares are offered in the IPO). If the shares are priced attractively (meaning a bit lower than what the market might be willing to pay), it can create buzz and lead to a listing gain. If they are priced too high, it might deter buyers.
*   **Lock-in and Anchor Investors:** Before an IPO opens to the public, some large institutional investors, called "anchor investors," commit to buying a certain portion of shares. These investors usually have a "lock-in" period (often 30 days) during which they cannot sell their shares. This helps stabilise the price initially. Once the lock-in period ends, these investors are free to sell, which can sometimes add to the supply of shares in the market.

A listing pop means that the market, on the first day, valued the shares higher than the IPO price. A listing drop means the market valued them lower.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just one moment in a company's journey on the stock exchange. It's like the opening day of a new shop – there might be a big crowd and excitement, but that doesn't guarantee long-term success.

Stock prices are subject to "volatility" (quick and sometimes large price changes), especially in the early days. The broader market conditions, news about the company or its industry, and even global economic events can all influence the price. A stock might list flat (meaning its price doesn't change much on the first day) and then see significant movement later, or it might have a big listing gain only to see its price drop in the following weeks or months. The first-day price reflects immediate demand, but the price after 30-60 days often gives a better sense of how the market has settled on the company's valuation.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a new restaurant opening in your neighbourhood. On day one, there's a huge crowd, long queues, and everyone is talking about it. This is like the IPO listing day – lots of excitement and high demand. But after a month or two, the initial hype settles. The restaurant's true popularity and long-term success depend on the quality of its food, service, and how many customers keep coming back regularly, not just the opening day rush. Similarly, an IPO's true market value often becomes clearer after the initial excitement fades and regular trading begins.

## Things people get confused about

### Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.
**Fact:** A strong listing gain primarily indicates high initial demand for the shares, often driven by market sentiment or attractive pricing. It does not guarantee the company's long-term performance or financial health.

### Myth → Fact: The IPO price is the "right" price for the share.
**Fact:** The IPO price is the price at which the company offers its shares to the public. Once listed, the market (buyers and sellers) determines the share's price based on various factors, which can be higher or lower than the IPO price.

### Myth → Fact: All IPOs are guaranteed to make money.
**Fact:** Investing in IPOs, like any stock market investment, carries risks. There is no guarantee of returns, and share prices can fall below the IPO price, leading to losses.

## FAQ

### What is an IPO?
An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public, allowing ordinary people to buy a part of the company.

### How do I apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your demat account provider (broker) using ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) or UPI.

### What is "allotment"?
"Allotment" is the process where shares are distributed to applicants who successfully bid for them in an IPO. Due to high demand, you might not get all the shares you applied for, or sometimes none at all.

### What is an SME IPO?
SME IPOs are for Small and Medium Enterprises that list on dedicated platforms (like NSE Emerge or BSE SME) for smaller companies, often with different listing rules than larger Mainboard IPOs.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger companies and list on the main exchanges (NSE and BSE), while SME IPOs are for smaller companies and list on special SME platforms with different market capitalisation and trading volume requirements.
