# Your Bank Can Now Help Companies Buy Other Businesses: RBI's New Rule

**TL;DR**
- The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has changed rules for how banks can lend money.
- Indian banks can now directly fund companies that want to buy other businesses.
- This helps companies grow faster by acquiring existing businesses, not just building from scratch.
- Banks can lend up to 75% of the acquisition cost, with the buying company putting in the rest.
- The new rule aims to boost business growth and make India's mergers and acquisitions (M&A) market more dynamic.

## What's this about?
Until recently, Indian banks generally couldn't directly lend money to companies specifically for buying shares to take control of another business. This was a way to keep banks from getting too involved in the risks of the stock market or funding speculative deals. But now, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has updated its rules. Indian banks can, for the first time, directly provide loans to eligible companies for acquiring other non-financial businesses. This is a big change that makes it easier for Indian companies to grow by purchasing other firms.

## Why it's in the news right now
This new policy is part of the RBI's "revised capital market exposure framework," and it officially came into effect on July 1, 2026. News outlets have been discussing this significant shift, highlighting how it will impact the way businesses expand and how deals are financed in India. It's seen as a move to make India's corporate landscape more dynamic and competitive.

## How it actually works

1.  **The Old Way**: Previously, if a company wanted to acquire another business by buying its shares, it largely had to use its own cash, raise money from investors, or borrow from non-banking financial companies (NBFCs). Banks were restricted from directly funding the purchase of shares for acquiring control.
2.  **The New Rule**: The RBI has updated its guidelines, allowing scheduled commercial banks in India to provide loans for "acquisition of control" over other companies.
3.  **Who Can Borrow**: The loans are available to "eligible corporate borrowers." This means companies that meet the bank's and RBI's criteria for financial health and creditworthiness.
4.  **What Can Be Acquired**: The target company (the one being bought) must be a "non-financial target." This means it cannot be another bank, a Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC), or any other financial services entity. It must be a company involved in manufacturing, services, or other non-financial sectors.
5.  **Funding Limits**: A bank can finance up to 75% of the total value of the acquisition. The company doing the buying must contribute at least 25% of the acquisition value from its own funds.
6.  **Purpose**: The loan is specifically for gaining control of another company, whether the target company is listed on a stock exchange or is a private, unlisted entity.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a successful *kirana* (grocery) shop owner, Mrs. Lakshmi, who wants to expand her business. She sees a smaller *kirana* shop in the next lane that is struggling, and she believes she can make it profitable by taking it over.

Earlier, if Mrs. Lakshmi wanted to buy that entire shop (its business, brand, and customer base), her bank might have given her a loan for things like buying new stock or renovating her existing shop. But they typically couldn't give her a loan specifically to buy the *ownership* of the other shop. She would have to use her own savings or borrow from other sources.

Now, with the RBI's new rule, if Mrs. Lakshmi's existing business is strong and she's an "eligible borrower," her bank can actually lend her money directly to buy the other *kirana* shop. The bank might say, "We'll lend you up to 75% of the value of that shop, but you need to put in at least 25% from your own pocket." This makes it much easier for Mrs. Lakshmi to grow her business by acquiring another one, rather than having to build a new shop from scratch.

## Why it matters to you

1.  **Faster Business Growth**: This rule helps Indian companies grow more quickly by acquiring other businesses. Larger, stronger Indian companies can mean more innovation, better products, and potentially more jobs.
2.  **Increased Competition**: As companies grow through mergers and acquisitions (M&A), it can lead to more competition in various sectors. This might benefit you as a consumer with more choices, better quality, and potentially more competitive prices for goods and services.
3.  **Dynamic Economy**: A more active M&A market signals a dynamic and evolving economy where businesses are constantly restructuring, consolidating, and expanding. This can attract more investment into the country.
4.  **Indirect Impact on Investments**: If companies grow and perform well, it can indirectly benefit individuals who invest in the stock market or in mutual funds that hold shares of these growing companies. (Remember, this is not investment advice).
5.  **Managed Risk for Stability**: While banks are now taking on a new type of lending, the RBI has put safeguards in place, such as the 75% funding limit and strict eligibility criteria for borrowers and target companies, to ensure the financial system remains stable.

## Common confusions cleared up

*   **Myth**: Banks will now directly invest in the stock market with this new rule.
    *   **Fact**: No, banks are *lending money to companies* so those companies can acquire other businesses. Banks are not directly buying shares for their own investment portfolios or engaging in speculative trading. The risk is primarily with the corporate borrower, not directly the bank's market exposure.
*   **Myth**: This means anyone can get a loan from a bank to buy shares of any company for personal investment.
    *   **Fact**: Absolutely not. This facility is strictly for "eligible corporate borrowers" aiming to acquire "control" over "non-financial target" companies for strategic business growth. It is not for individual stock market investments or speculative purposes.
*   **Myth**: This will make the banking system much riskier.
    *   **Fact**: The RBI has introduced this under a "revised capital market exposure framework" and has set clear limits (like funding up to 75% of the acquisition value) and eligibility criteria. These measures are designed to manage and mitigate potential risks, ensuring financial stability.

## FAQ

### What does "acquisition of control" mean?
It means buying enough shares or assets to gain significant influence or decision-making power over another company, often leading to majority ownership.

### Are there any restrictions on which companies can be acquired with these loans?
Yes, the company being acquired must be a "non-financial target." This means it should not be another bank, a Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC), or any other entity primarily involved in financial services.

### What happens if the acquiring company fails to repay the loan?
Like any other corporate loan, if the borrower company defaults, the bank will follow its standard recovery procedures. This might involve taking over the assets or shares that were pledged as security for the loan.

### Does this mean banks will now actively encourage companies to merge and acquire?
Banks will likely offer this as a new financing product to their corporate clients, supporting their growth strategies. While it facilitates M&A, the decision to merge or acquire ultimately rests with the companies themselves.
