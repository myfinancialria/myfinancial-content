## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The day these shares start trading on the stock exchange is called the **listing day**. Often, there's a lot of excitement on listing day, and the share price might jump significantly, leading to a **listing gain** (the percentage increase from the IPO price to the listing day closing price).

However, this initial excitement can sometimes fade quickly. Many factors, like the demand from large investors (called anchor investors) or short-term traders, can cause big price swings on the first day. Anchor investors, who are big institutional players, often have a "lock-in" period, meaning they cannot sell their shares for a certain number of days (often 30 days for a portion of their shares).

By looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after its listing, we get a calmer, more settled view. The initial frenzy has usually passed, and the market has had more time to assess the company's value. This period often gives a more realistic picture of how the stock is performing beyond the first-day buzz.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's price movement on listing day, whether it "pops" (rises sharply) or "drops" (falls), is a result of basic **demand and supply** in the market.

Imagine a school fete stall selling a special limited-edition snack.
- If many students want the snack but there are only a few available (high demand, low supply), the price might go up quickly. This is like an IPO that sees high **subscription levels** (many times more applications than shares offered), leading to a "pop" on listing.
- If very few students are interested in the snack, and there are many left (low demand, high supply), the stall might have to lower the price to sell them. This is similar to an IPO with low demand, which might "drop" on listing.

Other factors include:
- **Market mood:** If the overall stock market is doing well, it can create a positive sentiment for new IPOs. If the market is down, new listings might struggle.
- **Pricing:** The company and its investment bankers decide the **issue price** (the price at which shares are offered in the IPO). If the price is set too high, investors might not find it attractive, leading to a drop. If it's priced conservatively, there might be room for a listing gain.
- **Anchor investors:** These are large institutional investors (like mutual funds or insurance companies) who subscribe to a significant portion of the IPO before it opens to the public. Their participation often builds confidence. However, a portion of their shares typically has a **lock-in period** (a time during which they cannot sell their shares). Once this lock-in period ends, if these investors decide to sell, it can increase the supply of shares and affect the price.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot. The stock market is dynamic, and prices are always changing.
- **Volatility:** New stocks can be very volatile (prices can swing up and down a lot) in their early days as the market tries to figure out their true value.
- **Broader market:** The performance of the overall stock market can also influence individual stock prices. A great company might see its stock price fall if the entire market is in a downturn, and vice-versa.
- **Long-term view:** A stock might list flat (no big gain or loss) but perform very well over several months or years as the company grows. Similarly, a stock that has a huge listing gain might not sustain that growth and could fall later. The initial price action doesn't guarantee future performance.

## A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a new shop opening in your neighbourhood. On the first day, there might be a lot of buzz, free samples, and special offers, drawing a big crowd. The initial rush might make it seem like the shop is super busy and successful. But after a month or two, the initial excitement fades. The real test is whether people keep coming back because they like the products and service, or if the crowd disappears. The stock price 30-60 days later is like seeing how many customers are still visiting the shop after the initial hype has settled.

## Things people get confused about

### Myth → Fact

- **Myth:** A big listing gain means it's a great company.
  **Fact:** A strong listing gain only reflects high initial demand for the shares, not necessarily the company's long-term performance or fundamental strength. The company's business results and future prospects are more important for long-term value.

- **Myth:** If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a "bad" IPO.
  **Fact:** A listing below the issue price means the market currently values the shares less than the IPO price. This could be due to market conditions, pricing, or investor sentiment at that time. It doesn't automatically mean the company itself is bad or that the stock won't perform well in the future.

- **Myth:** IPOs are a guaranteed way to make quick money.
  **Fact:** IPOs carry market risks, just like any other stock market investment. There is no guarantee of listing gains, and prices can go down as well as up.

## FAQ

### What does "issue price" mean?
The issue price is the fixed price per share at which a company offers its shares to the public during an IPO.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME (Small and Medium Enterprises) IPOs are for smaller companies, listed on dedicated SME platforms, often with different rules and lower minimum investment amounts.

### How do I calculate my return if I was allotted shares?
You can calculate your return by taking the current market price, subtracting your IPO issue price, dividing by the IPO issue price, and then multiplying by 100 to get a percentage.

### Should I sell my shares on listing day if there's a gain?
Whether to sell on listing day or hold depends on your personal financial goals and risk tolerance. Some investors aim for quick listing gains, while others prefer to hold for the long term, believing in the company's growth potential.

### Is it too late to invest if an IPO has already listed?
No, once an IPO lists, its shares can be bought and sold on the open market just like any other stock. You can invest in a listed company at any time, subject to market conditions and your own research.
