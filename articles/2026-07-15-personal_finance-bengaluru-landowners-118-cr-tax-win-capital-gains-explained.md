# Bengaluru Landowner's ₹11.8 Cr Tax Win: Capital Gains Explained

**TL;DR**
- A Bengaluru resident successfully claimed full tax exemption on capital gains from selling 17 apartments, amounting to ₹11.8 crore.
- The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) ruled that each property sale should be treated as an independent transaction for exemption purposes.
- This landmark decision clarifies that the 'one house' restriction for capital gains exemption applies to the *investment* made, not the number of properties sold.
- Taxpayers can now claim exemptions under Section 54 or 54F for gains arising from multiple property sales, provided conditions are met for each.
- This ruling offers significant relief and clarity for individuals selling multiple residential properties and reinvesting the proceeds into new homes.

## What this means in plain terms

When you sell a property in India, any profit you make is generally considered a 'capital gain' and is subject to income tax. To encourage housing, the Income Tax Act offers exemptions, primarily under Section 54 and Section 54F, if you reinvest these gains into buying or constructing a new residential house. The common understanding, and often the tax department's interpretation, was that these exemptions were primarily meant for reinvesting the proceeds from *one* sale into *one* new house.

However, the recent Bengaluru case has provided crucial clarity. A landowner sold 17 flats over time, making a substantial profit of ₹11.8 crore. He claimed tax exemptions on these gains by reinvesting them into new residential properties. The Income Tax Department initially rejected his claims, arguing that he couldn't claim exemptions for multiple properties sold. The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) sided with the taxpayer. The ITAT's ruling essentially states that each sale of a residential property is an independent transaction. Therefore, if you meet the conditions for exemption (like reinvesting the capital gains into a new house within specified timelines) for *each* of those independent sales, you can claim the exemption for all of them, even if you sold multiple properties. This means the 'one house' rule applies to the *new investment* you make from each specific capital gain, rather than limiting the total number of exemptions you can claim from various sales.

## Understanding Capital Gains on Property Sales

When you sell a property in India, the profit or gain you make is categorised as a 'capital gain'. This gain is taxable under the Income Tax Act, 1961. The taxation depends largely on how long you held the property.

### Long-Term vs. Short-Term Capital Gains

*   **Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG):** If you sell a property within 24 months (two years) of acquiring it, the gains are considered short-term. These gains are added to your total income and taxed according to your applicable income tax slab rates.
*   **Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG):** If you sell a property after holding it for more than 24 months, the gains are considered long-term. LTCG on property is typically taxed at a flat rate of 20% after factoring in indexation benefits. Indexation allows you to adjust the purchase price for inflation, thereby reducing your taxable gain.

### The Exemption Framework: Section 54 and Section 54F

To promote investment in housing, the Income Tax Act provides specific exemptions from capital gains tax if the proceeds are reinvested into a new residential house.

*   **Section 54:** This section applies when you sell a *residential house property* (which results in LTCG) and use the net consideration to purchase or construct *another residential house property* in India. The exemption is available to individuals and Hindu Undivided Families (HUFs). The entire capital gain is exempt if the cost of the new house is equal to or more than the capital gain. If the cost of the new house is less than the capital gain, the exemption is limited to the cost of the new house.
*   **Section 54F:** This section applies when you sell *any long-term capital asset other than a residential house* (e.g., land, commercial property, shares) and invest the *net sale consideration* into purchasing or constructing a *residential house property* in India. The exemption here depends on the proportion of the net sale consideration invested. If the entire net sale consideration is invested, the entire capital gain is exempt. If only a part is invested, the exemption is proportionate to the investment made.

## The 'One House' Conundrum and the ITAT Ruling

Historically, there has been ambiguity and often contention between taxpayers and the Income Tax Department regarding the interpretation of the "one residential house" clause in Sections 54 and 54F.

### The Conventional View vs. Taxpayer Claims

The tax department often interpreted the exemption narrowly, especially when a taxpayer sold multiple properties. The conventional view was that the benefit of Section 54/54F was limited to the purchase or construction of a *single* new residential house, or that the exemption could only be claimed once per assessment year, irrespective of the number of properties sold. This led to many disputes where taxpayers who sold multiple flats or plots and reinvested the gains were denied full exemptions.

### The Bengaluru Case Precedent

The recent case involving a Bengaluru landowner selling 17 flats for ₹11.8 crore in capital gains highlighted this ambiguity. The assessee claimed exemptions under Section 54 for these gains by reinvesting in new residential properties. The Income Tax Department initially rejected these claims, arguing against the applicability of the exemption for multiple sales.

### ITAT's Clarification: Each Sale is Independent

The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) delivered a significant ruling in favour of the taxpayer. The core of the ITAT's decision was that "each independent transaction of sale of a residential house property would qualify for exemption under Section 54 of the Act, provided the conditions specified therein are fulfilled." This means that if a taxpayer sells multiple residential properties, each sale is to be treated as a distinct event. For each such distinct event, if the capital gains arising from that specific sale are reinvested into a new residential property (or properties, subject to certain conditions regarding the *new* purchase itself), the exemption under Section 54 can be claimed.

This ruling effectively separates the *number of sales* from the *number of investments*. It clarifies that the restriction, if any, on the number of residential houses applies to the *new asset* acquired for claiming exemption, not to the number of original assets sold. For instance, if you sell two separate flats and invest the gain from each into one new house, or into two separate new houses, the exemption can be claimed for both original sales. This interpretation provides much-needed relief and a clearer path for individuals and HUFs dealing with multiple property transactions.

## Conditions for Claiming Section 54/54F Exemptions

While the ITAT ruling provides flexibility on the number of properties sold, it's crucial to remember that the underlying conditions for claiming exemptions under Section 54 and 54F must still be rigorously met.

### Time Limits for Reinvestment

The most critical condition relates to the timeline for reinvesting the capital gains:
*   **Purchase:** You must purchase the new residential house either one year before the date of sale of the original asset or within two years after the date of sale.
*   **Construction:** If you are constructing a new house, the construction must be completed within three years from the date of sale of the original asset.

These timelines are strict, and failing to adhere to them can lead to the denial of the exemption.

### Investment in India

The new residential property acquired (either purchased or constructed) must be located in India. Exemptions are not available for investments made in properties outside India.

### Capital Gains Account Scheme (CGAS)

If you have not invested the capital gains into a new house before the due date for filing your income tax return for the year in which the original asset was sold, you must deposit the unutilised capital gains amount into a Capital Gains Account Scheme (CGAS) with a public sector bank. This deposit must be made before the income tax return filing deadline. If the amount deposited in CGAS is not utilised for purchasing or constructing the new house within the stipulated timelines (2 or 3 years), the unutilised amount becomes taxable as LTCG in the financial year in which the 2-year or 3-year period expires.

### Restriction on Sale of New Property

The new residential property acquired to claim the exemption cannot be sold within three years from its date of purchase or completion of construction. If it is sold within this lock-in period, the exemption originally claimed will be revoked, and the capital gains will become taxable in the year of sale of the new property.

### Number of Properties for Reinvestment

While the Bengaluru ITAT ruling clarifies that multiple *sales* can qualify for exemption, the law regarding the *new property acquired* also has nuances. For Section 54, if the capital gain does not exceed ₹2 crore, an assessee can opt to invest in two residential houses in India. This option is available only once in the lifetime of the assessee. For Section 54F, the proceeds from the sale of a non-residential asset must generally be invested in *one* residential house in India to claim the exemption, unless the aforementioned ₹2 crore limit and lifetime option apply. The key takeaway from the ITAT ruling is that the source of funds from *each independent sale* can be traced to a qualifying new investment.

## Navigating the Nuances for Multiple Property Sellers

The ITAT's ruling in the Bengaluru case marks a significant shift in how capital gains exemptions are viewed for taxpayers selling multiple properties. Understanding and strategically applying these nuances is crucial for maximising tax efficiency.

### Independent Transactions: The Cornerstone

The core principle established by the ITAT is that each sale of a residential property is an "independent transaction." This means that the eligibility for exemption under Section 54 (or 54F, by extension) should be assessed for each sale individually, rather than clubbing all sales together under a single exemption limit. For instance, if you sell Flat A and then Flat B, you can claim Section 54 exemption for gains from Flat A by reinvesting its proceeds, and separately claim Section 54 exemption for gains from Flat B by reinvesting its proceeds, even if both sales happen in the same financial year.

### Strategic Reinvestment Planning

For individuals who own and plan to sell multiple properties, this ruling opens avenues for strategic tax planning:
*   **Clear Fund Segregation:** It becomes vital to clearly segregate the capital gains arising from each sale. While practically, money might flow into a common bank account, maintaining meticulous records to show how the gains from 'Sale 1' were used for 'New Property 1' and gains from 'Sale 2' for 'New Property 2' (or even the same 'New Property 1' if its cost is high enough) is paramount.
*   **Matching Gains to Investment:** If you sell multiple properties and buy one expensive new property, you can attribute the gains from each sale towards the cost of the single new property, up to its total cost, to claim full exemption for all gains. For example, if you sell two flats with ₹50 lakhs gain each, and buy one new house for ₹1.2 crore, you can claim ₹50 lakhs exemption from the first sale and another ₹50 lakhs from the second sale, as the total investment of ₹1.2 crore covers both gains.
*   **Utilising the ₹2 Crore Limit:** Remember the lifetime option under Section 54 to invest in two residential houses if the capital gain does not exceed ₹2 crore. This adds another layer of flexibility for smaller gains from individual sales.

### Documentation is Key

Given the tax department's historical stance, meticulous record-keeping is non-negotiable. For each property sale and subsequent reinvestment, ensure you have:
*   Sale deeds and purchase agreements of both old and new properties.
*   Bank statements showing the flow of funds from sale proceeds to the new investment.
*   Cost of acquisition and improvement documents for indexation purposes.
*   Details of deposits into the Capital Gains Account Scheme, if applicable.
*   Any other relevant financial documents proving the linkage between the capital gain and the reinvestment.

### Potential Challenges and Professional Advice

While ITAT rulings provide strong precedents, they are not always binding on lower tax authorities in all cases. The Income Tax Department may still raise queries or challenges, especially if documentation is not robust. Therefore, it is highly advisable to consult with a Certified Financial Planner or a tax expert who is well-versed in capital gains laws and recent judicial pronouncements. They can help structure your transactions and documentation to minimise potential disputes and ensure compliance.

## A real example

Let's consider **Priya, 45, self-employed, Mumbai**, who has a diverse property portfolio. She decided to downsize her holdings and upgrade to a larger family home.

**Scenario:**
Priya sold two small residential flats in Mumbai in the financial year 2025-26:
1.  **Flat A:** Sold in July 2025 for ₹80 lakhs. It was purchased in 2010 for ₹20 lakhs.
    *   Indexed Cost of Acquisition (assuming a Cost Inflation Index, CII, of 346 for 2024-25 and 167 for 2010-11): ₹20,00,000 * (346 / 167) = ₹41,43,713.
    *   Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG) from Flat A: ₹80,00,000 - ₹41,43,713 = ₹38,56,287.
2.  **Flat B:** Sold in November 2025 for ₹95 lakhs. It was purchased in 2012 for ₹25 lakhs.
    *   Indexed Cost of Acquisition (assuming CII of 346 for 2024-25 and 200 for 2012-13): ₹25,00,000 * (346 / 200) = ₹43,25,000.
    *   Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG) from Flat B: ₹95,00,000 - ₹43,25,000 = ₹51,75,000.

**Total Capital Gains:** ₹38,56,287 (Flat A) + ₹51,75,000 (Flat B) = ₹90,31,287.

**Reinvestment:**
In March 2026, Priya purchased a new, larger residential apartment in Mumbai for **₹1.60 crore**.

**Tax Implication without ITAT Ruling (Traditional View):**
Under the traditional, stricter interpretation, the tax department might argue that Priya can only claim exemption for *one* sale, or up to the cost of one new house, or that the 'one house' rule applies to the new property, making it ambiguous for two sources of gain. If only one exemption was allowed, say for Flat A's gain, then ₹51,75,000 from Flat B would be taxable at 20%, leading to a tax liability of ₹10,35,000 (plus cess).

**Tax Implication with ITAT Ruling (Bengaluru Precedent):**
Based on the ITAT ruling, each sale is an independent transaction.
*   **For Flat A's sale:** Priya generated ₹38,56,287 in LTCG. She invested ₹1.60 crore in a new house. Since the investment is greater than the capital gain from Flat A, the entire ₹38,56,287 is exempt under Section 54.
*   **For Flat B's sale:** Priya generated ₹51,75,000 in LTCG. She invested ₹1.60 crore in a new house. Since the investment is greater than the capital gain from Flat B, the entire ₹51,75,000 is exempt under Section 54.

**Outcome:**
Priya successfully claims full exemption for both capital gains as the total investment (₹1.60 crore) exceeds the sum of both individual capital gains (₹90,31,287).
**Tax Saved:** Without the exemption, ₹90,31,287 would be taxed at 20% (plus cess), which is approximately ₹18,06,257. By leveraging the ITAT ruling and Section 54, Priya pays **₹0** tax on these capital gains.

This example clearly demonstrates how treating each sale as an independent transaction, and having sufficient reinvestment, can lead to significant tax savings for individuals selling multiple properties.

## FAQ

### Can I claim Section 54 exemption for selling more than one house?
Yes, based on the recent ITAT ruling, you can claim Section 54 exemption for capital gains arising from the sale of multiple residential houses, provided each sale is treated as an independent transaction and its proceeds are reinvested as per the rules.

### Does the 'one house' rule apply to the new property purchased, or the properties sold?
The 'one house' rule primarily applies to the *new property* purchased or constructed for claiming the exemption. However, there's a lifetime option to invest in two residential houses if the capital gain does not exceed ₹2 crore. The ITAT ruling clarifies that the number of properties *sold* does not restrict the number of exemptions.

### What if I sell multiple properties but buy only one new house?
You can still claim exemptions for capital gains from multiple sales if the total cost of the single new house is equal to or greater than the combined capital gains from all the properties sold. The key is to demonstrate that the gains from each sale were effectively reinvested.

### Is this ITAT ruling binding on all tax authorities?
ITAT rulings are binding on the Income Tax Department within the jurisdiction of that specific ITAT bench. While not binding on other ITAT benches or higher courts, they serve as strong judicial precedents and are generally considered persuasive by tax authorities across India.

### What is the Capital Gains Account Scheme?
The Capital Gains Account Scheme (CGAS) is a facility where you can deposit your unutilised capital gains if you haven't reinvested them into a new property before the income tax return filing deadline. This allows you to still claim the exemption, provided you utilise the funds within the stipulated 2 or 3-year period.

### Can NRIs claim Section 54/54F exemptions?
Yes, Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) are eligible to claim exemptions under Section 54 and 54F, provided all the conditions, including the reinvestment in a residential house located in India, are met.

### What is the time limit for reinvesting capital gains?
You must purchase the new residential house one year before or two years after the sale of the original asset, or construct it within three years after the sale.
