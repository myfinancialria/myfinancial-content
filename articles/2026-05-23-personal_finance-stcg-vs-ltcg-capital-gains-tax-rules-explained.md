# STCG vs LTCG: Capital Gains Tax Rules Explained

**TL;DR**
- Capital gains are profits earned from selling a capital asset, such as shares, mutual funds, or property.
- These gains are classified as either short-term (STCG) or long-term (LTCG) based on the asset's holding period.
- The holding period for an asset determines whether the gain is short-term or long-term, varying by asset type.
- STCG is generally taxed at higher rates, often aligned with your income tax slab, while LTCG usually enjoys preferential, lower tax rates.
- Understanding these differences is crucial for effective tax planning and optimising your investment returns.

## What this means in plain terms

When you sell an asset like shares, a house, or even gold, and make a profit, the Indian tax authorities call this a "capital gain." This profit isn't just free money; it's subject to tax, just like your salary or business income. The specific tax you pay on this profit depends on how long you held the asset before selling it.

Think of it this way: if you sell something quickly after buying it, the profit is usually called a Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG). If you hold onto it for a longer period before selling, the profit becomes a Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG). The tax rules for STCG and LTCG are quite different, with long-term gains often enjoying more favourable tax treatment, encouraging long-term investing.

The holding period that separates short-term from long-term varies significantly depending on the type of asset. For instance, the period for shares is different from that for real estate or debt mutual funds. Knowing these distinctions is vital for anyone investing in India, as it directly impacts your net returns after tax.

## Understanding Capital Gains: The Basics

Capital gains arise from the transfer of a "capital asset." As per the Income Tax Act, 1961, a capital asset includes property of any kind held by an assessee, whether connected with their business or profession or not. This broad definition encompasses a wide range of assets, including:

*   **Financial Assets:** Shares, debentures, mutual funds (equity-oriented and debt-oriented), bonds.
*   **Immovable Property:** Land, buildings, house property.
*   **Movable Property:** Jewellery, archaeological collections, drawings, paintings, sculptures, any work of art.

The capital gain is calculated as the difference between the net sale consideration (selling price minus transfer expenses like brokerage) and the cost of acquisition (purchase price plus improvement costs). For long-term capital gains on certain assets, an adjustment called "indexation" is allowed, which accounts for inflation over the holding period, thereby reducing the taxable gain. This is a significant benefit for investors, as we will explore further. For detailed provisions, one can refer to the Income Tax Act, 1961, available on the Income Tax India website (https://incometax.gov.in).

## Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG) Rules and Taxation

A capital gain is classified as short-term if the asset is held for a period not exceeding a specified duration, which varies by asset type. The tax rates for STCG are generally higher and can sometimes be added to your regular income, depending on the asset.

### Holding Periods for STCG

*   **Equity Shares and Equity-Oriented Mutual Funds (EOMFs):** If held for **12 months or less**, the gain is short-term. EOMFs are those where at least 65% of the corpus is invested in Indian equities, as per SEBI (https://sebi.gov.in) guidelines.
*   **Debt Mutual Funds, Gold, Real Estate, and Other Assets:** If held for **36 months or less**, the gain is short-term.
*   **Unlisted Shares:** If held for **24 months or less**, the gain is short-term.

### Taxation of STCG

*   **Equity Shares and Equity-Oriented Mutual Funds (EOMFs):** STCG on these assets, if transacted on a recognised stock exchange and subject to Securities Transaction Tax (STT), is taxed at a flat rate of **15%** under Section 111A of the Income Tax Act. This is applicable irrespective of your income tax slab.
*   **Debt Mutual Funds, Real Estate, Gold, and Other Assets:** STCG from these assets is added to your total income and taxed at your applicable income tax slab rate. For instance, if you are in the 30% tax bracket, your STCG from selling a property quickly will be taxed at 30% (plus surcharge and cess, if applicable).

It's important to remember that these rates are exclusive of applicable surcharge and health & education cess.

## Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) Rules and Taxation

Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) arise when an asset is held for a period exceeding the specified duration. LTCG typically enjoys more favourable tax treatment compared to STCG, with lower tax rates and the benefit of indexation for certain assets.

### Holding Periods for LTCG

*   **Equity Shares and Equity-Oriented Mutual Funds (EOMFs):** If held for **more than 12 months**, the gain is long-term.
*   **Debt Mutual Funds, Gold, Real Estate, and Other Assets:** If held for **more than 36 months**, the gain is long-term.
*   **Unlisted Shares:** If held for **more than 24 months**, the gain is long-term.

### Taxation of LTCG

*   **Equity Shares and Equity-Oriented Mutual Funds (EOMFs):** LTCG exceeding ₹1 lakh in a financial year from the sale of these assets (on which STT has been paid) is taxed at a special rate of **10%** without the benefit of indexation, under Section 112A. Gains up to ₹1 lakh in a financial year are exempt from tax.
*   **Debt Mutual Funds, Real Estate, Gold, and Other Assets:** LTCG from these assets is taxed at a rate of **20%** after considering the benefit of indexation. Indexation adjusts the cost of acquisition for inflation using the Cost Inflation Index (CII) published by the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) (https://incometaxindia.gov.in). This significantly reduces the taxable gain.
*   **Other Long-Term Assets (e.g., unlisted shares, certain bonds):** These are generally taxed at **20%** with indexation benefit, or **10%** without indexation, whichever is more beneficial to the taxpayer, under Section 112.

The benefit of indexation is particularly powerful for assets held for many years, as it significantly reduces the effective tax liability by increasing the 'indexed cost of acquisition'.

## Capital Gains Treatment for Key Asset Classes

Understanding the specific holding periods and tax implications for common investment assets is crucial for tax planning.

### Equity Shares and Equity-Oriented Mutual Funds

*   **STCG (held ≤ 12 months):** Taxed at 15% (Section 111A) if STT paid.
*   **LTCG (held > 12 months):** Taxed at 10% on gains exceeding ₹1 lakh per financial year (Section 112A) if STT paid. No indexation benefit.

### Debt Mutual Funds

*   **STCG (held ≤ 36 months):** Added to income and taxed at slab rates.
*   **LTCG (held > 36 months):** Taxed at 20% with indexation benefit. For debt mutual funds purchased before April 1, 2023, this rule applies. For those purchased on or after April 1, 2023, gains are treated as short-term and taxed at slab rates, eliminating the LTCG benefit for new investments in these funds.

### Real Estate (Land & Building)

*   **STCG (held ≤ 36 months):** Added to income and taxed at slab rates.
*   **LTCG (held > 36 months):** Taxed at 20% with indexation benefit. Exemptions are available under Sections 54, 54EC, and 54F if the capital gain is reinvested in specified assets.

### Gold (Physical Gold, Gold ETFs, Gold Mutual Funds)

*   **STCG (held ≤ 36 months):** Added to income and taxed at slab rates.
*   **LTCG (held > 36 months):** Taxed at 20% with indexation benefit. Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs) have a special treatment: LTCG on redemption is exempt from tax for individual investors. However, if SGBs are sold on an exchange before maturity, LTCG is taxed at 10% without indexation or 20% with indexation.

## A real example

Let's consider **Aisha, 42, ₹35L CTC, Mumbai**, who made several investment decisions in the past year. Her income places her in the 30% tax bracket.

1.  **Equity Mutual Fund Sale (STCG):** Aisha invested ₹5 lakhs in an equity-oriented mutual fund on January 15, 2023. She sold it on November 20, 2023, for ₹5.80 lakhs.
    *   Holding Period: Less than 12 months (short-term).
    *   Capital Gain: ₹5.80 lakhs - ₹5 lakhs = ₹80,000.
    *   Tax: Since it's an equity-oriented fund and held for less than 12 months, it's STCG under Section 111A. Taxable at 15%.
    *   STCG Tax = 15% of ₹80,000 = ₹12,000.

2.  **Equity Shares Sale (LTCG):** Aisha bought shares of a listed company for ₹10 lakhs on June 1, 2021. She sold them on July 10, 2023, for ₹12.50 lakhs.
    *   Holding Period: More than 12 months (long-term).
    *   Capital Gain: ₹12.50 lakhs - ₹10 lakhs = ₹2.50 lakhs.
    *   Tax: This is LTCG under Section 112A. The first ₹1 lakh of LTCG on equity is exempt.
    *   Taxable LTCG = ₹2.50 lakhs - ₹1 lakh (exemption) = ₹1.50 lakhs.
    *   LTCG Tax = 10% of ₹1.50 lakhs = ₹15,000.

3.  **Debt Mutual Fund Sale (LTCG with Indexation):** Aisha invested ₹8 lakhs in a debt mutual fund on April 1, 2020. She sold it on May 5, 2023, for ₹10.50 lakhs. Assume the Cost Inflation Index (CII) was 301 for FY 2020-21 and 348 for FY 2023-24 (the year of sale).
    *   Holding Period: More than 36 months (long-term).
    *   Original Cost: ₹8 lakhs.
    *   Indexed Cost of Acquisition = Original Cost * (CII of sale year / CII of acquisition year)
    *   Indexed Cost = ₹8,00,000 * (348 / 301) = ₹8,00,000 * 1.156146 = ₹9,24,917.
    *   LTCG = Sale Price - Indexed Cost = ₹10,50,000 - ₹9,24,917 = ₹1,25,083.
    *   Tax: Taxable at 20% with indexation.
    *   LTCG Tax = 20% of ₹1,25,083 = ₹25,016.60.

In total, Aisha's capital gains tax liability for the year would be ₹12,000 (STCG) + ₹15,000 (LTCG equity) + ₹25,016.60 (LTCG debt) = ₹52,016.60 (excluding cess and surcharge for simplicity). This example highlights how different assets and holding periods lead to varying tax treatments.

## What to do this week

1.  **Review your investment portfolio:** List all your capital assets (shares, mutual funds, property, gold) and their respective purchase dates and costs.
2.  **Estimate potential gains/losses:** For any assets you plan to sell soon, calculate the estimated capital gain or loss and determine if it's short-term or long-term based on holding periods.
3.  **Understand your tax slab:** Be aware of your current income tax slab, as it directly impacts the taxation of STCG from non-equity assets.
4.  **Consult the latest CII:** If you are considering selling long-term non-equity assets, check the latest Cost Inflation Index (CII) on the Income Tax India website (https://incometax.gov.in) to estimate your indexed cost.
5.  **Consider tax-loss harvesting:** If you have capital losses, explore whether they can be set off against capital gains to reduce your overall tax liability.

## FAQ

### ### What is a capital asset for tax purposes?
A capital asset includes any property held by an individual, whether connected to their business or not. This encompasses financial assets like shares and mutual funds, immovable property such as land and buildings, and certain movable assets like jewellery.

### ### How is the holding period calculated for capital gains?
The holding period is calculated from the date of acquisition of the asset to the date of its sale or transfer. The specific duration that classifies a gain as short-term or long-term varies by asset type, typically 12, 24, or 36 months.

### ### Is indexation benefit available for all long-term capital gains?
No, indexation benefit is primarily available for long-term capital gains on non-equity assets like real estate, debt mutual funds (for investments before April 1, 2023), and gold. It is not available for LTCG on listed equity shares or equity-oriented mutual funds under Section 112A.

### ### What is Section 112A?
Section 112A of the Income Tax Act deals with long-term capital gains from the sale of listed equity shares or equity-oriented mutual funds. It stipulates that LTCG exceeding ₹1 lakh in a financial year is taxed at a flat rate of 10%, provided Securities Transaction Tax (STT) was paid.

### ### Can I set off capital losses against capital gains?
Yes, capital losses can be set off against capital gains. Short-term capital losses can be set off against both short-term and long-term capital gains. Long-term capital losses can only be set off against long-term capital gains. Unutilised losses can be carried forward for up to 8 assessment years.

### ### Are there any exemptions for Long-Term Capital Gains on property?
Yes, certain exemptions are available under Sections 54, 54EC, and 54F of the Income Tax Act. These typically involve reinvesting the capital gains or the net sale consideration into a new residential house or specified bonds within a stipulated timeframe.

### ### How are Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs) taxed?
LTCG arising from the redemption of SGBs by an individual investor is exempt from tax. However, if SGBs are sold on a stock exchange before maturity, the capital gains (STCG or LTCG) are taxable as per standard rules for gold, with LTCG benefiting from indexation.
