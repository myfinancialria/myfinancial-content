## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public through an IPO (Initial Public Offering), there's often a lot of excitement. The first day of trading, known as "listing day," can see a lot of activity. The "listing gain" is the percentage change between the IPO price (the price you paid for the shares) and the price the shares close at on their very first day of trading.

However, this initial excitement can sometimes lead to very high or very low prices that don't always reflect the company's true value in the long run. Many short-term traders might buy and sell quickly, causing big swings. Also, a group of investors called "anchor investors" (large institutional investors who commit to buying a significant portion of the IPO) usually have a "lock-in" period, meaning they cannot sell their shares for 30 days.

Looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after it lists helps us see how the market has settled down after this initial frenzy and after the anchor investor lock-in period ends. It gives a calmer, more instructive read on how the shares are performing once the early excitement has faded and more regular buying and selling patterns emerge.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's performance on listing day is mainly a game of demand versus supply.
*   **Demand:** How many people want to buy the shares? If many investors, big and small, want to buy the shares, the "subscription levels" (how many times over the IPO shares are applied for) will be high. High demand can push the price up.
*   **Supply:** How many shares are available? The company decides how many shares to offer.
*   **Pricing:** The company and its bankers set the "issue price" (the IPO price) based on their valuation. If the price is seen as "reasonable" or even a little low, it can attract more buyers.
*   **Market Mood:** The overall sentiment of the stock market also plays a role. If the broader market is doing well, new listings often get a warmer reception.
*   **Anchor Investors and Lock-in:** As mentioned, anchor investors are large investors who get shares before the public IPO. Their commitment often builds confidence. They usually have a "lock-in" period (often 30 days) during which they cannot sell their shares, which can help stabilise the price initially.

When an IPO "pops" (lists higher than its issue price), it generally means there was strong demand, possibly because the issue was priced attractively or the company was highly anticipated. When an IPO "drops" (lists lower than its issue price), it could be due to lower-than-expected demand, a perceived high issue price, or a weak overall market sentiment.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

Think of it like a new shop opening in your neighbourhood. On the first day, there might be a huge crowd because of curiosity, special offers, or just the buzz. The shop might sell out quickly. But after a few weeks, the initial rush settles down. The real test is whether customers keep coming back regularly.

Similarly, the first-day price of an IPO is often influenced by short-term trading and initial excitement. Share prices are subject to "volatility" (they can go up and down quite a bit) as the market tries to figure out the company's true worth. The "broader market" (the overall stock market) can also affect individual stock prices. A stock might list flat (close near its IPO price) but then climb significantly over the next few weeks as more investors discover its potential, or vice-versa. The long-term performance is what truly matters for an investor.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a school fete where a new stall opens selling unique handmade crafts. On opening morning, everyone rushes to buy, and the prices might even go up due to high demand. But by afternoon, the initial rush is over. The price then settles based on how much people genuinely value the crafts, not just the excitement of a new stall. The 30-to-60-day mark for an IPO is like checking the craft stall's sales a few weeks later – it tells you what people are willing to pay when the initial buzz has worn off.

## Things people get confused about

### Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.
**Fact:** A strong listing gain primarily reflects high initial demand and market excitement, not necessarily the company's long-term business strength or future growth potential.

### Myth → Fact: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment forever.
**Fact:** Listing below the issue price means initial market sentiment was not strong. However, a company's performance can change over time based on its business results, industry trends, and overall market conditions.

### Myth → Fact: The IPO price is the "right" price for the company's shares.
**Fact:** The IPO price is the price at which the company offers its shares to the public. Once listed, the market (buyers and sellers) determines the ongoing price, which can be higher or lower than the IPO price.

## FAQ

### What is an IPO?
An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary people to buy a part of the company.

### What is an SME IPO?
An SME IPO is an IPO by a Small and Medium Enterprise (SME) that lists on a separate platform of the exchange (like NSE Emerge or BSE SME) with different rules compared to mainboard IPOs.

### What is "issue price"?
The "issue price" is the fixed price per share at which a company offers its shares to investors during an IPO.

### What is "listing price"?
The "listing price" is the price at which a company's shares start trading on the stock exchange on its first day.

### What does "return vs. issue price" mean?
This figure tells you the percentage difference between the current market price of a share and its original IPO (issue) price. A positive percentage means the share is trading above its IPO price, while a negative percentage means it's below.
