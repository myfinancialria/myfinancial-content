## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public through an Initial Public Offering (IPO), there's often a lot of excitement. On the "listing day" (the first day its shares trade on the stock exchange), prices can sometimes "pop" significantly, leading to a "listing gain" (the percentage difference between the IPO issue price and the price at which the shares open for trading).

However, this initial frenzy can be short-lived. Many factors, like speculative trading and the "lock-in" period for anchor investors (large institutional investors who buy shares before the main IPO and cannot sell them for a certain period, usually 30 or 90 days), can influence the listing day price.

Looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after it lists gives us a calmer, more settled view. By this time, the initial excitement has usually faded, and for many IPOs, the 30-day anchor investor lock-in period might have ended. This means the current price often reflects a more natural supply and demand balance, giving a better sense of how the market is valuing the company after the initial buzz.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's performance on its listing day, whether it "pops" (opens higher than its issue price) or "drops" (opens lower), is influenced by several factors:

*   **Demand vs. Supply:** If there's very high "demand" (many investors want to buy shares) compared to the "supply" (the number of shares the company is offering), the price tends to go up. If demand is low, prices might drop.
*   **Subscription Levels:** This refers to how many times the IPO shares were applied for. A highly "oversubscribed" IPO (e.g., applied for 10 times over) usually indicates strong investor interest, which can lead to a listing gain.
*   **Market Mood:** The overall sentiment of the broader stock market plays a big role. If the general market is doing well, new listings often get a boost. A weak market can dampen enthusiasm for IPOs.
*   **Pricing:** If the company prices its IPO shares at a level that investors feel is fair or even a bit undervalued, it can attract more buyers and lead to a pop. If the price is seen as too high, it might deter investors.
*   **Lock-in for Anchor Investors:** "Anchor investors" are large institutional investors who commit to buying a significant portion of shares before the main IPO opens. They typically have a "lock-in period" (often 30 days) during which they cannot sell their shares. This reduces the immediate supply of shares in the market, which can help support the price on listing day.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

While a big listing gain can be exciting, the first-day price is rarely the full picture of a company's long-term value.

*   **Volatility:** The first day of trading can be very "volatile" (prices can swing up and down sharply) due to speculative trading and short-term interest. These movements might not reflect the company's fundamental strength.
*   **Broader Market Influence:** A company might list on a day when the entire market is performing exceptionally well or poorly. This "broader market" movement can overshadow the individual company's performance.
*   **Long-term Value:** A company's true value and growth potential often take time to become evident. A stock might list flat or even drop initially, only to perform well over months or years as the company executes its business plans. Conversely, a stock with a huge listing pop might not sustain that growth.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a new sweet shop opening in your neighbourhood. On the first day, everyone rushes to try it out – there's a huge crowd, and the sweets might even sell out quickly at a slightly higher price due to the novelty and excitement. This is like the IPO listing day frenzy.

But after a month or two, the initial buzz fades. People now buy sweets from that shop based on how good they actually taste, the quality of ingredients, the service, and whether the prices are reasonable for the long term. The shop's daily sales and reputation settle into a more stable pattern. Similarly, an IPO's price after 30-60 days often reflects the market's more considered opinion of the company's value, rather than just the opening day excitement.

## Things people get confused about

*   **Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.**
    *   **Fact:** A big listing gain is a short-term market reaction, often driven by demand and sentiment. It does not guarantee the company's long-term success or future stock performance.
*   **Myth → Fact: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment.**
    *   **Fact:** An initial drop can happen due to various market conditions or pricing. It doesn't automatically mean the company is bad; its long-term prospects might still be strong, or it could recover later.
*   **Myth → Fact: You must apply for every IPO to make money.**
    *   **Fact:** Not all IPOs perform well after listing. It's important to research each company individually and understand its business before deciding to apply, rather than blindly participating in every offering.

## FAQ

### What is an IPO?
An IPO, or Initial Public Offering, is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise capital, allowing ordinary investors to buy a piece of the company.

### What is an issue price?
The "issue price" is the fixed price at which a company offers its shares to investors during its IPO.

### What is a listing gain?
A "listing gain" is the percentage increase from the IPO issue price to the price at which the shares open for trading on the stock exchange on their first day.

### What does "return vs. IPO price" mean?
This metric shows how much the current share price has changed, in percentage terms, compared to the original IPO issue price. It tells you the overall gain or loss from the IPO price to now.

### What is an anchor investor lock-in?
An "anchor investor lock-in" is a period (often 30 or 90 days) during which large institutional investors, who bought shares before the main IPO, are restricted from selling their shares in the open market.
