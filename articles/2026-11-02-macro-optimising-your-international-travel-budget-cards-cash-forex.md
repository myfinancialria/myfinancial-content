# Optimising Your International Travel Budget: Cards, Cash & Forex

**TL;DR**
-   For international holidays, strategically combine forex cards, zero-markup credit cards, and a small amount of local cash to minimise costs.
-   Forex cards are excellent for pre-loading major currencies, locking in exchange rates, and avoiding high conversion fees.
-   Zero-markup credit cards offer convenience and interbank exchange rates for most purchases, significantly cutting down on foreign transaction fees.
-   Always carry some local currency cash for small, everyday expenses, tips, and places where digital payments are not accepted.
-   Be aware of the updated Tax Collected at Source (TCS) rules, effective April 1, 2026, impacting overseas remittances and tour packages.

## What this means in plain terms

Planning an international holiday involves more than just booking flights and hotels; how you manage your money abroad can significantly impact your overall expenses. Many Indian travellers inadvertently incur extra costs through unfavourable exchange rates, hidden bank charges, and hefty foreign transaction fees. This article will guide you through intelligently splitting your travel budget across different payment methods – cash, forex cards, and credit cards – to ensure you get the most value for your rupees. We'll also break down the recent changes in Tax Collected at Source (TCS) regulations that directly affect your overseas spending, helping you navigate these financial aspects calmly and practically.

## Forex Cards: Your Rate Lock Companion

Forex cards, also known as multi-currency travel cards, are prepaid instruments designed specifically for international use. You load them with Indian Rupees (INR), which can then be converted and held in one or multiple foreign currencies (like USD, EUR, GBP) on the card's balance. This feature is a significant advantage as it allows you to lock in an exchange rate at the time of loading, protecting you from potential currency fluctuations that might occur during your trip.

**Benefits of using a forex card:**
*   **Rate Locking:** The primary benefit is securing an exchange rate beforehand. If the rupee weakens against your destination currency, you're insulated from the adverse impact.
*   **Lower Fees:** Forex cards typically offer more competitive exchange rates compared to standard credit/debit cards and often have lower or zero foreign transaction markups when you spend in the loaded currency.
*   **Security:** Your forex card is separate from your primary bank account, limiting your exposure in case of theft or fraud. Most cards come with PIN protection and can be easily blocked or reloaded if needed.
*   **Budget Control:** Since it's a prepaid card, you can only spend the amount loaded, helping you stick to your travel budget.

**Considerations:**
*   **Issuance and Reload Fees:** Some banks may charge a fee for issuing the card or for subsequent reloads.
*   **Cross-Currency Charges:** If you load USD but spend in EUR, a cross-currency conversion fee might apply, negating some of the savings. It's best to load the currency of your primary destination.
*   **Unused Balance:** Converting any leftover foreign currency back to INR might incur a fee, or you might get a less favourable exchange rate.

**TCS on Forex Card Loading:**
When you load a forex card, it falls under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). Effective April 1, 2026, for remittances *other than* overseas tour packages, there will be no Tax Collected at Source (TCS) on amounts up to ₹10 lakh per financial year. A TCS of 20% will be applicable on amounts exceeding this ₹10 lakh threshold. This TCS is an advance tax and can be adjusted against your income tax liability or claimed as a refund when filing your Income Tax Return (ITR).

## Credit Cards Abroad: Convenience with Caveats

Credit cards offer unparalleled convenience and are essential for booking accommodations, rental cars, and larger purchases. They also provide a safety net for emergencies and often come with benefits like travel insurance or reward points. However, using standard Indian credit cards abroad can be surprisingly expensive due to various fees.

**Foreign Exchange Markup:**
Most standard Indian credit cards charge a foreign exchange (forex) markup fee, typically ranging from 2% to 3.5% on the total transaction value. With an additional 18% Goods and Services Tax (GST) on this markup, the effective cost can climb to around 2.36% to 4.13% of your spending. This can quickly add up, especially for significant expenses.

**Zero-Markup Credit Cards:**
In recent years, several Indian banks and fintech companies have introduced specialised travel credit cards with zero foreign transaction markup. These cards convert currency at or very close to the interbank exchange rate, making them a cost-effective option for international spending. Examples include cards from IDFC FIRST Bank (which announced 0% forex markup across its credit cards from September 2026), Scapia, and OneCard. If you travel frequently, investing in such a card can lead to substantial savings.

**Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC): The Hidden Trap:**
When paying by card abroad, you might be asked if you want to pay in the local currency (e.g., Euros in Europe) or in Indian Rupees (INR). Always choose to pay in the **local currency**. If you opt for INR, the merchant or their payment processor will use their own exchange rate, which is often less favourable than your bank's rate, leading to extra charges. This practice is known as Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC).

**Cash Advances:**
Using your credit card to withdraw cash from an ATM abroad is generally discouraged. Cash advances typically incur an immediate cash advance fee (a percentage of the withdrawn amount), high interest rates from the day of withdrawal, and potentially the standard forex markup. It's an expensive way to get cash and should be reserved for absolute emergencies.

**TCS on Credit Card Transactions:**
While for a long time, credit card transactions were largely outside the direct purview of the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) for TCS purposes, the landscape is evolving. As per clarifications, aggregate foreign cash withdrawals through ATM, POS, or e-commerce transactions can fall under LRS. Therefore, if your total overseas remittances (including forex card loads and direct transfers) for "other purposes" exceed ₹10 lakh in a financial year, a 20% TCS will apply to the amount above this threshold, effective April 1, 2026. International credit card spending abroad is currently deferred from LRS inclusion and does not attract TCS.

## The Essential Role of Cash

Despite the rise of digital payments, cash remains an indispensable part of international travel. There are many scenarios where cards are not accepted, or cash offers greater convenience.

**When Cash is Indispensable:**
*   **Small Vendors and Local Markets:** Many street vendors, small shops, and local markets, especially in less touristy areas, operate primarily on cash.
*   **Local Transport:** Auto-rickshaws, local buses, and some taxis may only accept cash.
*   **Tips and Gratuities:** Cash is the easiest way to tip service staff, porters, and tour guides.
*   **Emergencies:** Having some local currency cash provides a crucial backup if your cards are lost, stolen, or temporarily blocked.

**How Much Cash to Carry:**
The ideal amount of cash depends on your destination, duration of stay, and spending habits. A good rule of thumb is to carry enough local currency for your immediate needs upon arrival (e.g., airport transfer, first meal) and for daily small expenses. Avoid carrying large wads of cash due to security risks. A mix of small and medium denominations is always useful.

**Where to Exchange Currency:**
*   **Avoid Airports for Bulk Exchange:** Airport currency exchange counters are convenient but often offer less favourable exchange rates due to higher operational costs. Exchange only a small amount for immediate needs.
*   **Banks and Authorised Money Changers:** For larger amounts, exchange currency at banks or authorised money changers in major cities, which generally offer better rates. Compare rates before committing.
*   **ATM Withdrawals:** Using a zero-forex debit card or a forex card to withdraw cash from ATMs (where available and safe) can offer competitive rates, though be mindful of ATM fees charged by the local bank and your Indian bank.

**Security:**
Always be mindful of where you keep your cash. Distribute it across different secure locations (e.g., money belt, separate pockets, hotel safe) and avoid flaunting large amounts.

## Navigating Tax Collected at Source (TCS) for Overseas Spending

The Indian government, through the Income-tax Act, 1961 (and subsequently section 394 of the Income-tax Act, 2025), mandates Tax Collected at Source (TCS) on certain foreign remittances made under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI). The LRS allows resident individuals to remit up to USD 250,000 (approximately ₹2 crore) per financial year (April to March) for various permissible current or capital account transactions, including travel, education, medical treatment, and investments.

It is crucial to understand the updated TCS rules, particularly those effective from April 1, 2026, as per the Union Budget 2026:

**1. Overseas Tour Program Packages:**
*   For payments made for overseas tour packages, a flat 2% TCS will be applicable from the first rupee, with no threshold.
*   This is a significant reduction from previous rates and simplifies the calculation for travellers booking bundled tour packages.

**2. Other Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) Purposes (excluding tour packages, education, and medical treatment):**
*   This category includes loading money onto forex cards, direct bank transfers for travel expenses (like accommodation not part of a package), and debit card spends for general purchases if they fall under LRS. International credit card spending abroad is currently deferred from LRS inclusion and does not attract TCS.
*   For these remittances, there will be **no TCS up to an aggregate amount of ₹10 lakh** per financial year.
*   A **20% TCS** will be collected on any amount exceeding this ₹10 lakh threshold in a financial year.
*   The ₹10 lakh threshold is cumulative across all your LRS remittances (other than tour packages, education, and medical) per PAN within a financial year.

**Important Note on TCS:**
TCS is **not an additional tax** on your income. It is an advance tax that is collected by the authorised dealer (bank or money changer) at the time of remittance. This amount will reflect in your Form 26AS or Annual Information Statement (AIS) and can be adjusted against your overall income tax liability when you file your Income Tax Return. If your total tax liability is less than the TCS collected, you can claim a refund for the excess amount.

## A real example

Let's consider **Meera, 35, ₹25L CTC, Hyderabad**, planning a 10-day trip to Thailand with her husband in May 2027. Their estimated total expenses are ₹3.50 lakh.

Here’s how they could optimally split their budget:

1.  **Pre-trip Booking (Flights, Accommodation, some activities):** ₹1.50 lakh
    *   Meera booked flights and a significant portion of their accommodation online using her **zero-markup credit card**. This ensures she gets the interbank exchange rate without additional forex markup.
    *   **TCS Impact:** Since this is a direct payment not part of a "tour package" and her aggregate LRS spending (including this) is below ₹10 lakh for the financial year (April 2027 - March 2028), no TCS is applicable on this amount.

2.  **Forex Card Loading for Major On-ground Expenses:** ₹1.20 lakh
    *   Meera decides to load a multi-currency forex card with Thai Baht (THB) equivalent to ₹1.20 lakh for planned shopping, dining at mid-range restaurants, and larger activity bookings in Thailand. She locks in a favourable exchange rate a few weeks before her trip.
    *   **TCS Impact:** This ₹1.20 lakh is for "other LRS purposes." Since it's well below the ₹10 lakh threshold for the financial year, no TCS is collected on this amount.

3.  **Cash for Daily Expenses & Emergencies:** ₹80,000
    *   Meera exchanges ₹20,000 equivalent of THB at a local authorised money changer in Hyderabad before leaving for immediate needs upon arrival.
    *   She plans to withdraw the remaining ₹60,000 equivalent in smaller tranches from ATMs in Thailand using her forex card, ensuring she doesn't carry too much cash at once.
    *   **TCS Impact:** The initial ₹20,000 exchange is part of her LRS limit. The ATM withdrawals via forex card also fall under LRS. Combined with her forex card loading, her total "other LRS purposes" spending is ₹1.20 lakh + ₹0.80 lakh = ₹2.00 lakh. This is below the ₹10 lakh threshold, so no TCS is applicable.

**Summary of Meera's Spending & TCS:**
*   Credit Card (zero-markup): ₹1.50 lakh (No TCS, as international credit card spending is currently deferred from LRS inclusion)
*   Forex Card (loaded): ₹1.20 lakh (No TCS, as it's below ₹10 lakh LRS threshold)
*   Cash (exchanged/withdrawn): ₹0.80 lakh (No TCS, as it's below ₹10 lakh LRS threshold)
*   **Total Trip Cost: ₹3.50 lakh**
*   **Total TCS paid: ₹0**

By strategically using a zero-markup credit card for large bookings and a forex card for on-ground expenses, and keeping her total LRS remittances for "other purposes" below ₹10 lakh, Meera avoids paying any TCS upfront and minimises foreign transaction fees. She carries just enough cash for practical daily needs.

## What to do this week

1.  **Review Your Credit Cards:** Check your existing credit cards for their foreign transaction markup fees. If they're high (e.g., 3.5% + GST), consider applying for a zero-markup travel credit card well in advance of your trip.
2.  **Research Forex Cards:** Compare forex card options from different banks (e.g., HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank) or authorised dealers. Look at issuance fees, reload charges, and the exchange rates offered for your destination currency.
3.  **Estimate Cash Needs:** Based on your destination and planned activities, estimate how much local currency cash you'll need for small purchases, transport, and tips. Plan to exchange a small amount before departure and use ATMs for larger withdrawals if needed.
4.  **Understand TCS Rules:** Familiarise yourself with the updated TCS rules (effective April 1, 2026) for overseas tour packages (flat 2%) and other LRS remittances (0% up to ₹10 lakh, then 20% above). Keep track of your aggregate LRS spending if it's nearing the ₹10 lakh threshold.
5.  **Inform Your Bank:** Before travelling, notify your credit and debit card issuers about your travel dates and destinations to prevent your transactions from being flagged as fraudulent and your cards being blocked.

## FAQ

### What is the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS)?
The LRS is an RBI framework allowing resident individuals to remit up to USD 250,000 per financial year (April-March) for various permissible current and capital account transactions, including travel, education, medical treatment, and investments.

### How does TCS on foreign travel work from April 1, 2026?
From April 1, 2026, overseas tour packages attract a flat 2% TCS from the first rupee. For other LRS remittances (like forex card loading, direct travel expenses), there is no TCS up to ₹10 lakh per financial year; a 20% TCS applies above this threshold.

### Is TCS an extra tax I have to pay?
No, TCS is not an extra tax. It is an advance tax collected by the authorised dealer. This amount is adjusted against your income tax liability when you file your Income Tax Return or can be claimed as a refund if there's an excess.

### Should I always pay in local currency when using my card abroad?
Yes, always choose to pay in the local currency of the country you are visiting. Opting to pay in Indian Rupees (INR) via Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC) often results in less favourable exchange rates and additional markups from the merchant or their payment processor.

### Are zero-markup credit cards truly free of all foreign transaction costs?
Zero-markup credit cards typically waive the bank's foreign transaction fee, converting at interbank rates. However, you might still encounter local ATM fees for cash withdrawals or charges for specific cross-currency transactions depending on the card's terms.

### How much cash should I carry for an international trip?
It's advisable to carry a small amount of local currency cash for immediate expenses upon arrival (e.g., airport transfer, first meal) and for daily small purchases, tips, and places where cards aren't accepted. Avoid carrying large sums due to security risks.
