## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offer (IPO). The first day these shares are traded on the stock exchange is known as the **listing day**. Often, there's a lot of excitement, and the share price might jump significantly, leading to a **listing gain** (the percentage increase from the IPO price to the closing price on the first trading day).

However, this initial excitement can be short-lived. Many factors, like strong demand from investors or market sentiment, can push prices up on day one. But what happens a month or two later? By looking at how shares are performing 30 to 60 days after listing, we get a calmer, more realistic picture. The initial buzz usually fades, and sometimes the shares bought by large investors (called **anchor investors**) have a lock-in period (often 30 days), meaning they can't sell their shares immediately. Once this period passes, the market gets a better sense of the actual demand and supply for the stock. This window gives us a more stable view, beyond the listing day frenzy.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

Imagine a popular sweet shop announcing that it will sell a limited number of special Diwali sweets at a fixed price. If many people want these sweets, but there aren't enough for everyone, the shop might sell them for a higher price on the open market later. This is similar to how IPOs work.

An IPO's price movement on listing day is mainly driven by **demand vs. supply**.
*   **Demand:** If many investors want to buy shares of a particular company, the IPO is said to have high **subscription levels**. This high demand, coupled with limited shares (supply), can push the price up when trading begins.
*   **Supply:** The number of shares the company offers to the public is fixed. If this supply is low compared to demand, prices can rise.
*   **Market Mood:** The overall sentiment of the stock market also plays a big role. If the broader market is doing well, new listings often get a warmer reception.
*   **Pricing:** The company and its merchant bankers decide the **issue price** (the price at which shares are offered in the IPO). If this price is seen as reasonable or even a little low compared to what investors believe the company is worth, demand can be high.
*   **Lock-in and Anchor Investors:** Some large investors, called **anchor investors**, buy a portion of the IPO shares before the public offering. Their shares often have a **lock-in period** (e.g., 30 or 90 days) during which they cannot sell. This can temporarily reduce the supply of shares in the market.

A "pop" on listing day happens when demand is much higher than supply, and investors are willing to pay more than the IPO price. A "drop" happens if demand is low, or if many investors who got shares want to sell them quickly, increasing supply and pushing prices down.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot of the market's initial reaction. Think of it like a grand opening sale for a new shop. There might be a huge crowd and excitement on day one, leading to big sales. But the real test is whether customers keep coming back weeks and months later.

Stock prices are subject to **volatility** (they can move up and down quickly) due to many factors. The **broader market** performance, news about the company, its financial results, industry trends, and even global events can all influence a stock's price long after its listing day.

A stock might list flat (meaning its price doesn't change much from the IPO price) but then perform very well over the next few months as the company shows good results. Conversely, a stock might have a huge listing gain but then see its price drop significantly as the initial excitement fades or new information comes to light. The initial price is just the starting point; the journey of the stock price continues every trading day.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a new restaurant opening in your neighbourhood. On day one, there might be a long queue because of curiosity, heavy advertising, or special introductory offers. Everyone wants to try it. This is like the listing day frenzy.

But after a month or two, the initial hype dies down. The real test is whether the food is good, the service is consistent, and people genuinely enjoy it enough to become regular customers. The price the market settles on after the excitement fades is like the restaurant's steady customer base and reputation – it reflects its true perceived value over time, not just the initial buzz.

## Things people get confused about

*   **Myth:** A big listing gain means it's a great company.
    *   **Fact:** A big listing gain only reflects high initial demand for the shares, not necessarily the company's long-term business strength or future performance.
*   **Myth:** If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment forever.
    *   **Fact:** While a listing loss can be disappointing, many companies that listed flat or below their IPO price have gone on to perform well over the long term, and vice-versa. Long-term company fundamentals matter more.
*   **Myth:** The IPO price is the "right" or "fair" price for the company.
    *   **Fact:** The IPO price is determined by the company and its bankers to attract investors. Once listed, the market (buyers and sellers) decides the share's value every day based on new information, company performance, and overall market conditions.

## FAQ

### What is an IPO?
An IPO (Initial Public Offer) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary people to buy a part of the company.

### What is issue price?
The issue price is the fixed price per share at which a company sells its shares to investors during its IPO.

### What is current price?
The current price is the latest trading price of a company's share on the stock exchange, reflecting what buyers and sellers are agreeing upon right now.

### What does "return vs issue price" mean?
This percentage shows how much the current market price of a share has changed compared to its original IPO issue price. A positive percentage means the price has increased, and a negative percentage means it has decreased.

### What is an SME IPO?
An SME IPO is an IPO by a Small and Medium Enterprise (SME) that lists on a dedicated platform for SMEs on exchanges like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, typically with smaller issue sizes and different listing requirements than mainboard IPOs.
