# Mutual Fund Tax: How Portfolio Mix Changes Your Capital Gains Bill

**TL;DR**
- The capital gains tax on your mutual fund investments depends on the fund's underlying portfolio, not just its category.
- Funds holding over 65% in Indian equities are treated as equity-oriented for tax purposes.
- Funds holding less than 65% in Indian equities are considered non-equity oriented for tax, regardless of their category.
- This classification dictates the applicable holding period (12 or 36 months) for long-term capital gains and the tax rates.
- Always check the fund's portfolio disclosure or factsheet to confirm its tax classification before investing or redeeming.

## What this means in plain terms

When you invest in mutual funds and eventually sell your units, any profit you make is subject to capital gains tax. Many investors assume that a fund's official category – like "hybrid fund" or "multi-asset fund" – directly tells them how it will be taxed. However, this isn't always true. The Income Tax Department has its own rules for classifying mutual funds for tax purposes, which primarily depend on how much of the fund's assets are invested in Indian equities.

Essentially, if a fund invests a significant portion (more than 65%) of its corpus in shares of Indian companies, it's treated as an 'equity-oriented' fund for tax. If it invests less than 65% in Indian equities, it's generally considered 'non-equity oriented'. This distinction is crucial because it directly impacts how long you need to hold the investment to qualify for lower long-term capital gains tax, and what tax rate applies. So, a fund that might be called a 'hybrid' fund by SEBI could be taxed like a 'debt' fund if its equity allocation falls below the 65% threshold.

## Understanding Equity-Oriented vs. Non-Equity Oriented Funds

The Income Tax Act, 1961, categorises mutual funds into two broad types for capital gains tax purposes: equity-oriented funds and non-equity oriented funds. This classification is critical because it determines the holding period required for an investment to be considered 'long-term' and the tax rates applicable to both short-term and long-term gains.

### Defining the Tax Classification Threshold

A mutual fund is considered an "equity-oriented mutual fund" if at least 65% of its investible funds are invested in equity shares of domestic companies. If the equity allocation falls below this 65% threshold, the fund is generally treated as a "non-equity oriented mutual fund" for tax purposes. This distinction is irrespective of the fund's official category as defined by SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India), such as balanced funds, multi-asset funds, or even certain aggressive hybrid funds, which might have varying equity exposures.

### Impact on Capital Gains Treatment

The tax classification directly influences:
*   **Holding Period:** The duration for which you must hold the fund units to qualify for long-term capital gains (LTCG).
*   **Tax Rates:** The specific percentage of tax applied to your capital gains, which differs for short-term and long-term gains.

It's vital for investors to understand that a fund house might classify a scheme as, say, a "conservative hybrid fund" due to its investment mandate, but if its actual equity exposure consistently stays below 65%, it will be taxed as a non-equity oriented fund.

## Capital Gains Tax Rules: Holding Periods and Rates

The tax treatment of capital gains from mutual funds varies significantly based on whether the fund is classified as equity-oriented or non-equity oriented, and the holding period of the units.

### For Equity-Oriented Mutual Funds

*   **Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG):** If you sell units after holding them for **12 months or less**, the gains are considered STCG. These gains are taxed at a flat rate of 15% (plus applicable surcharge and cess).
*   **Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG):** If you sell units after holding them for **more than 12 months**, the gains are considered LTCG. LTCG up to ₹1 lakh in a financial year is exempt from tax. LTCG exceeding ₹1 lakh in a financial year is taxed at a rate of 10% (plus applicable surcharge and cess), without indexation benefit.

### For Non-Equity Oriented Mutual Funds

*   **Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG):** If you sell units after holding them for **36 months or less**, the gains are considered STCG. These gains are added to your total income and taxed according to your applicable income tax slab rate.
*   **Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG):** If you sell units after holding them for **more than 36 months**, the gains are considered LTCG. These gains are taxed at a flat rate of 20% (plus applicable surcharge and cess), after providing the benefit of indexation. Indexation adjusts the purchase cost for inflation, thereby reducing the taxable gain.

## Impact of Recent Tax Changes on Debt Funds

A significant change was introduced by the Finance Act 2023 concerning the taxation of mutual funds that invest predominantly in debt. Previously, debt-oriented mutual funds enjoyed indexation benefits if held for more than 36 months, with gains taxed at 20%.

However, with effect from April 1, 2023, investments made in mutual funds where less than 35% of the corpus is invested in equity shares of domestic companies are now treated differently. Any capital gains arising from such funds, irrespective of the holding period, are now considered **short-term capital gains**. These gains are added to the investor's total income and taxed at their applicable income tax slab rate.

This change effectively removed the long-term capital gains benefit and indexation for debt funds and funds with very low equity exposure. It means that for such funds, the holding period no longer differentiates between short-term and long-term gains for tax purposes, and all gains are taxed at the marginal income tax rate. This makes it even more crucial for investors to understand the underlying portfolio allocation for tax classification. Funds that maintain an equity allocation above 35% but below 65% (e.g., some hybrid funds) will still fall under the "non-equity oriented" classification with the 36-month holding period for LTCG and indexation benefit.

## A real example

Let's consider two investors, Priya and Sameer, both 35 years old, earning ₹25 lakhs CTC, residing in Mumbai. They both invested ₹5 lakhs each in different "hybrid" mutual funds on April 1, 2023, and redeemed them on April 1, 2026, making a gain of ₹1 lakh each.

**Priya's Investment: "Aggressive Growth Hybrid Fund"**
*   This fund consistently maintained **70% equity exposure** to Indian equities and 30% debt.
*   For tax purposes, it is classified as an **equity-oriented fund**.
*   **Holding Period:** 36 months (more than 12 months).
*   **Capital Gain:** ₹1,00,000.
*   **Tax Calculation:** Since the holding period is more than 12 months, it's LTCG. As the LTCG is exactly ₹1 lakh, it falls within the annual exemption limit for equity-oriented funds.
*   **Tax Payable:** ₹0.

**Sameer's Investment: "Balanced Income Hybrid Fund"**
*   This fund consistently maintained **50% equity exposure** to Indian equities and 50% debt.
*   For tax purposes, it is classified as a **non-equity oriented fund** (as equity exposure is less than 65% but more than 35%).
*   **Holding Period:** 36 months (more than 36 months).
*   **Capital Gain:** ₹1,00,000.
*   **Tax Calculation:** Since the holding period is more than 36 months, it's LTCG. Sameer will get the benefit of indexation.
    *   Let's assume the Cost Inflation Index (CII) for FY 2023-24 was 348 and for FY 2025-26 (redemption year) was 370.
    *   Indexed Cost of Acquisition = Original Cost * (CII of redemption year / CII of acquisition year)
    *   Indexed Cost = ₹5,00,000 * (370 / 348) = ₹5,31,609 (approx)
    *   Long-Term Capital Gain = Sale Price - Indexed Cost = ₹6,00,000 - ₹5,31,609 = ₹68,391
    *   Tax @ 20% on LTCG = 20% of ₹68,391 = ₹13,678.20
*   **Tax Payable:** ₹13,678 (approx, plus cess).

This example clearly shows how two funds, both categorised as 'hybrid', can have vastly different tax implications based purely on their underlying equity allocation.

## FAQ

### How can I check a mutual fund's tax classification?
You can check the fund's monthly factsheet, scheme information document (SID), or portfolio disclosure on the AMC's website. Look for the percentage of assets invested in Indian equities. AMFI also provides categorisation details.

### Does a fund's category name guarantee its tax treatment?
No, a fund's SEBI-defined category (e.g., "hybrid," "multi-asset") does not guarantee its tax treatment. The actual portfolio allocation, specifically the percentage of Indian equity holdings, determines its tax classification.

### What if a fund's equity allocation changes over time?
If a fund's equity allocation crosses the 65% threshold (either above or below), its tax classification can change. The tax treatment at the time of redemption will depend on its classification during the period of your holding, which can get complex. It's usually based on the average allocation maintained.

### Do international equity funds qualify as equity-oriented for tax?
No, investments in international equities are generally not considered for the 65% Indian equity threshold. Funds investing primarily in foreign equities are typically treated as non-equity oriented funds for tax purposes.

### Is the 35% equity threshold for debt funds still relevant after the Finance Act 2023?
Yes, for funds with less than 35% equity exposure, all gains are now taxed as short-term capital gains at slab rates, irrespective of holding period. Funds with 35-65% equity exposure are still non-equity oriented with 36-month LTCG and indexation.

### Where can I find the official tax rules for mutual funds?
The official tax rules for capital gains from mutual funds are outlined in the Income Tax Act, 1961, and subsequent notifications from the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT). You can find details on the Income Tax India website.
