## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The first day these shares are traded on the stock exchange is known as the "listing day." Often, there's a lot of excitement, and the share price might jump significantly, leading to a "listing gain" (the percentage difference between the IPO price and the closing price on the listing day).

However, this initial excitement can sometimes fade quickly. Many large investors, called "anchor investors," who subscribe to a portion of the IPO, have a "lock-in period" (a time when they cannot sell their shares), which is staggered: 50% of their allotted shares are locked in for 30 days, and the remaining 50% for 90 days from the date of allotment. Once this lock-in period ends, these investors are free to sell, which can sometimes affect the share price.

Looking at an IPO's performance 30 to 60 days after listing gives us a calmer, more settled picture. It shows how the market values the company after the initial buzz has worn off and anchor investors are free to trade. This period offers a more stable view of how the shares are performing compared to their original IPO price.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's listing performance is often a dance between "demand" (how many people want to buy the shares) and "supply" (how many shares are available).

*   **Demand vs. Supply:** If many investors want to buy shares but only a limited number are available, the price tends to go up. This is called "oversubscription." If there are more shares than buyers, the price might drop.
*   **Subscription Levels:** High subscription numbers (e.g., an IPO subscribed 50 times) suggest strong demand, often leading to a "pop" (a significant rise in price) on listing day. Lower subscription might mean a flat or negative listing.
*   **Market Mood:** The overall sentiment of the stock market plays a big role. If the broader market is doing well, new IPOs tend to perform better. A cautious or negative market can dampen enthusiasm.
*   **Pricing:** The company and its bankers decide the "issue price" (the price at which shares are offered in the IPO). If the price is set too high, investors might not see much value, leading to a weak listing. If it's priced attractively, it can create strong demand.
*   **Anchor Investors and Lock-in:** "Anchor investors" are large institutional investors (like mutual funds or insurance companies) who commit to buying a substantial portion of the IPO shares before it opens to the public. They usually have a "lock-in period" (often 30 days) during which they cannot sell their shares. This lock-in can create a temporary scarcity of shares, which might support the price. Once the lock-in ends, if many anchor investors decide to sell, it can increase supply and potentially affect the share price.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot. It shows the market's initial reaction, which can be driven by short-term excitement or speculation.

*   **Volatility:** New stocks can be very "volatile" (their prices can change quickly and sharply) in the first few days or weeks. This means the price can swing up and down quite a bit.
*   **Broader Market Influence:** The overall performance of the stock market can also affect an IPO's price, regardless of the company's individual performance. A general market rally might lift all stocks, including newly listed ones, and a downturn can pull them down.
*   **Long-Term Value:** A company's true value often takes time to be recognised by the market. A stock might list flat (not much change from IPO price) but then grow significantly over months or years as the company performs well. Conversely, a stock with a huge listing gain might struggle later if its business fundamentals don't support the high valuation.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a new shop opening in your neighbourhood. On its grand opening day, there's a lot of buzz, special offers, and a big crowd. The sales are fantastic! But after a month or two, the initial excitement settles down. The shop's true performance depends on its regular customers, quality of products, and how it manages its business day-to-day. Similarly, an IPO's listing day is like the grand opening, full of initial hype. The 30-60 day mark is when the market starts to assess its regular business, beyond the opening day rush.

## Things people get confused about

### Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.
**Fact:** A big listing gain simply means there was high demand for the shares on the first day. It doesn't guarantee the company will perform well in the long run. The company's business performance over time is what truly matters.

### Myth → Fact: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment.
**Fact:** A weak listing means initial market demand was low or the IPO was priced aggressively. It doesn't necessarily mean the company is bad. Sometimes, such stocks can offer value over time if the company's fundamentals are strong.

### Myth → Fact: The IPO price is the "right" price for the company.
**Fact:** The IPO price is a starting point determined by the company and its bankers. The market then discovers the "fair" price through trading activity over time, based on demand, supply, and the company's performance.

## FAQ

### What is an IPO?
An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary investors to buy a part of the company.

### How do I apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility or through your stockbroker's platform, typically using your UPI ID for payment.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE or BSE), while SME (Small and Medium Enterprise) IPOs are for smaller companies that list on dedicated SME platforms of these exchanges, with different rules and regulations.

### What does "issue price" mean?
The "issue price" is the fixed price per share at which a company offers its shares to investors during its IPO.
