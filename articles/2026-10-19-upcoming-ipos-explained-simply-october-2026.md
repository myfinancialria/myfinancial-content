## First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local chai shop that wants to open more branches across the city. To do this, they need a lot of money. One way to get this money is to sell a small part of their ownership to the public. This is what an IPO, or **Initial Public Offering**, is all about.

When a company decides to sell its shares to the public for the very first time, it's called an IPO. This is done in the **primary market** (where new shares are sold directly by the company). Once these shares are sold, they then get **listed** (start trading) on stock exchanges like the NSE or BSE, where people can buy and sell them among themselves.

## How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When an IPO comes along, you'll see a few standard terms and numbers. Understanding them helps you make sense of the offering:

1.  **Price Band:** This is the **price band** (a fixed range of prices within which investors can bid for the shares). For example, if the price band is ₹665 to ₹700, you can choose to bid at any price within this range. The highest price in the band (₹700 in this example) is often called the "cap price" or "upper price band".

2.  **Lot Size:** This is the **lot size** (the minimum number of shares you must apply for). You cannot apply for just one share; you have to apply in multiples of this lot size. So, if the lot size is 20 shares, you must apply for 20, 40, 60 shares, and so on. The exact lot size for the current IPOs is not specified in the provided data.

3.  **Minimum Investment:** This is the **minimum investment** (the smallest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO). You calculate this by multiplying the lot size by the lowest price in the price band. For instance, if the lot size was 20 shares and the lower price band was ₹665, your minimum investment would be ₹665 x 20 = ₹13,300.

4.  **Issue Size:** This is the **issue size** (the total number of shares the company is offering to the public). It tells you how many shares are available for new investors to buy. Sometimes, this is also expressed as the total value in rupees.

5.  **Fresh Issue vs. Offer for Sale (OFS):** An IPO can be made up of two parts:
    *   **Fresh Issue:** This is when the company issues brand new shares. The money raised from these shares goes directly to the company to fund its growth, pay off debt, or for other business needs.
    *   **Offer for Sale (OFS):** This is when existing shareholders (like the company's founders, early investors, or private equity firms) sell some of their shares. The money raised from an OFS goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself. Many IPOs are a mix of both.

6.  **RHP/DRHP:** This stands for **Red Herring Prospectus (RHP)** or **Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP)**. This is the official document that a company files with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) before its IPO. It contains almost all the information you could want about the company – its business, financials, risks, management, how it plans to use the money, and much more. It's like the company's detailed report card and business plan for potential investors.

## The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO involves a few clear stages:

1.  **DRHP Filing:** The company first prepares a detailed **DRHP** (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) and submits it to SEBI for approval. This document outlines all the details of the company and the proposed IPO.
2.  **SEBI Approval:** SEBI reviews the DRHP to ensure all necessary disclosures are made and that the IPO complies with regulations. Once approved, the company can proceed.
3.  **Price Band Announcement:** The company, in consultation with its investment bankers, decides and announces the **price band** (the range within which investors can bid for shares) and the **lot size** (the minimum number of shares to apply for).
4.  **Bidding Period:** The IPO opens for applications, usually for three working days. During this time, investors can place their bids. Investors are typically divided into categories:
    *   **Retail Individual Investors (RIIs):** Individuals applying for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs) or High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs):** Individuals or entities applying for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs):** Large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.
5.  **Allotment:** After the bidding closes, the company and its registrars process the applications. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more demand than shares available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
6.  **Listing:** Once shares are allotted, they are **listed** (start trading) on the stock exchanges (NSE and BSE). This is when you can buy or sell these shares in the open market.

## A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a school fete stall that needs money to buy new sports equipment. The school decides to sell "ownership tickets" for the stall. Each ticket represents a tiny piece of the stall. When you buy these tickets, you're giving money to the school for the new equipment, and in return, you become a part-owner of the stall. If the stall does well, your tickets might become more valuable. An IPO is similar: a company sells parts of itself (shares) to the public to raise money for its future plans, and those who buy shares become part-owners.

## Things people get confused about

Here are a few common misunderstandings about IPOs:

*   **Myth:** Listing gain is guaranteed.
    *   **Fact:** While some IPOs see their share prices rise on the first day of trading (known as a "listing gain"), this is not guaranteed. Share prices can also fall below the IPO price, depending on market conditions and company performance.
*   **Myth:** You must apply for many lots to get shares.
    *   **Fact:** For retail investors, if an IPO is oversubscribed, shares are typically allotted through a lottery system. Applying for just one lot gives you the same chance in the lottery as applying for multiple lots (within the retail limit).
*   **Myth:** IPOs are only for rich people.
    *   **Fact:** IPOs are open to everyone. Retail investors can apply for shares with amounts as low as ₹10,000-₹15,000 (depending on the lot size and price band), making them accessible to a wide range of individuals.

## FAQ

### How do I even apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility via net banking, or through your stockbroker's online platform.

### What is a lot size?
The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot bid for fewer than this number of shares.

### What is a price band?
The price band is the fixed range (e.g., ₹665 to ₹700) within which you can place your bid for the IPO shares.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE), while SME (Small and Medium Enterprise) IPOs are for smaller companies listing on dedicated SME platforms of the exchanges, with different regulatory requirements.

### Why do companies do IPOs?
Companies conduct IPOs primarily to raise capital for growth, expansion, debt repayment, or to allow existing shareholders to sell their holdings and exit their investment.
