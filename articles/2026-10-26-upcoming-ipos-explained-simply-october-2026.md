## First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local shop that has been doing very well. Now, the owners want to expand, maybe open new branches or buy new equipment. To raise the money for this expansion, they decide to sell a small part of their shop's ownership to the public. This act of a private company selling its shares for the first time to the public is called an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)**.

When a company offers its shares through an IPO, it's happening in the **primary market** (where new shares are issued). Once the IPO process is complete and the shares are bought by the public, they get **listed** – meaning they start trading on a stock exchange like the NSE or BSE. After listing, you can buy or sell these shares from other investors in the secondary market.

## How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When an IPO is announced, you'll see several key numbers and terms. Understanding them helps you make sense of the offering:

1.  **Price Band:** This is the range of prices within which investors can bid for the shares. For example, if a price band is ₹100-₹105, you can bid at any price within this range. The lowest price is called the "floor price," and the highest is the "cap price."
2.  **Lot Size:** Companies set a minimum number of shares you must apply for. This minimum is called a "lot." You cannot apply for fewer shares than one lot. You must apply for one lot or multiples of it.
3.  **Minimum Investment:** This is the smallest amount of money you need to invest in an IPO. It's calculated by multiplying the lot size by the upper end of the price band. For example, if the lot size is 100 shares and the upper price band is ₹105, the minimum investment would be ₹10,500.
4.  **Issue Size:** This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public through the IPO. It tells you how many new shares are being made available.
5.  **Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS):**
    *   **Fresh Issue:** When a company issues new shares, and the money raised goes directly to the company. This money is typically used for expansion, debt repayment, or other business needs.
    *   **Offer-for-Sale (OFS):** When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares. In this case, the money raised goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself. An IPO can be a combination of both.
6.  **RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus):** This is the official, detailed document filed with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India). It contains comprehensive information about the company, its business, financials, risks, management, and the IPO details. It's like the company's full report card and business plan for potential investors.

## The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO involves several stages:

1.  **DRHP Filing:** The company first files a **Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP)** with SEBI. This document contains all the necessary information about the company and its planned IPO.
2.  **SEBI Approval:** SEBI reviews the DRHP. Once satisfied, it gives its approval, allowing the company to proceed with the IPO.
3.  **Price Band & Dates Announced:** The company, along with its merchant bankers, announces the **price band** (the bidding range) and the **open and close dates** for the IPO.
4.  **Bidding Period:** This is when you, as an investor, can apply for shares. There are different categories of investors:
    *   **Retail Individual Investors (RIIs):** Ordinary individual investors who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs) / Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs):** Individuals or entities applying for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs):** Large institutional investors like mutual funds, foreign institutional investors, and banks.
5.  **Allotment:** After the bidding closes, the company determines the final offer price (within the price band) and allocates shares to successful bidders. If the IPO is oversubscribed (more demand than shares available), shares are typically allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
6.  **Listing:** Once the shares are allotted, they are listed on the stock exchanges (NSE and/or BSE). This is when they start trading publicly, and you can buy or sell them like any other stock.

## A simple way to think about it

Think of a school fete where the organisers want to raise money for new sports equipment. They decide to sell "ownership tickets" for a small part of the fete itself. Each ticket represents a tiny share. When they first offer these tickets to parents and students, that's like an IPO. The money collected from selling these tickets goes to the fete (the company) to buy the new equipment. Once all tickets are sold, they can then be traded among parents and students who want to buy or sell their "ownership tickets" – that's like the shares listing and trading on the stock market.

## Things people get confused about

Here are a few common misconceptions about IPOs:

*   **Myth:** Listing gain is guaranteed in every IPO.
*   **Fact:** While some IPOs see their share prices rise on the first day of listing (a "listing gain"), it is by no means guaranteed. Share prices can also fall below the issue price, leading to a "listing loss." The market decides the price.

*   **Myth:** Applying for more lots automatically guarantees you more shares.
*   **Fact:** For retail investors, if an IPO is oversubscribed, the allotment is often done through a lottery system to ensure fair distribution. Applying for more than one lot might increase your chances slightly in some categories, but it doesn't guarantee a higher number of shares if demand is very high.

*   **Myth:** You must apply at the highest price in the band to get shares.
*   **Fact:** You can bid at any price within the price band. Many investors choose to bid at the "cut-off price," which means they are willing to pay the final price discovered by the company, whatever it turns out to be within the band.

## FAQ

### How do I even apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your demat account provider or your stockbroker's online platform. Most brokers offer an easy-to-use interface for IPO applications, often integrated with ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount).

### What is a lot size?
A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for when applying in an IPO. You cannot apply for individual shares; you must apply in multiples of the specified lot.

### What is a price band?
The price band is the fixed range (e.g., ₹100 to ₹105) within which a company offers its shares for sale in an IPO. Investors must place their bids within this specified range.

### What is "allotment"?
Allotment is the process where shares are distributed to successful applicants after the IPO bidding period closes. If you receive an allotment, shares are credited to your demat account.

### What happens if I don't get shares?
If you don't receive an allotment of shares, the money you applied with (which was blocked in your bank account via ASBA) will be unblocked and returned to your account.
