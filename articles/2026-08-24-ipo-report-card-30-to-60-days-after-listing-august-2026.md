## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The first day these shares are traded on the stock exchange is known as the "listing day." Often, there's a lot of excitement and buzz around new IPOs, which can lead to a "listing gain" (when the share price closes higher than its IPO price on the first day).

However, the listing day price can be quite volatile (meaning it can swing a lot). It reflects immediate demand and market sentiment, which might not always last. Many IPOs also have "anchor investors" (large institutional investors who commit to buying shares before the IPO opens to the public). These anchor investors usually have a "lock-in period" (a time, typically 30 days, during which they cannot sell their shares).

By looking at an IPO 30 to 60 days after its listing, we get a calmer, more settled picture. The initial frenzy has usually faded, and the anchor investor lock-in period is over. This means the stock price is often reflecting more sustained demand and supply, giving us a better sense of how the market is truly valuing the company after the initial excitement has passed. It's like checking how a new shop is doing a month or two after its grand opening, once the initial rush has settled down.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

An IPO's performance on its listing day is a result of several factors, mainly the balance between "demand" (how many investors want to buy shares) and "supply" (how many shares are available).

*   **Subscription Levels:** If an IPO receives very high "subscription" (meaning investors bid for many more shares than the company is offering), it signals strong demand. This often leads to a "pop" (a significant price increase) on listing day. If subscription is low, demand is weak, and the price might drop.
*   **Market Mood:** The overall sentiment of the broader stock market plays a big role. If the market is generally positive and rising, new IPOs tend to perform better. A cautious or falling market can dampen enthusiasm for new listings.
*   **Pricing:** The company's "issue price" (the price at which shares are offered in the IPO) is crucial. If investors feel the shares are offered at a reasonable or even slightly undervalued price, it creates an incentive to buy, leading to a potential listing gain. If the price is seen as too high, investors might not be interested, causing the stock to drop.
*   **Anchor Investors:** The participation of large, well-known "anchor investors" can boost confidence among other investors, signaling that the company is a good prospect.
*   **Lock-in Periods:** As mentioned earlier, anchor investors have a lock-in period. During this time, the supply of shares in the market is limited. Once the lock-in ends, more shares become available for trading, which can sometimes affect the price.

Think of it like a popular new food stall at a school fete. If many students want to buy the food (high demand) and the price seems fair, it might sell out quickly and even lead to a small queue (a "pop"). But if few students are interested, or the price seems too high, the stall might struggle to sell its items, and the price might even be lowered (a "drop").

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

While a big listing gain (or loss) can grab headlines, it's important to remember that the first-day price is just a snapshot.

*   **Initial Volatility:** Listing day prices are often very "volatile" (they can fluctuate wildly) due to the initial rush of buying and selling. Many short-term traders might enter and exit positions quickly, which can exaggerate price movements.
*   **Broader Market Influence:** The performance of the overall stock market (like the Nifty or Sensex) on listing day can also influence a new stock's debut, sometimes overshadowing the company's own fundamentals.
*   **Long-term Value:** A company's true value and growth potential usually take time to be reflected in its share price. A stock might list flat (meaning it doesn't gain or lose much on its first day) but then steadily grow over weeks or months as investors learn more about the business and its performance. Conversely, a stock with a strong listing gain might see its price fall later if the company's actual results don't meet expectations.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine you've bought a ticket to a big cricket match. On the day of the match, everyone is excited, and the ticket prices might even be much higher than what you paid. But after the match is over, and a month has passed, the true value of that ticket (as a memory or collectible) settles down. The initial match-day excitement is like the listing day, and the settled value after a month is closer to how the stock price behaves after the initial buzz fades.

## Things people get confused about

### Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.
**Fact:** A strong listing gain primarily reflects high short-term demand and positive market sentiment at the time of listing. It does not automatically guarantee the company's long-term financial health or future stock performance.

### Myth → Fact: If an IPO lists below its issue price, it's a bad investment forever.
**Fact:** A weak listing can be due to various market conditions, pricing decisions, or temporary sentiment. Some stocks that list below their issue price can recover and perform well over time, just as some that list strongly might later decline.

### Myth → Fact: IPOs are a guaranteed way to make quick money.
**Fact:** Investing in IPOs, like any stock market investment, carries risks. There is no guarantee of returns, and prices can go up or down. It is important to understand the company and the market before applying.

## FAQ

### What is an SME IPO?
SME IPOs are Initial Public Offerings by Small and Medium Enterprises, which list on dedicated platforms like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, typically for smaller companies with different listing requirements than Mainboard IPOs.

### What is a Mainboard IPO?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger companies that list on the main stock exchanges (National Stock Exchange and Bombay Stock Exchange), generally requiring higher market capitalisation and meeting more stringent regulations.

### What does 'return vs. issue price' mean?
This figure shows the percentage change between the original IPO price and the current market price, indicating the profit or loss an investor would have if they were allotted shares in the IPO and held them until today.

### Can an IPO price go below its issue price?
Yes, absolutely. If market demand is not strong enough, or if investors perceive the company's valuation as too high, the share price can fall below its original IPO price after listing.

### What is the purpose of an IPO?
Companies issue IPOs primarily to raise capital (money) from the public to fund business expansion, reduce debt, or for other corporate purposes, and also to provide liquidity for existing shareholders to sell their shares.
