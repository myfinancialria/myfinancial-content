# Why Derivatives Trading Just Got More 'Cash-Heavy' in India

**TL;DR**
- The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) recently tightened rules for bank guarantees used in derivatives trading.
- Now, banks must fully back these guarantees with collateral, with at least half of it in cash.
- This change makes it harder for traders to use borrowed money (leverage) in the derivatives market.
- It increases funding costs for trading firms and limits their ability to execute large trades.
- The new rules aim to reduce risks for banks and the financial system.

## What's this about?
The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has introduced new, stricter rules for how bank guarantees are used in the derivatives market. A **bank guarantee** is essentially a promise from a bank that if a client fails to meet their financial obligations, the bank will step in and cover the amount. In the world of **derivatives** (financial contracts whose value comes from an underlying asset like stocks or indices, commonly used for hedging or speculation), these guarantees often help traders meet margin requirements without blocking all their cash.

The new directive from the RBI requires banks to ensure that these guarantees are fully backed by **collateral** (assets pledged by a borrower to a lender as security for a loan), with at least 50% of this collateral needing to be in cash. This means trading firms can no longer rely heavily on non-cash assets to secure their bank guarantees for derivatives trading.

## Why it's in the news right now
This change is in the news because it was implemented by the RBI "last month" (July 2026), and its effects are now visible. Data for July 2026 showed a significant drop in derivatives trading activity on the National Stock Exchange (NSE). The average daily notional turnover for futures and options (F&O) on the NSE declined by 23% in July compared to June, reaching its lowest point since February 2025. This slump in trading volumes is largely attributed to these tighter funding rules from the RBI.

## How it actually works
Before these new rules, trading firms often used bank guarantees to meet their margin requirements for derivatives trading. **Margins** are like a security deposit that traders need to keep with the exchange or their broker to cover potential losses. Instead of putting up a lot of cash, they could get a bank guarantee, which the bank would issue based on various forms of collateral, not necessarily all cash.

Here’s how the new system works:
1.  **Bank Guarantees for Margins**: When a trading firm wants to trade in derivatives, they need to provide margins. They can do this by depositing cash, shares, or by submitting a bank guarantee.
2.  **RBI's New Collateral Rule**: The RBI now mandates that for any bank guarantee issued for derivatives trading, the issuing bank must hold 100% collateral from the trading firm.
3.  **Cash Component**: Crucially, at least half (50%) of this 100% collateral must be in cash. This is the biggest change. Previously, a bank might accept mostly non-cash assets (like property, fixed deposits, or shares) as collateral for a guarantee.
4.  **Impact on Leverage**: This means trading firms need to block more of their actual cash to get bank guarantees. This directly reduces their ability to use **leverage** (trading with borrowed money to amplify returns, but also risks).
5.  **Funding Costs**: With more cash being blocked, the cost of funding for these trading operations goes up, as that cash cannot be used for other purposes.

## A simple way to think about it
Imagine you run a small kirana shop and you buy goods from a big wholesaler on credit. The wholesaler asks for a guarantee from your bank, promising they'll pay if you can't.

Earlier, your bank might have given this guarantee by just looking at your shop's inventory or some other assets you own. You didn't need to put up much actual cash.

Now, it's like the bank tells you: "We can still give that guarantee, but you need to keep *all* the money for the guaranteed amount with us. And half of that money *must* be in actual cash, not just your shop's stock."

This means you have less cash free to buy new goods or run your daily shop operations, making it harder to expand your credit purchases. The wholesaler (like the derivatives market) is safer, but you (the trading firm) need more upfront cash to play.

## Why it matters to you
1.  **Reduced Market Activity**: If you are an active trader in the F&O segment, you might notice lower trading volumes and potentially less liquidity in some contracts, as trading firms face higher costs and reduced capacity.
2.  **Higher Costs for Brokers**: Brokers and trading firms might face increased operational costs due to these new collateral requirements. This could, in turn, lead to adjustments in brokerage charges or other fees for their clients.
3.  **Impact on Derivatives Strategies**: For those who use derivatives for hedging (reducing risk) or speculation, the dynamics might shift. Strategies relying heavily on leverage could become more expensive or difficult to execute.
4.  **Systemic Stability**: While it might make trading more expensive for some, the RBI's goal is to reduce the risk exposure for banks and the overall financial system. This means a more stable market environment in the long run, which benefits all investors by reducing the chances of large-scale financial disruptions.
5.  **Shift in Capital Allocation**: Trading firms might re-evaluate their capital allocation, potentially shifting some funds away from derivatives if the new rules make it less profitable or too capital-intensive.

## Common confusions cleared up
**Myth:** The RBI has banned derivatives trading.
**Fact:** No, the RBI has not banned derivatives trading. It has only tightened the rules for how bank guarantees are collateralized when used for derivatives margins, making it more 'cash-heavy' for trading firms.

**Myth:** This affects all stock market trading.
**Fact:** These specific rules primarily impact the **derivatives segment** (futures and options) where bank guarantees are used to meet margin requirements. Regular equity cash market trading is not directly affected by this particular change.

**Myth:** This is a punishment for traders.
**Fact:** The RBI's move is aimed at strengthening the financial system by reducing the risk exposure of banks. While it affects trading firms' leverage and costs, the primary objective is regulatory prudence, not to penalize market participants.

## FAQ
### What is a bank guarantee?
A bank guarantee is a promise from a bank to cover a financial obligation if their client defaults. It acts as a form of security.

### What are derivatives?
Derivatives are financial contracts whose value is derived from an underlying asset, like stocks, commodities, or currencies. They are used for managing risk (hedging) or for making speculative bets on future price movements.

### What is collateral?
Collateral refers to assets that a borrower pledges to a lender as security for a loan. If the borrower fails to repay, the lender can seize the collateral.

### Why did the RBI make this change?
The RBI introduced these tighter rules to reduce the risk for banks that issue guarantees for derivatives trading. By requiring more cash collateral, banks are better protected if a trading firm defaults.

### Does this mean derivatives trading will stop?
No, derivatives trading will continue. However, the new rules are expected to make it more capital-intensive and reduce the amount of leverage available to trading firms, which has already led to a dip in trading volumes.
