# ELSS Outflows: Are Tax-Saving Mutual Funds Losing Relevance?

**TL;DR**
- Equity Linked Savings Schemes (ELSS) have seen significant net outflows recently, with ₹650 crore redeemed in May alone.
- This trend suggests a potential shift in how Indian investors approach tax-saving instruments.
- The increasing adoption of the New Tax Regime, which offers no Section 80C deductions, is a key factor influencing ELSS popularity.
- Despite outflows, ELSS remains a viable option for those opting for the Old Tax Regime, offering a blend of tax benefits and equity growth.
- Investors should evaluate ELSS based on their tax regime choice and long-term financial goals, not solely on recent redemption trends.

## What this means in plain terms

Equity Linked Savings Schemes, or ELSS funds, are a type of mutual fund that allows you to save tax under Section 80C of the Income Tax Act, 1961. In return for this tax benefit, your investment is locked in for three years. Recently, these funds have seen a notable trend: more money is being withdrawn than invested. In May, for instance, ELSS funds experienced net outflows of ₹650 crore, marking the fifth consecutive month of such redemptions. This pattern has been consistent, with outflows recorded in 13 of the past 14 months.

This doesn't necessarily mean ELSS funds are "bad" investments. Instead, it signals a shift in investor behaviour and the broader tax landscape. A significant reason for this change could be the growing popularity of the New Tax Regime, introduced by the government, which simplifies taxes but removes many deductions, including those under Section 80C. For individuals choosing this new regime, the primary incentive for investing in ELSS – tax savings – no longer exists. Consequently, investors might be redeeming their money once the three-year lock-in period ends, choosing other investment avenues that align with their current tax planning or financial goals.

## Understanding ELSS and its Dual Advantage

ELSS funds are unique among Section 80C instruments because they invest predominantly in equity and equity-related instruments. This means they offer the potential for higher returns compared to traditional fixed-income tax-saving options like Public Provident Fund (PPF) or tax-saving Fixed Deposits (FDs). However, this also comes with higher market risk.

### The Tax Benefit under Section 80C
Under the Old Tax Regime, investments up to ₹1.5 lakhs in ELSS funds in a financial year are eligible for a deduction from your taxable income under Section 80C of the Income Tax Act. This can significantly reduce your overall tax liability. For example, if you are in the 30% tax bracket and invest ₹1.5 lakhs in ELSS, you could save up to ₹46,800 in taxes (including cess).

### The Mandated Lock-in Period
A key feature of ELSS is its three-year lock-in period, which is the shortest among all Section 80C instruments. This lock-in encourages a disciplined, long-term approach to equity investing. While some investors might view it as a restriction, it can be beneficial by preventing impulsive redemptions during market volatility, allowing the investment to potentially compound over time.

## The New Tax Regime and Its Impact on ELSS

The government introduced the New Tax Regime in Budget 2020, offering individuals an alternative to the Old Tax Regime. The primary appeal of the New Tax Regime is its lower tax slabs, but it comes with a trade-off: taxpayers choosing this regime have to forgo most exemptions and deductions, including those under Section 80C, House Rent Allowance (HRA), and Leave Travel Allowance (LTA).

### Shifting Investor Preferences
For individuals who opt for the New Tax Regime, the primary incentive to invest in ELSS – the Section 80C tax deduction – simply vanishes. This directly impacts the demand for ELSS funds. As more salaried and self-employed individuals find the New Tax Regime beneficial due to its simplified structure and lower tax rates at certain income levels, their need for tax-saving instruments like ELSS diminishes. The recent outflows from ELSS funds strongly correlate with the increasing adoption of this new regime.

### ELSS as a Pure Equity Investment
For those under the New Tax Regime, ELSS loses its unique tax-saving appeal. However, it still remains an equity mutual fund. If an investor wishes to invest in diversified equities with a three-year time horizon, they could still consider ELSS. But without the tax benefit, other diversified equity funds with no lock-in period might become more attractive, offering similar market exposure without the restriction.

## ELSS vs. Other Tax-Saving Avenues

While ELSS offers tax benefits coupled with equity exposure, it's important to compare it with other popular Section 80C investment options available to Indian taxpayers. Each instrument serves different financial goals and risk appetites.

### Public Provident Fund (PPF)
PPF is a government-backed, long-term savings scheme with a 15-year lock-in period. It offers guaranteed returns, which are currently tax-free. PPF is ideal for conservative investors seeking capital safety and predictable returns, but it lacks the growth potential of equity.

### National Pension System (NPS)
NPS is a voluntary retirement savings scheme regulated by PFRDA. It offers tax benefits under Section 80C, 80CCD(1B), and 80CCD(2). While it invests in a mix of equities, corporate bonds, and government securities, it has a long lock-in till retirement (age 60) and complex withdrawal rules. It's primarily a retirement planning tool.

### Tax-Saving Fixed Deposits (FDs)
These are bank fixed deposits with a five-year lock-in period, offering a tax deduction under Section 80C. They provide guaranteed, albeit modest, returns. However, the interest earned on these FDs is taxable as per your income slab. They are suitable for extremely risk-averse investors.

### Unit-Linked Insurance Plans (ULIPs)
ULIPs combine life insurance coverage with investment, offering tax benefits under Section 80C. They invest in various funds (equity, debt, hybrid) based on the policyholder's choice. However, ULIPs often come with higher charges and a five-year lock-in, making them less transparent and potentially less efficient than direct mutual fund investments for pure investment goals.

ELSS stands out with its shortest lock-in period (3 years) and pure equity exposure, making it suitable for investors willing to take on market risk for potentially higher, inflation-beating returns.

## A real example

Meet Rahul, 38, a software engineer in Bengaluru, with an annual CTC of ₹28 lakhs. He has been consistently investing in ELSS funds for the past five years to save tax under the Old Tax Regime.

In Financial Year 2022-23, Rahul invested ₹1.5 lakhs in an ELSS fund. Assuming he is in the 30% tax bracket (plus 4% cess), his tax saving for that year was approximately:
Tax saving = ₹1,50,000 * 30% = ₹45,000
Cess = ₹45,000 * 4% = ₹1,800
Total tax saved = ₹45,000 + ₹1,800 = ₹46,800.

Now, let's look at the investment growth. Suppose his ₹1.5 lakh ELSS investment made in FY 2022-23 (which completed its 3-year lock-in by early FY 2025-26) grew at an average annualised return of 15%.
Investment after 3 years = ₹1,50,000 * (1 + 0.15)^3
= ₹1,50,000 * 1.520875
= ₹2,28,131.25

Rahul would have saved ₹46,800 in taxes and his investment would have grown to ₹2,28,131.25 from an initial ₹1.5 lakhs. The long-term capital gains (LTCG) from ELSS are taxable at 10% on gains exceeding ₹1 lakh in a financial year. If Rahul redeemed this entire amount, his capital gain would be ₹2,28,131.25 - ₹1,50,000 = ₹78,131.25. Since this is below the ₹1 lakh exemption limit for LTCG from equity, he would pay no tax on these gains.

However, for FY 2025-26, Rahul decided to switch to the New Tax Regime as his deductions had reduced. Consequently, he chose not to invest in ELSS this year, aligning with the broader trend of outflows as investors exit after their lock-in period and adjust to the new tax structure.

## FAQ

### What is the lock-in period for ELSS funds?
ELSS funds have a mandatory lock-in period of three years from the date of investment. This is the shortest lock-in among all Section 80C tax-saving instruments.

### Is ELSS still relevant under the New Tax Regime?
No, if you choose the New Tax Regime, you cannot claim deductions under Section 80C, so ELSS loses its primary tax-saving advantage. However, it still functions as an equity mutual fund for wealth creation.

### How are ELSS returns taxed?
Long-term capital gains (LTCG) from ELSS, exceeding ₹1 lakh in a financial year, are taxed at 10% without indexation. Dividends, if any, are taxed as per the investor's income slab.

### Can I invest in ELSS through SIP?
Yes, you can invest in ELSS through a Systematic Investment Plan (SIP). Each SIP instalment will have its own three-year lock-in period from its respective investment date.

### What happens if I redeem ELSS before the lock-in?
You cannot redeem ELSS units before the completion of the three-year lock-in period. Premature withdrawal is not permitted under any circumstances.

### Should I switch from ELSS to another fund after lock-in?
This depends on your financial goals, risk appetite, and market outlook. If you need the funds or prefer a different investment strategy, you can redeem and reinvest. If your goals align with continued equity exposure, you can remain invested.

### Are ELSS funds suitable for conservative investors?
ELSS funds invest primarily in equities, making them subject to market risks. They are generally more suitable for investors with a moderate to high-risk appetite and a long-term investment horizon.
