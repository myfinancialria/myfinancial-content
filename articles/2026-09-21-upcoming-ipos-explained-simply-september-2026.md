## First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine your favourite local chai shop has been doing incredibly well. They have a loyal customer base, and now the owner wants to open several new branches across the city. To get the money needed for this big expansion, they decide to sell a small part of their ownership (called 'shares') to you and me, the public. This first-time sale of shares to the public is called an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)**.

When a company does an IPO, it's selling shares for the first time in the **primary market** (think of it as the 'first sale' market). Once the IPO process is complete, these shares get **listed** on a stock exchange like the NSE or BSE. This means they can then be bought and sold by anyone in the regular stock market, which we call the **secondary market**. Companies do an IPO mainly to raise money for growth, pay off debts, or sometimes, for existing owners to sell some of their shares.

## How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, there are a few key numbers and terms that help you understand what's happening:

1.  **Price Band:** This is the fixed range within which you can bid for shares. For example, Pooja Logistics has a price band of ₹109 to ₹115. You can bid at any price within this range. The company usually sets a "floor price" (the lowest) and a "cap price" (the highest).
2.  **Lot Size:** This is the minimum number of shares you must apply for. You cannot apply for just one or two shares; you have to apply for at least one "lot." For Pooja Logistics, one lot is 1,200 shares. This system helps manage the large number of applications.
3.  **Minimum Investment:** This is the smallest amount of money you need to apply for one lot. You calculate this by multiplying the lot size by the upper end of the price band. For Pooja Logistics, if you apply at the upper price band of ₹115 per share, your minimum investment for one lot (1,200 shares) would be ₹115 x 1,200 = ₹1,38,000. For IPOs where the lot size is not yet announced, this amount cannot be calculated yet.
4.  **Issue Size:** This refers to the total number of shares the company is offering to the public in the IPO. It tells you how many shares are being made available for sale. For instance, National Stock Exchange of India Limited is offering 88,642,911 shares.
5.  **Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS):** An IPO typically consists of two parts.
    *   A **Fresh Issue** is when the company issues brand new shares, and the money raised from these shares goes directly to the company. This money is used for its business needs, like expansion, buying new equipment, or repaying debt.
    *   An **Offer-for-Sale (OFS)** is when existing shareholders (like the company's founders or early investors) sell some of their shares to the public. In this case, the money raised from these shares goes to those selling shareholders, not to the company itself. The data provided does not specify the breakdown between fresh issue and OFS for these IPOs, but it's an important distinction to look for in the company's official documents.
6.  **RHP/DRHP (Red Herring Prospectus / Draft Red Herring Prospectus):** This is the most important document for any IPO. It's a detailed legal document that the company files with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India). Think of it as the company's complete report card and blueprint for the IPO. It contains everything you need to know: the company's business, financial performance, management team, risks involved, and the full details of the IPO.

## The IPO process, step by step

Here's how an IPO typically unfolds:

1.  **DRHP Filing:** The company first prepares a detailed document called the **Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP)** and submits it to SEBI for approval. This document contains all the necessary information about the company and its plans.
2.  **SEBI Approval & RHP:** SEBI reviews the DRHP to ensure all disclosures are proper. Once approved, the company files the final **Red Herring Prospectus (RHP)**.
3.  **Price Band & Dates Announced:** The company then publicly announces the **price band** (the range within which investors can bid) and the dates when the IPO will be open for applications.
4.  **Bidding Period:** During the announced dates, investors can apply for shares. Applications are usually divided into categories:
    *   **Retail Individual Investors (RIIs):** Ordinary individuals who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs) / High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs):** Individuals or entities applying for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs.
    *   **Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs):** Large institutional investors like mutual funds, foreign institutional investors, and insurance companies.
5.  **Allotment:** After the bidding closes, the company processes the applications. If the IPO receives more applications than shares offered (called **oversubscription**), shares are allotted to investors. For retail investors, this is usually done through a lottery system to ensure fairness. If the IPO is undersubscribed, everyone who applied typically gets shares.
6.  **Listing:** Once the shares are allotted, they get **listed** on the stock exchange (like NSE or BSE). This means they can now be freely bought and sold by anyone in the secondary market, just like any other existing stock.

## A simple way to think about it

Think of your local school holding its annual fete. There's a very popular stall selling delicious homemade snacks. This stall has been so successful that the students running it decide they want to open a proper, permanent shop outside of school. To get the money to set up this new shop, they decide to offer "ownership tickets" (shares) for the first time to everyone at the fete. If you buy one of these tickets, you become a small part-owner of that snack business. This entire process – from offering tickets for the first time to getting them ready for trading – is like an IPO.

## Things people get confused about

Here are a few common misunderstandings about IPOs:

*   **Myth:** Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares.
    *   **Fact:** If an IPO is very popular and receives many more applications than the shares available (it's "oversubscribed"), the shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors. This means you might not get any shares, even if you applied.
*   **Myth:** All IPOs give quick profits on listing day.
    *   **Fact:** An IPO's performance on its listing day depends on many market factors and the company's business prospects. While some IPOs may see gains, there is no guarantee of profits, and share prices can go down too.
*   **Myth:** You should apply for as many lots as possible to increase your chances of getting shares.
    *   **Fact:** For retail investors, applying for more than one lot from the same PAN card usually does not increase your chances of allotment in an oversubscribed IPO. It's typically one application per PAN card that is considered for the lottery. Applying for just one lot is often sufficient.

## FAQ

### How do I even apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) facility via net banking, or through your stockbroker's online platform. Your money remains blocked in your account until allotment.

### What is a lot size?
A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot apply for fewer shares than the specified lot size.

### What does "Mainboard" vs. "SME" mean?
**Mainboard** IPOs are for larger, more established companies and list on the main platforms of stock exchanges like NSE and BSE. **SME** (Small and Medium Enterprises) IPOs are for smaller companies and list on dedicated SME platforms of the exchanges, often with different trading rules and investor categories.

### Can I sell my shares immediately after listing?
Yes, once your allotted shares are listed on the stock exchange, you can buy or sell them like any other stock in the secondary market, subject to market conditions.

### What happens if I don't get an allotment?
If you don't get any shares in an IPO, the money that was blocked in your bank account (via ASBA) is simply unblocked and becomes available to you again, usually within a few days after the allotment process.
