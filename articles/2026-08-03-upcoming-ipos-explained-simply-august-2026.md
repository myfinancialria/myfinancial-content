## First, what is an an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a popular local shop that has been doing well for years. Now, the owners want to grow bigger – maybe open more branches or buy new equipment. To do this, they need a lot of money. Instead of borrowing from a bank, they decide to sell a small part of their ownership to the public for the very first time.

This act of a private company selling its shares to the public for the first time is called an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)**. When a company does an IPO, it's raising money from the "primary market" (where new shares are sold). Once the shares are sold and listed, they can be bought and sold by anyone on the stock exchange, which is called "listing".

## How to actually read an IPO (the 6 things on every IPO)

When you look at an IPO, there are a few key numbers and terms that help you understand what's being offered:

1.  **Price Band:** This is the range of prices within which investors can bid for the shares. For example, if the price band is ₹214 to ₹225, you can choose to bid at ₹214, ₹220, or ₹225, or any price in between. The highest price in the band (₹225 in this example) is often called the "cap price" or "cut-off price".
2.  **Lot Size:** Shares in an IPO are not sold individually, but in fixed bundles called "lots". If the lot size is 50 shares, you must apply for at least 50 shares, or multiples of 50 (like 100, 150, etc.). This makes it easier for the company to manage applications.
3.  **Minimum Investment:** This is the smallest amount of money you need to apply for an IPO. You calculate it by multiplying the lot size by the lowest price in the price band. For example, if the lot size is 50 and the lowest price is ₹214, your minimum investment would be 50 shares * ₹214/share = ₹10,700.
4.  **Issue Size:** This tells you the total number of shares (or sometimes the total value in rupees) that the company is offering to the public through the IPO. A larger issue size means more shares are available.
5.  **Fresh Issue vs. Offer-for-Sale (OFS):**
    *   **Fresh Issue:** When a company issues new shares, and the money raised from these shares goes directly to the company. This money is used for things like business expansion, debt repayment, or working capital.
    *   **Offer-for-Sale (OFS):** When existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares to the public. In this case, the money raised goes to those selling shareholders, not to the company itself. An IPO can be a mix of both.
6.  **Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) / Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP):** This is the official document that a company files with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) before an IPO. It contains all the detailed information about the company, its business, financials, risks, management, and how the IPO money will be used. It's like a detailed report card and business plan rolled into one. The DRHP is the draft version, and the RHP is the final version filed before the IPO opens.

## The IPO process, step by step

Here's how an IPO typically unfolds:

1.  **DRHP Filing:** The company first files its Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) with SEBI. This document contains all the details about the company and its plans for the IPO. SEBI reviews this document to ensure everything is in order.
2.  **Price Band & Dates Announcement:** Once SEBI approves, the company announces the IPO's price band (e.g., ₹214 to ₹225), the lot size, and the open and close dates for bidding.
3.  **Bidding Period:** During the open dates, investors can place their bids. There are usually different categories of investors:
    *   **Retail Individual Investors (RIIs):** Ordinary individuals who can apply for shares up to a certain value (currently ₹2 lakhs).
    *   **Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs) / High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs):** Individuals or entities applying for more than ₹2 lakhs worth of shares.
    *   **Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs):** Large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors.
4.  **Allotment:** After the bidding closes, the company and its bankers decide how to distribute the shares. If the IPO is "oversubscribed" (meaning more people applied for shares than available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for retail investors.
5.  **Listing:** Once shares are allotted, they are listed on the stock exchanges (like NSE and BSE). This is when the shares start trading publicly, and you can buy or sell them like any other listed stock.

## A simple way to think about it

Think of an IPO like a school fete stall. The school needs money for new books. They decide to sell "ownership tickets" in their popular snack stall. Each ticket (share) costs a certain amount (price band), and you have to buy a minimum number of tickets (lot size). When you buy a ticket, you become a tiny owner of the snack stall's future profits. The money you pay goes to the school (fresh issue) or to the student who first set up the stall and is now selling some of their tickets (offer-for-sale). Once the tickets are sold, they can be traded among students (listing).

## Things people get confused about

*   **Myth:** Applying for an IPO guarantees you shares.
    *   **Fact:** If an IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more people want shares than are available), shares are allotted through a lottery system, especially for retail investors. There's no guarantee you'll get shares.
*   **Myth:** IPOs always give "listing gains" (meaning the share price will go up on the first day of trading).
    *   **Fact:** While some IPOs do see gains on listing day, it's not guaranteed. Share prices can also fall below the issue price. The market decides the price based on demand and supply.
*   **Myth:** Investing in an IPO is a quick way to get rich.
    *   **Fact:** IPOs are just one way to invest in the stock market. Like any investment, they come with risks. It's important to understand the company and its business before investing, and to have a long-term perspective.

## FAQ

### How do I even apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your bank or broker using the ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility. Your application money is blocked in your bank account and only debited if you are allotted shares.

### What is a lot size?
A lot size is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot apply for fewer than the specified lot size, and you must apply in multiples of it.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger companies and list on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME (Small and Medium Enterprises) IPOs are for smaller companies and list on dedicated SME platforms (like NSE Emerge or BSE SME) with slightly different regulations and typically smaller issue sizes.

### Can I apply for multiple lots?
Yes, as a retail investor, you can apply for multiple lots, but your total application value cannot exceed ₹2 lakhs. If you wish to apply for more than ₹2 lakhs, you would fall under the HNI (High Net-worth Individual) category.

### What is the cut-off price?
The cut-off price is the highest price within the price band. When you apply for an IPO, you can bid at a specific price or choose to bid at the "cut-off price," which means you are willing to pay whatever the final issue price is, provided it falls within the announced price band. This increases your chances of allotment in case the final price is fixed at the higher end of the band.
