## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?
When a company first launches its shares to the public through an IPO (Initial Public Offering), there's often a lot of excitement. The first day of trading, known as the listing day, can see prices jump significantly, leading to a 'listing gain' (the percentage increase from the IPO price to the closing price on the first day). However, this initial buzz can fade. Looking at a company's share price 30 to 60 days after it lists gives you a calmer picture. By this time, the initial frenzy has usually settled, and any 'lock-in' periods for anchor investors (large investors who commit to buying shares before the IPO) might be ending. This allows the market to find a more stable price based on actual demand and supply, rather than just initial excitement.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?
An IPO's performance on listing day is mainly about demand and supply. If many investors want to buy shares and there aren't enough available (high 'subscription levels'), the price can 'pop' or increase. This strong demand might be due to positive market mood or the IPO being 'priced' (set at an initial share price) attractively. Conversely, if there's less interest or too many shares available, the price might 'drop'. Sometimes, 'anchor investors' (large institutional investors) buy a significant portion of shares before the IPO. Their shares often have a 'lock-in period' (a time when they cannot sell their shares), which can influence supply after this period ends.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story
The listing day price is just a snapshot. Share prices can be quite 'volatile' (change rapidly) in the first few days or weeks. A stock might list with a big gain, but then its price could fall later, or it might list flat and then climb steadily. The broader market conditions (how the overall stock market is doing) also play a big role. A company's true value often takes time to reflect in its share price, beyond the initial excitement or disappointment of listing day.

## A simple way to think about it
Imagine a new restaurant opening with a lot of hype and special inaugural offers. Everyone rushes in on day one. But after a month or two, the initial buzz settles down. The prices and customer footfall then reflect the actual quality of food and service, and what people are truly willing to pay regularly. An IPO's listing day is like the grand opening, and the 30-60 day mark is when the market starts to assess its regular performance.

## Things people get confused about
### Myth → Fact: Big listing gain means a great company
Fact: A strong listing gain shows high initial market demand, but it doesn't guarantee the company will perform well in the long term. Long-term success depends on business fundamentals.

### Myth → Fact: Listing below IPO price means a bad investment
Fact: An initial drop can be due to various market factors or temporary sentiment. A company's long-term prospects might still be strong, and the price could recover later.

## FAQ
### What is a Mainboard IPO?
These are IPOs by larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges like NSE and BSE. They have stricter eligibility criteria and higher disclosure requirements.

### What is an SME IPO?
SME stands for Small and Medium Enterprises. These are IPOs by smaller companies, listed on dedicated SME platforms of NSE and BSE. They have different rules and often involve smaller issue sizes.

### What is the 'Issue Price'?
This is the fixed price per share at which the company offers its shares to the public during the IPO application period. It's the price you pay if your application is successful.

### What does 'Return vs Issue Price' mean?
This percentage tells you how much the current market price of the share has changed compared to its original IPO issue price. It shows the gain or loss for someone who bought shares in the IPO.
