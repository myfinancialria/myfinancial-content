# Gold, Silver, Platinum Import Tax Relief Ends: What It Means for Buyers

**TL;DR**
- The Indian government has withdrawn the upfront IGST exemption for banks and nominated agencies importing precious metals.
- This means importers now pay IGST at the time of import, impacting their working capital.
- A new 3% customs levy has also been introduced on these precious metal imports since April 1, 2026.
- This additional 3% customs levy is a direct cost increase for importers, likely passed on to consumers.
- Buyers of physical gold, silver, and platinum jewellery or articles could see slightly higher prices domestically.

## What this means in plain terms

Until recently, banks and other government-nominated agencies importing precious metals like gold, silver, and platinum enjoyed an upfront exemption from paying Integrated Goods and Services Tax (IGST) at the time of import. This meant they didn't have to block capital for this tax component, which they would later collect from the end consumer.

However, this exemption has now been withdrawn. Since April 1, 2026, these importers must pay IGST upfront when bringing these precious metals into the country. More significantly for the end consumer, a new 3% customs levy has also been introduced on these imports. This 3% levy is an additional cost for the importers, and it is highly probable that this will translate into higher retail prices for gold, silver, and platinum products in the domestic market.

## Understanding the Previous Tax Treatment for Precious Metal Imports

Before April 1, 2026, the import of precious metals by nominated agencies and banks benefited from a specific tax arrangement. While the end sale of gold, silver, or platinum to a consumer always attracted IGST (or CGST/SGST, depending on the transaction), the importers themselves were exempt from paying IGST upfront at the time of import. This was primarily a cash flow benefit for these entities, as it reduced the immediate capital required to bring in large quantities of precious metals. The rationale was to streamline the import process for authorised agencies and ensure liquidity in the supply chain for a commodity that is frequently traded. This upfront exemption was a significant operational advantage for these nominated agencies and banks.

## The New Tax Regime for Precious Metal Imports

The government has now altered this tax framework, effective April 1, 2026. The key changes are twofold:

### Withdrawal of Upfront IGST Exemption
Banks and nominated agencies importing gold, silver, and platinum can no longer avail the upfront IGST exemption. This means that at the point of import, they are now required to pay IGST on the value of the imported precious metals. While importers can typically claim this IGST back as Input Tax Credit (ITC) against their outward GST liabilities, it does impact their working capital. They must now fund this tax payment initially, which was not the case before.

### Introduction of a New 3% Customs Levy
In addition to the withdrawal of the IGST exemption, a new 3% customs levy has been imposed on these imports. This 3% is a direct increase in the cost of importing precious metals. Unlike IGST, which can be claimed back as ITC, this customs levy is an outright cost for the importer. This levy is over and above any existing basic customs duties (BCD) that may already apply to these metals. For instance, if basic customs duty on gold is 15%, this new 3% levy would be an additional charge on the import value, further increasing the landed cost of the metal. This change is a direct result of government policy aimed at adjusting the taxation structure for these high-value imports.

## Impact on Domestic Prices and Buyers

The combined effect of these changes is likely to lead to higher domestic prices for gold, silver, and platinum.

### Direct Price Increase from 3% Customs Levy
The introduction of the 3% customs levy is a direct cost addition for importers. Since this is an unrecoverable cost for them, it will almost certainly be passed on to the end consumers. For example, if a certain quantity of gold previously cost ₹100 to import (excluding existing duties), it will now cost ₹103 due to this new levy. This increment will then be factored into the retail price, along with other costs like making charges and GST on the final sale.

### Cash Flow Impact for Importers
While the upfront IGST paid by importers is recoverable as ITC, the need to block capital for this payment can still have an indirect impact. Importers might factor in the cost of financing this blocked capital into their pricing, leading to a marginal increase. However, the primary driver for higher prices will be the non-recoverable 3% customs levy.

### Overall Effect on Consumers
For individuals looking to purchase physical gold, silver, or platinum in the form of jewellery, coins, or bars, the new tax regime means they will likely pay more. This applies to both salaried individuals and self-employed professionals, including Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) who purchase precious metals in India. The extent of the price increase will be directly proportional to the 3% levy and any other associated costs passed on by the supply chain.

## Implications for Investments and Jewellery Purchases

The changes in import duties and taxes on precious metals will have specific implications for consumers considering both physical purchases and certain investment avenues.

### Physical Gold, Silver, and Platinum
For those buying physical gold, silver, or platinum, whether as jewellery for personal use or as bars/coins for investment, the direct impact will be higher retail prices. This means that a piece of jewellery or a gold coin will simply cost more than it would have under the previous tax regime. This could potentially temper demand for physical precious metals to some extent, especially for discretionary purchases.

### Gold-backed Financial Products
While Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs) are not directly impacted as they are government securities denominated in grams of gold and do not involve physical import by the investor, their pricing is linked to the prevailing market price of gold. If the domestic price of physical gold rises due to these new levies, it could indirectly influence the pricing of SGBs and Gold Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs) over time. However, the primary impact is on the physical market. Investors in gold ETFs hold units representing physical gold, and the underlying price of this gold will reflect the new import costs. Therefore, the Net Asset Value (NAV) of gold ETFs could also see a corresponding increase in line with physical gold prices.

### Jewellery Industry
The jewellery industry, which relies heavily on imported precious metals, will face increased input costs. This could lead to adjustments in pricing strategies, potentially affecting sales volumes and profit margins if the increased costs cannot be fully absorbed or passed on. Consumers might also see jewellers offering more innovative designs or promotions to offset the price increase.

## A real example

Let's consider **Priya, 35, a self-employed architect in Mumbai, earning ₹35L/year**, who plans to buy a 50-gram gold chain.

**Old Scenario (Before April 1, 2026):**
*   Assume the international price for 50 grams of gold is ₹3,00,000.
*   Basic Customs Duty (BCD) at 15% (for example): ₹45,000.
*   Importer's cost (excluding IGST upfront): ₹3,00,000 + ₹45,000 = ₹3,45,000.
*   Retail price to customer (assuming a 5% markup by jeweller + making charges, ignoring IGST on final sale for comparison of import cost): Let's say ₹3,70,000.

**New Scenario (After April 1, 2026):**
*   International price for 50 grams of gold: ₹3,00,000.
*   Basic Customs Duty (BCD) at 15%: ₹45,000.
*   **New 3% Customs Levy:** 3% of ₹3,00,000 = ₹9,000.
*   Total Import Cost for Importer (before their markup and final IGST): ₹3,00,000 (gold) + ₹45,000 (BCD) + ₹9,000 (new levy) = ₹3,54,000.
*   This means the importer's direct cost has increased by ₹9,000.
*   If this ₹9,000 is fully passed on, Priya's gold chain, which previously might have cost around ₹3,70,000, would now likely start from around ₹3,79,000 (₹3,70,000 + ₹9,000), plus any additional impact from the importer's increased working capital costs due to upfront IGST and the final GST on the higher value.

In essence, Priya would pay an additional ₹9,000 (or more) for the same 50-gram gold chain due to the new 3% customs levy.

## FAQ

### What is the new 3% customs levy on precious metals?
The 3% customs levy is a new additional duty imposed on the import of gold, silver, and platinum by nominated agencies and banks, effective April 1, 2026. It adds to the existing basic customs duties.

### How does the withdrawal of upfront IGST exemption affect buyers?
While the upfront IGST exemption withdrawal primarily impacts the working capital of importers, the 3% customs levy is a direct cost increase that will likely be passed on to the end consumer, leading to higher retail prices.

### Will gold prices in India increase significantly?
The 3% customs levy will directly increase the landed cost of precious metals. While other market factors also influence prices, this change introduces a definite upward pressure on domestic prices for gold, silver, and platinum.

### Are Sovereign Gold Bonds (SGBs) affected by these changes?
SGBs are not directly affected as they are financial instruments. However, their pricing is linked to the domestic market price of gold, which could see an indirect impact if physical gold prices rise due to the new levies.

### Does this apply to all types of gold purchases?
Yes, this change impacts the import cost of the raw precious metal. Therefore, any physical purchase of gold, silver, or platinum in India, whether jewellery, coins, or bars, will likely reflect these increased costs.

### Where can I find official government notifications on these tax changes?
Official notifications regarding customs duties and GST are typically published by the Ministry of Finance and the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC).
