# Five everyday money stories

*Five practical money moments: what happened, what the numbers mean, and a small check you can do yourself.*

> **How these topics were chosen:** Recent Indian personal-finance coverage has highlighted November card-charge changes, mutual-fund plan costs and pension paperwork. The other stories draw on RBI and SEBI financial-education guidance. These are news and education signals, not measured search-volume data. All people and household situations below are fictional composites.

## 1. Rohan learns that “minimum due” is not the same as “paid off”

Rohan’s monthly card bill is ₹20,000. A tight month makes the minimum payment look tempting: the bill is marked as handled, and there is still cash for groceries and school transport. But he pauses to check what the bank means by “minimum.”

PNB’s current Key Fact Statement says the minimum amount due is generally 5% of the total due, or another amount the bank determines; EMI amounts are added in full. If that 5% formula applied to Rohan’s ₹20,000 bill and there were no EMIs, the minimum would be ₹1,000. Paying ₹1,000 would still leave ₹19,000 before interest, taxes or any new spending. PNB’s KFS lists 2.95% monthly finance charges and a 35.89% annual percentage rate for revolving credit on its standard retail cards; some card variants have different terms.

The lesson is bigger than one bank: a minimum payment is a due-date amount, not a debt-clearing plan. Rohan writes down the total balance, APR, due date and the amount he can realistically pay, then stops treating the unused credit limit as spare income.

**Try this:** On your latest statement, find “total amount due,” “minimum amount due,” and the interest rate. If you can only pay the minimum, contact the issuer and review the full repayment cost before adding more card spending.

## 2. Meera puts a rupee value on a mutual-fund cost

Meera sees “direct” and “regular” beside the same mutual-fund scheme. She assumes one must invest in better companies. The label alone does not tell her that. SEBI’s investor education material explains that the plans hold the same underlying portfolio, while the cost structure differs: regular plans include distribution costs, and direct plans generally have lower expense ratios.

She finds that the displayed annual expense ratio difference between two versions of a scheme she is comparing is 0.50 percentage points. To understand the scale, she uses a simple illustration: on a flat ₹10 lakh balance, 0.50% is ₹5,000 over a year. The actual rupee cost will change as the balance and expense ratios change. It is not a forecast of returns.

That calculation gives Meera a better question than “Which one is best?” What service is included in the regular plan? What work would she need to do herself in a direct plan? How is any separate advisory service paid for? A lower cost can matter, but making an investment decision without understanding the plan, risk and service can create a different problem.

**Try this:** Compare the exact scheme, plan name, current expense ratio, services received and the fund’s risks. Do not switch based only on the word “direct.”

## 3. Asha turns her father’s life-certificate reminder into a family checklist

Asha’s father depends on his pension for regular household expenses. A November reminder prompts her to check the life-certificate process with him. Recent coverage says government pensioners below 80 can submit during November, while those 80 and above can begin in October. Because dates and procedures can vary by pension arrangement, Asha checks her father’s own pension-disbursing authority instead of relying on a forwarded message.

They use the official Jeevan Pramaan guidance to understand the digital option. The service describes submitting pension details, completing biometric authentication and making the resulting certificate available to the disbursing agency. Asha helps her father keep the acknowledgement and certificate ID, then confirms the agency can see the submission. That last check matters: completing a form and confirming that the right office received it are two separate steps.

They add three details to a family folder: the pension payment order number, the disbursing bank or authority, and where to check submission status. They do not share passwords or one-time codes with anyone offering to “speed things up.”

**Try this:** If a parent receives a government pension, confirm the deadline and accepted method with their authority, submit through an official channel, and retain the confirmation. For the current November cycle, check promptly.

## 4. Nisha puts all the repayments on one page

Nisha earns ₹90,000 a month after tax. She has a home-loan EMI of ₹28,000, a personal-loan EMI of ₹12,000 and a ₹6,000 card payment. Together, those scheduled debt payments are ₹46,000, or about 51% of her monthly take-home pay. That arithmetic is not a universal “safe” or “unsafe” cutoff. It simply shows why each payment can feel manageable alone while the total leaves less room for food, school costs, repairs and savings.

When an unexpected expense arrives, Nisha considers another short-term loan. The RBI’s National Strategy for Financial Inclusion calls for financial education that promotes sustainable borrowing and highlights the risk of repaying loans with fresh borrowing. Nisha lists each lender, outstanding balance, interest rate, due date, EMI or minimum payment, remaining term and any early-payment charge. Now she can see which obligations overlap and what information to ask each lender for before taking on anything new.

The useful first step is not to guess which loan to close. It is to see the full picture and understand the costs and consequences of each option.

**Try this:** Add all monthly debt payments, then compare the total with take-home income. Mark any payment being covered by another loan. This is a cash-flow check, not a rule that determines what you should do.

## 5. Pallavi turns a college goal into a monthly number

Pallavi and Karan want to set aside ₹6 lakh for a college expense in three years. They already have ₹1.5 lakh earmarked. The remaining gap is ₹4.5 lakh. Dividing that by 36 months gives roughly ₹12,500 a month before investment growth, inflation, tax or fees. It is not a suggested investment amount; it is a starting estimate that shows the goal needs a plan.

They also have a home-repair goal and a much longer-term retirement goal. Putting all three into one vague “savings” bucket made it difficult to tell whether they were on track. Now each goal has four notes: target amount, target date, money already set aside and the monthly gap. They can discuss short-term needs separately from longer-term goals instead of assuming one investment choice fits every timeline.

SEBI’s investor education guidance recommends considering goals, time horizon and risk tolerance. It explains that diversification can reduce the effect of poor performance in any single investment, though it cannot remove investment risk or guarantee returns.

**Try this:** Write down your next three goals, when each is due, and what is already set aside. Revisit the estimates if costs, income or timelines change.
