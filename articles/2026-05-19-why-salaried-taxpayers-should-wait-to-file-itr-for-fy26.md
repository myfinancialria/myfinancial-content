# Why Salaried Taxpayers Should Wait to File ITR for FY26

**TL;DR**
- The Income-Tax Department has released ITR forms for the Financial Year 2025-26 (Assessment Year 2026-27), but salaried taxpayers should exercise caution.
- It is generally advisable for salaried individuals to wait until at least June 15 before filing their income tax returns.
- This waiting period allows employers to issue accurate Form 16s, which summarise salary income and tax deductions.
- Key documents like the Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS) on the income tax portal also need time to update with all financial transactions.
- Waiting helps ensure that all income and tax details are correctly reflected, preventing discrepancies and the need for revised returns later.

## What this means in plain terms

The Income-Tax Department makes the forms for filing your annual income tax returns available quite early. For the current filing cycle, covering income earned between April 1, 2025, and March 31, 2026 (Financial Year 2025-26, or FY26), the forms are already out. While it might seem efficient to file your returns as soon as possible, especially for salaried individuals, there's a practical reason to hold back.

The primary reason to wait is to ensure all your financial information is accurately compiled and reported by various entities like your employer and banks to the Income-Tax Department. Your employer, for instance, has a deadline of June 15 to issue your Form 16, which is a crucial document detailing your salary, allowances, and the tax deducted at source (TDS). Similarly, your bank interest, mutual fund dividends, and other transactions are reported by financial institutions, and these take time to reflect correctly in your Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS) on the income tax portal.

Filing your return before these documents are fully updated and cross-verified can lead to errors. If there's a mismatch between what you report and what the Income-Tax Department has on record, you might receive a notice or have to file a revised return, which can be a tedious process. Waiting a few weeks ensures you have all the correct data at hand, making your filing accurate and hassle-free.

## Understanding the ITR Filing Window

The Income Tax Act mandates that individuals file their income tax returns for a given financial year by July 31 of the subsequent assessment year. For FY26 (April 1, 2025, to March 31, 2026), the deadline for filing is July 31, 2026. While the window opens early, typically in April, the initial availability of forms doesn't necessarily mean it's the optimal time for everyone to file.

For salaried individuals, the income structure is relatively straightforward, primarily revolving around salary, allowances, and deductions. However, the data points required for accurate filing often come from third parties. Employers are responsible for deducting Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) from salaries and depositing it with the government. They then issue Form 16, which is a consolidated statement of your salary income and TDS for the financial year. According to the Income Tax Act, employers must issue Form 16 by June 15 of the assessment year (for FY26, this means June 15, 2026). This statutory deadline is a key reason why waiting is prudent.

### Why the June 15 Deadline Matters

The June 15 deadline for Form 16 is critical. Before this date, your employer might still be finalising their payroll and tax calculations for the financial year. Filing your return without a final Form 16 means you're relying on provisional data, which could change. Any discrepancies between your provisional figures and the final Form 16 could lead to an incorrect return. Furthermore, other financial institutions also report various transactions to the tax department, and these data points are continuously updated on the AIS and TIS on the income tax e-filing portal (https://incometax.gov.in). These statements might not be fully comprehensive until well into June.

## The Crucial Role of AIS, TIS, and Form 16

For a salaried taxpayer, three documents form the bedrock of accurate income tax filing: Form 16, the Annual Information Statement (AIS), and the Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS). Understanding their purpose and timing is key to a smooth filing experience.

### Form 16: Your Salary Income and TDS Certificate

Form 16 is issued by your employer and is essentially a certificate of the income earned from salary and the tax deducted at source (TDS) on that income. It has two parts:
*   **Part A:** Contains details of TDS deducted and deposited by the employer, your PAN, the employer's PAN and TAN, and assessment year.
*   **Part B:** Provides a detailed break-up of your salary, allowances, perquisites, and any deductions claimed under Chapter VI-A (like 80C, 80D, 80G, etc.) that you declared to your employer.
As per the Income Tax Rules, employers must furnish Form 16 to employees by June 15 of the assessment year (Rule 31(1)(a) of the Income Tax Rules, 1962). Waiting for this document ensures that all your salary-related figures, including exemptions and deductions, are accurate and align with what your employer has reported to the tax department.

### Annual Information Statement (AIS) and Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS)

The AIS and TIS are comprehensive statements available on the income tax e-filing portal (https://incometax.gov.in). They provide a consolidated view of all your financial transactions reported by various entities (banks, mutual fund houses, employers, etc.) to the Income-Tax Department.
*   **AIS:** This is a detailed statement covering various categories of information, including salary, interest income, dividend income, mutual fund transactions, securities transactions, foreign remittances, and more. It also includes information related to tax deducted at source (TDS), tax collected at source (TCS), and advance tax payments.
*   **TIS:** This is a simplified summary of the information available in AIS, providing aggregated values for different categories of income. It helps taxpayers get a quick overview.

These statements are dynamic and get updated as and when reporting entities furnish information to the tax department. While some information might be available earlier, it's common for certain data points, especially from banks or other financial institutions, to be updated later in the filing season. Cross-verifying your Form 16 with your AIS and TIS is crucial to ensure all your income sources and TDS details are consistent. Any discrepancy should be flagged and resolved before filing your return to avoid future notices.

## Avoiding Discrepancies and Revisions

Filing an income tax return is a statutory obligation, and accuracy is paramount. An incorrect return can lead to a host of complications, making the initial act of waiting a small price to pay for peace of mind.

### The Hassle of Filing a Revised Return

If you file your ITR based on incomplete or incorrect information and later discover an error (e.g., a mismatch with your final Form 16 or updated AIS), you will need to file a revised return. While the Income Tax Act allows for filing a revised return under Section 139(5), it's an additional step that can be time-consuming and frustrating. You must file the revised return before the end of the assessment year (i.e., by March 31, 2027, for FY26) or before the completion of the assessment, whichever is earlier. Each revision carries the risk of introducing new errors or attracting further scrutiny.

### Potential Penalties and Interest

Filing an incorrect return can have financial consequences.
*   **Interest under Section 234A, 234B, and 234C:** If an incorrect return leads to a higher tax liability that was not paid on time, the taxpayer may be liable to pay interest. For instance, if you under-report income and underpay tax, interest under Section 234B might be levied for default in payment of advance tax.
*   **Penalty for Misreporting Income (Section 270A):** The Income Tax Act provides for a penalty for misreporting of income. If the under-reported income is due to misreporting, a penalty of 50% of the tax payable on such under-reported income may be levied. In cases of "misreporting due to misrepresentation or suppression of facts," the penalty can go up to 200% of the tax payable on such under-reported income. While these are usually applied in more severe cases, minor discrepancies can still trigger scrutiny.
*   **Notices from the Income-Tax Department:** A mismatch between the income declared in your ITR and the information available with the Income-Tax Department (via Form 16, AIS, TIS, Form 26AS) can lead to a notice under Section 143(1)(a) for prima facie adjustment or even a detailed scrutiny notice under Section 143(2). Responding to these notices requires time, effort, and often professional assistance.

Waiting for all official documents to be available and cross-verified significantly reduces the chances of such discrepancies, ensuring your filing is accurate from the outset and you avoid unnecessary complications.

## A real example

Rahul, 38, ₹28L CTC, Bengaluru

Rahul is a software engineer with a Cost-to-Company (CTC) of ₹28 lakhs per annum. He has a home loan, invests in ELSS mutual funds, and has health insurance for his family. He usually files his ITR-1 as soon as the forms are released.

This year, the ITR forms for FY26/AY27 were released in April. Rahul, wanting to get it over with, decided to file his return by the end of April.
He downloaded his provisional payslips and estimated his income and deductions. He had declared his home loan interest (₹2.5 lakhs), ELSS investments (₹1.5 lakhs), and health insurance premium (₹35,000) to his employer. Based on these, his employer had deducted TDS throughout the year.

Rahul filed his return, expecting a small refund.

However, in the first week of June, his employer issued the final Form 16. Upon reviewing it, Rahul noticed a discrepancy:
1.  **HRA Exemption Mismatch:** Due to a change in his rental agreement mid-year, the HRA exemption calculated by his employer in the final Form 16 was ₹15,000 lower than what he had initially estimated and reported in his early filing.
2.  **Bank Interest:** He also realised he had forgotten to include ₹12,000 in interest income from a new fixed deposit opened in January 2026, which was not reflected in his provisional bank statements he used for early filing, nor was it updated in his AIS yet.

Because of these two oversights, Rahul's taxable income was actually higher by ₹27,000 (₹15,000 + ₹12,000) than what he had declared. This meant he had underpaid his tax by approximately ₹8,100 (assuming a 30% tax bracket).

Now, Rahul has to:
1.  **File a Revised Return:** He must re-calculate his taxes accurately and file a revised ITR-1 before the July 31, 2026 deadline or March 31, 2027, whichever is earlier.
2.  **Pay Additional Tax + Interest:** He will have to pay the additional tax of ₹8,100, potentially along with interest under Section 234B and 234C for underpayment of advance tax, if applicable.

If Rahul had simply waited until after June 15, when his final Form 16 was available and his AIS/TIS were more complete, he could have filed an accurate return in one go, avoiding the hassle of revision and potential interest payments.

## What to do this week

While waiting for the final Form 16 and updated AIS/TIS, you can take several proactive steps to prepare for a smooth ITR filing. These actions will help you gather necessary information and cross-check details efficiently once all official documents are available.

1.  **Gather All Investment Proofs and Documents:** Collect all documents related to your investments (e.g., ELSS statements, PPF passbook, life insurance premium receipts, health insurance premium receipts, home loan interest certificates, education loan interest certificates) for FY26. Also, consolidate bank statements for all your accounts to identify interest income.
2.  **Review Your Form 26AS:** Log in to the income tax e-filing portal (https://incometax.gov.in) and check your Form 26AS. This statement provides details of tax deducted at source (TDS) and tax collected at source (TCS) against your PAN. Ensure that all TDS entries made by your employer and banks (e.g., on fixed deposit interest) are reflected correctly.
3.  **Cross-Verify Bank Statements for Interest Income:** Go through all your savings account and fixed deposit statements for FY26. Note down all interest earned. This is crucial because bank interest, even if small, is taxable and must be reported. Compare this with what is reflected in your Form 26AS.
4.  **Prepare a Summary of Other Incomes:** If you have any other sources of income beyond salary (e.g., rental income, capital gains from selling shares/mutual funds, freelance income, dividend income), compile a summary of these. Ensure you have the necessary statements (e.g., capital gains statements from brokers, rent receipts).

## FAQ

### Can I file my ITR before June 15 if my employer provides a provisional Form 16?
It is strongly advised against filing with a provisional Form 16. Employers have until June 15 to issue the final Form 16, and provisional figures might change, leading to discrepancies and the need for a revised return.

### What is the Annual Information Statement (AIS) and why is it important?
The AIS is a comprehensive statement available on the income tax portal that provides a consolidated view of all your financial transactions reported by various entities to the Income-Tax Department. It's important for cross-verifying your income and tax details to ensure accuracy before filing.

### What should I do if my Form 16 is delayed beyond June 15?
If your employer delays issuing Form 16 beyond June 15, you should first follow up with them. If it's still not available, you can file your ITR using your payslips, bank statements, and Form 26AS, but ensure all calculations are accurate and match the TDS reflected in 26AS.

### What happens if there's a mismatch between my Form 16 and AIS/TIS?
If you find a mismatch, you should first approach the reporting entity (e.g., your employer or bank) to get the discrepancy rectified. If it's an error in the AIS/TIS, you can submit feedback on the e-filing portal to correct the information. File your ITR only after resolution or with a clear understanding of the discrepancy.

### Is there a penalty for filing my ITR late?
Yes, if you file your ITR after the due date (July 31 for individuals for FY26), a late filing fee under Section 234F is applicable. For income up to ₹5 lakhs, the fee is ₹1,000. For income above ₹5 lakhs, the fee is ₹5,000.

### Can I file a revised return if I make a mistake?
Yes, you can file a revised return under Section 139(5) of the Income Tax Act if you discover any omission or wrong statement in your original return. The revised return must be filed before the end of the assessment year (March 31, 2027, for FY26) or before the completion of the assessment, whichever is earlier.
