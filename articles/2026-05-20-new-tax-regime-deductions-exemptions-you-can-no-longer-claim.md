# New Tax Regime: Deductions & Exemptions You Can No Longer Claim

**TL;DR**
-   The new income tax regime, now the default, offers lower tax rates but requires taxpayers to forego most common deductions and exemptions.
-   Taxpayers still retain the option to choose between the old regime, which allows numerous benefits, and the new regime annually.
-   Key deductions lost in the new regime include Section 80C investments, House Rent Allowance (HRA), and interest on housing loans.
-   For salaried individuals, the standard deduction of ₹50,000 is one of the few significant benefits retained under the new regime.
-   The optimal choice between regimes depends on an individual's total eligible deductions and specific financial planning.

## What this means in plain terms

India's income tax system now operates with two distinct regimes: the 'old' and the 'new'. The old regime, which many taxpayers are familiar with, offers a higher tax rate structure but allows you to significantly reduce your taxable income by claiming various deductions and exemptions. These often include investments in instruments like PPF or ELSS, paying home loan interest, or claiming House Rent Allowance (HRA).

In contrast, the new tax regime, introduced to simplify the tax structure, comes with lower slab rates. However, this simplicity comes at a cost: it largely removes the ability to claim most of these popular deductions and exemptions. From Financial Year 2023-24 (Assessment Year 2024-25) onwards, the new tax regime has become the default option for all taxpayers, meaning if you don't explicitly choose the old regime, you will automatically be placed under the new one for tax calculation purposes.

This dual system means that taxpayers must carefully evaluate their financial situation each year to determine which regime offers them a lower tax outgo. It's a trade-off between enjoying lower headline tax rates and leveraging various tax-saving investments and expenses.

## Understanding the Two Regimes: Old vs. New

India's income tax framework offers taxpayers a choice between two distinct systems for calculating their tax liability. Understanding the fundamental differences is crucial for making an informed decision.

### The Old Regime's Appeal

The old tax regime, which has been in place for decades, is characterised by its higher tax slab rates compared to the new regime. However, its primary appeal lies in the extensive list of deductions and exemptions it allows. This regime was designed to incentivise certain types of savings, investments, and expenses considered beneficial for individuals and the economy. For instance, it encouraged long-term savings through instruments like Public Provident Fund (PPF) and Equity Linked Savings Schemes (ELSS), promoted homeownership by allowing deductions on home loan principal and interest, and supported health and education expenses. Many taxpayers with significant investments or loan commitments often found the old regime more beneficial despite its higher base rates, as their taxable income could be substantially reduced.

### The New Regime's Simplicity

The new tax regime, introduced in Budget 2020 and made the default from FY 2023-24, aims for simplicity. It offers lower income tax rates across various slabs. The core philosophy behind this regime is to reduce the compliance burden for taxpayers by eliminating most of the deductions and exemptions. The idea is that taxpayers can benefit from lower rates without needing to make specific tax-saving investments or track numerous expenses. This regime is often more attractive to individuals who do not have significant deductions to claim, prefer a straightforward tax calculation, or are just starting their careers with fewer financial commitments.

## Key Deductions and Exemptions Lost

The most significant aspect of opting for the new tax regime is the forfeiture of a wide array of tax benefits that were cornerstones of personal financial planning under the old regime. Understanding these lost benefits is critical for comparison.

### Section 80C Benefits

This is arguably the most impactful change for many Indian taxpayers. Under the old regime, Section 80C of the Income Tax Act allowed a deduction of up to ₹1.5 lakh for various investments and expenses. This included:
-   **Provident Fund (PF) contributions:** Employee's share to EPF or VPF.
-   **Public Provident Fund (PPF):** Contributions to this long-term savings scheme.
-   **Equity Linked Savings Schemes (ELSS):** Mutual funds with a three-year lock-in.
-   **Life Insurance Premiums:** For self, spouse, or children.
-   **Home Loan Principal Repayment:** The principal component of EMI.
-   **Children's Tuition Fees:** For up to two children.
-   **National Savings Certificates (NSC):** Government-backed savings scheme.
-   **Fixed Deposits (Tax-Saving FDs):** With a five-year lock-in.

All these popular avenues for tax saving are no longer eligible for deduction under the new tax regime.

### Housing-Related Benefits

For homeowners, the loss of housing-related deductions can be substantial:
-   **House Rent Allowance (HRA):** Salaried individuals receiving HRA as part of their salary could claim exemption under Section 10(13A) if they lived in rented accommodation. This exemption is not available in the new regime.
-   **Interest on Housing Loan (Section 24):** Under the old regime, interest paid on a home loan for a self-occupied property could be claimed as a deduction up to ₹2 lakh annually. For rented properties, the full interest could be deducted, subject to certain limits. This crucial benefit is entirely removed in the new regime.

### Other Common Deductions and Exemptions

Beyond 80C and housing benefits, numerous other deductions that helped reduce taxable income are also unavailable:
-   **Section 80D (Health Insurance Premiums):** Premiums paid for health insurance for self, family, and parents were deductible. This is disallowed in the new regime.
-   **Section 80TTA/80TTB (Interest on Savings Accounts/Deposits):** Deduction for interest earned on savings bank accounts (up to ₹10,000 for individuals) and interest from deposits for senior citizens (up to ₹50,000) is not available.
-   **Leave Travel Allowance (LTA):** Exemption for travel expenses during leave, usually available twice in a block of four years, is foregone.
-   **Professional Tax:** A small deduction for professional tax paid to the state government is also not allowed.
-   **Standard Deduction on Rent (Section 24b for House Property):** The 30% standard deduction on rental income from house property is also removed.
-   **Deductions under Section 80E (Education Loan Interest), 80G (Donations), 80U (Disability), etc.:** Most of these specific deductions are not permitted under the new regime.

## What Remains in the New Regime?

While the new tax regime eliminates a vast majority of deductions and exemptions, it's important to note that a few key benefits have been retained or introduced. These are crucial for salaried individuals and certain specific categories of taxpayers.

### Standard Deduction for Salaried Individuals

A significant relief for salaried and pensioner taxpayers under the new regime is the reintroduction of the standard deduction. From Financial Year 2023-24 onwards, salaried individuals and pensioners can claim a standard deduction of ₹50,000 from their gross salary, similar to the old regime. This offers a flat, unconditional reduction in taxable income, providing some parity with the old regime's benefits.

### Employer's Contribution to NPS (Section 80CCD(2))

One of the few retained deductions is for the employer's contribution to the National Pension System (NPS) account of an employee. Under Section 80CCD(2), an employee can still claim a deduction for the employer's contribution to their NPS account, up to 10% of their basic salary plus dearness allowance (14% for Central Government employees). This encourages participation in the NPS, a long-term retirement savings scheme regulated by the PFRDA (Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority) (Source: [PFRDA](https://pfrda.org.in)).

### Agniveer Corpus Fund (Section 80CCH(2))

Introduced more recently, contributions made to the Agniveer Corpus Fund by an Agniveer, or by the Central Government to the account of an Agniveer, are eligible for deduction under Section 80CCH(2) in the new tax regime. This specific deduction supports the Agnipath scheme for recruitment into the armed forces.

### Agricultural Income

Income derived from agricultural activities continues to be exempt from income tax under both regimes, as per Section 10(1) of the Income Tax Act. This remains a consistent exemption across the tax frameworks (Source: [Income Tax India](https://incometax.gov.in)).

## Who Benefits from Each Regime? Making the Choice

Deciding between the old and new tax regimes is a personalised financial decision that depends entirely on your income level, investment habits, and eligible expenses. There isn't a one-size-fits-all answer.

### When the Old Regime is Better

The old tax regime typically proves more beneficial for individuals who:
-   **Have significant Section 80C investments:** If you regularly invest in PPF, ELSS, life insurance, or repay home loan principal, these deductions can substantially lower your taxable income.
-   **Pay high home loan interest:** The deduction of up to ₹2 lakh under Section 24 for self-occupied property, or full interest for rented property, can make a considerable difference.
-   **Receive HRA and pay rent:** If you live in rented accommodation and your HRA exemption is substantial, the old regime is likely more advantageous.
-   **Pay health insurance premiums:** Section 80D deduction for health insurance for self, family, and parents adds to the benefits.
-   **Have other specific deductions:** Such as education loan interest (80E), donations (80G), or professional tax.

Generally, if your total eligible deductions and exemptions under the old regime exceed a certain threshold (often around ₹2.5 lakh to ₹3.75 lakh, depending on your income slab), the old regime will likely result in a lower tax liability than the new regime.

### When the New Regime is Better

The new tax regime is usually more suitable for individuals who:
-   **Have minimal tax-saving investments:** If you don't utilise Section 80C or other major tax-saving avenues, the lower tax rates of the new regime might be more appealing.
-   **Do not have a home loan or pay rent:** Without these significant deductions, the old regime loses much of its advantage.
-   **Prefer simplicity:** For those who wish to avoid complex tax planning and simply pay tax on their gross income (minus the standard deduction), the new regime offers a straightforward approach.
-   **Are early in their career:** Young professionals with fewer financial commitments and lower incomes might find the lower slab rates more beneficial.

### Default Status and Annual Choice

It's crucial to remember that from FY 2023-24, the new tax regime is the **default**. This means if you do not explicitly choose the old regime, your employer will deduct TDS (Tax Deducted at Source) based on the new regime's calculations. However, salaried individuals have the flexibility to choose their preferred regime **every financial year** when filing their Income Tax Return (ITR). Business income earners, however, can only switch once in their lifetime from the new to the old regime. This annual choice allows you to adapt your tax planning to your changing financial circumstances (Source: [Income Tax India](https://incometax.gov.in)).

## A real example

Let's consider **Priya, 35, ₹20L CTC, Mumbai**, a salaried professional, to illustrate the tax calculation under both regimes for Financial Year 2023-24 (Assessment Year 2024-25).

**Priya's Income and Deductions:**
*   Gross Salary: ₹20,00,000
*   Basic + DA: ₹10,00,000
*   House Rent Allowance (HRA): ₹4,00,000
*   Leave Travel Allowance (LTA): ₹50,000
*   Professional Tax: ₹2,500
*   Investments under Section 80C (PPF, ELSS): ₹1,50,000
*   Health Insurance Premium (Section 80D): ₹25,000
*   Home Loan Interest (Section 24 for self-occupied property): ₹2,00,000
*   Employee's contribution to NPS (Section 80CCD(1B)): ₹50,000

**1. Tax Calculation under the Old Tax Regime:**

*   **Gross Salary:** ₹20,00,000
*   **Less Exemptions:**
    *   Standard Deduction: ₹50,000
    *   HRA Exemption: (Assuming actual HRA received or 50% of Basic, or Rent Paid - 10% Basic, whichever is least. Let's assume ₹2,00,000 is exempt based on her rent and location).
    *   LTA Exemption: ₹50,000
    *   Professional Tax: ₹2,500
*   **Less Deductions:**
    *   Section 24 (Home Loan Interest): ₹2,00,000
    *   Section 80C: ₹1,50,000
    *   Section 80D: ₹25,000
    *   Section 80CCD(1B) (NPS): ₹50,000

*   **Total Deductions & Exemptions:** ₹50,000 (SD) + ₹2,00,000 (HRA) + ₹50,000 (LTA) + ₹2,500 (PT) + ₹2,00,000 (S24) + ₹1,50,000 (S80C) + ₹25,000 (S80D) + ₹50,000 (S80CCD) = ₹7,27,500
*   **Taxable Income:** ₹20,00,000 - ₹7,27,500 = **₹12,72,500**

*   **Tax on ₹12,72,500 (Old Regime Rates):**
    *   Up to ₹2,50,000: Nil
    *   ₹2,50,001 - ₹5,00,000 (₹2,50,000 @ 5%): ₹12,500
    *   ₹5,00,001 - ₹10,00,000 (₹5,00,000 @ 20%): ₹1,00,000
    *   ₹10,00,001 - ₹12,72,500 (₹2,72,500 @ 30%): ₹81,750
    *   **Total Tax:** ₹12,500 + ₹1,00,000 + ₹81,750 = ₹1,94,250
    *   Add 4% Health & Education Cess: ₹7,770
    *   **Total Tax Payable (Old Regime): ₹2,02,020**

**2. Tax Calculation under the New Tax Regime:**

*   **Gross Salary:** ₹20,00,000
*   **Less Deductions:**
    *   Standard Deduction: ₹50,000 (This is allowed in the new regime from FY 2023-24)
    *   Employer's NPS Contribution (Section 80CCD(2)): This example does not include employer's NPS contribution. If it did, it would be deductible.
*   **Total Deductions:** ₹50,000
*   **Taxable Income:** ₹20,00,000 - ₹50,000 = **₹19,50,000**

*   **Tax on ₹19,50,000 (New Regime Rates):**
    *   Up to ₹3,00,000: Nil
    *   ₹3,00,001 - ₹6,00,000 (₹3,00,000 @ 5%): ₹15,000
    *   ₹6,00,001 - ₹9,00,000 (₹3,00,000 @ 10%): ₹30,000
    *   ₹9,00,001 - ₹12,00,000 (₹3,00,000 @ 15%): ₹45,000
    *   ₹12,00,001 - ₹15,00,000 (₹3,00,000 @ 20%): ₹60,000
    *   ₹15,00,001 - ₹19,50,000 (₹4,50,000 @ 30%): ₹1,35,000
    *   **Total Tax:** ₹15,000 + ₹30,000 + ₹45,000 + ₹60,000 + ₹1,35,000 = ₹2,85,000
    *   Add 4% Health & Education Cess: ₹11,400
    *   **Total Tax Payable (New Regime): ₹2,96,400**

**Conclusion for Priya:**
In Priya's case, with her significant home loan interest, HRA exemption, and 80C investments, the **Old Tax Regime (₹2,02,020)** results in a substantially lower tax liability compared to the **New Tax Regime (₹2,96,400)**. This example clearly demonstrates how valuable deductions can be.

## FAQ

### What is the main difference between the old and new tax regimes?
The old tax regime has higher tax rates but allows numerous deductions and exemptions (like 80C, HRA, home loan interest). The new tax regime offers lower tax rates but eliminates most of these deductions, making tax calculation simpler.

### Is the new tax regime mandatory?
No, the new tax regime is the default from FY 2023-24, meaning it applies automatically if you don't choose. However, salaried individuals and pensioners can still opt for the old regime annually when filing their Income Tax Return.

### Which deductions are still available under the new tax regime?
Under the new tax regime, you can still claim the standard deduction of ₹50,000 for salaried individuals and pensioners, and the employer's contribution to NPS under Section 80CCD(2). Agricultural income also remains exempt.

### Can I switch between regimes every year?
Yes, salaried individuals and pensioners have the flexibility to choose between the old and new tax regimes every financial year when filing their ITR. Individuals with business income, however, can only switch once in their lifetime.

### How do I inform my employer about my chosen tax regime?
Your employer will typically ask you to declare your preferred tax regime at the beginning of the financial year or during specific periods. This declaration impacts your monthly TDS. If you don't declare, the new regime will be applied by default.

### Does the new tax regime affect capital gains tax?
No, the new tax regime primarily impacts income tax on salary, business income, and other income sources. Capital gains tax provisions and rates remain the same regardless of which income tax regime you choose.

### Is the standard deduction available in the new tax regime?
Yes, from Financial Year 2023-24 (Assessment Year 2024-25) onwards, a standard deduction of ₹50,000 is available for salaried individuals and pensioners under the new tax regime.
