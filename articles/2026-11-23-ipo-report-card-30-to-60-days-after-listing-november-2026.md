## Why look 30 to 60 days after listing?

When a company first offers its shares to the public, it's called an Initial Public Offering (IPO). The day these shares start trading on the stock exchange is known as the **listing day**. Often, there's a lot of excitement on this day, and the share price might jump significantly, leading to a **listing gain** (the difference between the IPO price and the closing price on listing day).

However, this initial "pop" can sometimes be misleading. The first day's price often reflects high demand from new investors and short-term traders. After a month or two, this initial frenzy usually settles down. Also, for some big investors called **anchor investors**, there's a lock-in period (a time when they cannot sell their shares) which typically ends around 30 days after listing. Once this lock-in ends, if these investors decide to sell, it can affect the share price.

Looking at the share price 30 to 60 days after listing gives us a more stable picture. It shows how the market truly values the company once the initial excitement has faded and more regular buying and selling takes place. It helps us understand if the initial listing gain (if any) has been maintained, grown, or reversed.

## Why do IPOs pop or drop on listing?

When a company lists on the stock exchange, its share price on the first day can either "pop" (increase significantly) or "drop" (decrease). This largely depends on **demand vs. supply**.

If many investors want to buy the shares (high **demand**) and there aren't enough shares available (low **supply**), the price tends to go up. This is often seen when an IPO is heavily **subscribed** (meaning applications for shares far exceed the number of shares offered). Factors like the company's business prospects, the overall **market mood** (whether investors are feeling positive or negative about the stock market), and the **pricing** of the IPO (if the company offered shares at a relatively low price) can all drive demand.

Conversely, if there's low demand or too many shares available, the price might drop. Sometimes, even if an IPO lists with a gain, the price might fall later if the initial demand was purely speculative, or if large investors like **anchor investors** (who get shares before the public IPO) sell their shares after their **lock-in period** ends.

## Why the first-day price is not the whole story

The listing day price is just a snapshot. The stock market is dynamic, and prices are constantly changing due to many factors. This constant change is called **volatility**. A stock might list flat (meaning its price doesn't change much from the IPO price) and then move significantly in the weeks or months that follow, either up or down. Similarly, a stock might have a huge listing gain but then see its price fall later.

The **broader market** conditions also play a role. If the overall stock market is doing well, it might lift many stocks, including newly listed ones. If the market is falling, even fundamentally strong companies might see their share prices decline. That's why observing the price 30 to 60 days later gives a more realistic view, as the market has had time to absorb the new stock and react to actual company performance or broader economic news, rather than just IPO hype.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine a new shop opening in your neighbourhood. On the first day, there's a big crowd, special offers, and a lot of buzz. Everyone rushes in to buy. The prices might seem great, and things fly off the shelves. But after a month or two, the initial excitement fades. The crowd thins, and people start buying based on the actual quality of products, the regular prices, and how useful the shop truly is to them. The prices then settle at a level that reflects the shop's true value to the community, not just the opening day fanfare. An IPO is similar – the 30-60 day mark is like seeing the shop's regular business, not just its grand opening.

## Things people get confused about

### Myth → Fact: A big listing gain means it's a great company.
**Fact:** A large listing gain indicates strong initial demand, but it doesn't guarantee the company's long-term success or that its stock will continue to rise. The company's fundamentals (like its profits, management, and business model) are more important for long-term performance.

### Myth → Fact: Low subscription means it's a bad IPO.
**Fact:** While high subscription often signals strong interest, some good companies might have lower subscription levels due to market conditions or less marketing. A low subscription doesn't automatically mean the company is bad; it just means there was less initial public demand.

### Myth → Fact: IPO investing is a quick way to get rich.
**Fact:** IPOs are part of stock market investing, which carries risks. While some IPOs offer quick gains, many can also lead to losses, and prices can fluctuate. It's important to approach IPOs with a long-term perspective and understand the risks involved.

## FAQ

### What is an IPO?
An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is when a private company first offers its shares to the public to raise money, allowing ordinary people to buy a part of the company.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies that list on the main exchanges (NSE or BSE). SME IPOs are for Small and Medium Enterprises, which list on separate platforms (NSE Emerge or BSE SME) with different rules, often involving smaller issue sizes and different trading dynamics.

### How do I calculate my return?
To calculate your return, you subtract the IPO price from the current price, divide by the IPO price, and then multiply by 100 to get a percentage. For example, if you bought at ₹100 and it's now ₹120, your return is ((120-100)/100) * 100 = 20%.

### What is an "issue price"?
The "issue price" is the fixed price per share at which a company offers its shares to the public during an IPO.

### What does "current price" mean?
The "current price" is the latest trading price of a share on the stock exchange at the time of reporting. It reflects what buyers and sellers are agreeing upon right now.
