## First, what is an IPO — in one minute?

Imagine a successful family business that has grown quite big. Now, they want to expand even more – maybe build a new factory, develop new products, or repay some loans. For this, they need a lot of money. One way to get this money is to sell a small part of their company to the general public.

This act of a private company selling its shares to the public for the very first time is called an **Initial Public Offering (IPO)**. When a company does an IPO, it moves from being privately owned to publicly owned.

The shares are first sold in what's called the **primary market** (think of it as the 'first-hand' market). After the IPO, these shares get **listed** on a stock exchange like the NSE or BSE. This means they can then be bought and sold by anyone in the regular stock market (the 'secondary market').

The **price band** is the price range within which investors can bid for shares. For example, if the price band is ₹161 to ₹170, you can bid for shares at any price within this range. The company will then decide the final **issue price** (also known as the cut-off price), which is usually the highest price in the band, if the IPO is fully subscribed.

The **lot size** is the minimum number of shares you must apply for in an IPO. You cannot buy just one share; you have to bid for shares in multiples of this lot size. For example, if the lot size is 88 shares, you must apply for 88 shares, or 176 shares, and so on.

The **minimum investment** is simply the lowest amount of money you need to bid for one lot of shares. You calculate it by multiplying the highest price in the price band by the lot size. For instance, if the lot size is 88 shares and the highest price is ₹170, your minimum investment would be 88 shares * ₹170 = ₹14,960.

The **issue size** refers to the total number of shares a company is offering to the public. Sometimes, it's also referred to as the total value of the shares being offered (e.g., an IPO of ₹500 crores). In our examples, the issue size for Knack Packaging is given as the number of shares.

An IPO can consist of two parts:
1.  **Fresh Issue**: This is when the company issues brand new shares. The money raised from these shares goes directly to the company. Companies use this money for expansion, debt repayment, or other business needs.
2.  **Offer for Sale (OFS)**: This is when existing shareholders (like promoters or early investors) sell some of their shares to the public. The money raised from an OFS goes to these selling shareholders, not to the company itself.

The **Red Herring Prospectus (RHP)** or **Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP)** is the official document filed by the company with SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India). Think of it as the company's detailed report card and business plan for potential investors. It contains everything you need to know about the company, its financials, risks, management, and how it plans to use the money from the IPO. It's a long document, but it holds all the crucial information.

## The IPO process, step by step

Applying for an IPO might seem complicated, but it follows a clear path:

1.  **DRHP Filing**: First, the company prepares its detailed DRHP and files it with SEBI for approval. This document outlines all the company's details, financials, and the purpose of the IPO.
2.  **SEBI Approval & RHP**: Once SEBI reviews and approves the DRHP, the company releases the final RHP. This document includes the **price band** (the fixed range within which you can bid) and the **issue dates** (when the IPO opens and closes).
3.  **Bidding Period**: This is when you, as an investor, can place your bids. You decide how many lots you want to apply for and at what price within the price band.
    *   **Retail Individual Investors (RIIs)**: This category is for individual investors who apply for shares worth up to ₹2 lakhs. This is where most everyday investors fall.
    *   **High Net-worth Individuals (HNIs)**: These are investors who apply for shares worth more than ₹2 lakhs. They are often called Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs).
    *   **Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs)**: These are large institutional investors like mutual funds, insurance companies, and foreign institutional investors. They typically bid for very large quantities of shares.
4.  **Allotment**: After the bidding closes, the company and its bankers decide how to distribute the shares. If the IPO is oversubscribed (meaning more people applied than there are shares available), shares are allotted through a lottery system for RIIs. You might get all the shares you applied for, some, or none at all.
5.  **Refund/Debit**: If you get an allotment, the money for the shares is debited from your bank account. If you don't get any shares, your money is unblocked or refunded.
6.  **Listing**: Finally, the shares are **listed** on the stock exchange (like NSE or BSE). This is when they start trading in the secondary market, and you can buy or sell them like any other stock.

## A simple way to think about it

Imagine your favourite local shop, 'Sharma Sweets', has become very popular. They want to open new branches across the city but need a lot of money. Instead of taking a big loan, Mr. Sharma decides to sell a small 'share' of his business to his loyal customers and the public.

He sets a price for each 'share' (the **price band**), and you can buy them in bundles (the **lot size**). The money you pay for these shares goes to Mr. Sharma to build his new shops (a **fresh issue**). Once you own a share, you become a part-owner of Sharma Sweets, and you can later sell your share to someone else if you wish, once it's listed on the 'Sharma Sweets Share Exchange'. That's essentially what an IPO is – a company inviting you to become a part-owner for the first time.

## Things people get confused about

It's easy to get caught up in the excitement around IPOs, but let's clear up some common misunderstandings:

*   **Myth**: Listing gain is guaranteed in every IPO.
    *   **Fact**: While some IPOs do list at a higher price than their issue price (giving a 'listing gain'), this is not guaranteed. The market can be unpredictable, and shares can also list at a lower price, leading to a 'listing loss'. All investments carry market risk.
*   **Myth**: You must apply for every IPO that comes out.
    *   **Fact**: There's no compulsion to apply for every IPO. Each IPO is for a different company, with different business prospects and risks. It's important to understand the company and its sector before considering an application.
*   **Myth**: IPOs are only for rich people.
    *   **Fact**: IPOs have a dedicated portion for Retail Individual Investors (RIIs) where the minimum investment amount is usually affordable for many. The maximum application for RIIs is ₹2 lakhs, making it accessible to a wide range of investors.

## FAQ

### How do I even apply for an IPO?
You can apply for an IPO through your bank's ASBA (Applications Supported by Blocked Amount) facility via net banking, or through your stockbroker's platform. Your money remains blocked in your account until allotment, and is only debited if you receive shares.

### What is a lot size?
The lot size is the minimum number of shares you must bid for in an IPO. You cannot apply for fewer shares than the lot size, and you must apply in multiples of it.

### What is the difference between Mainboard and SME IPOs?
Mainboard IPOs are for larger, more established companies and are listed on the main exchanges (NSE, BSE). SME IPOs are for smaller companies and are listed on separate platforms like NSE Emerge or BSE SME, often with different listing and trading rules.

### Can I sell my shares on the listing day?
Yes, once your allotted shares are credited to your demat account and the company lists on the stock exchange, you can sell them on the listing day itself, just like any other stock.

### What happens if I don't get an allotment?
If you don't receive any shares in the allotment process, the money that was blocked in your bank account for the IPO application will be unblocked, usually within a few days after the allotment date.
